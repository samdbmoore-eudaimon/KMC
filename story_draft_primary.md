# Ninefold Orchard and the Scarecrow Who Forgot

## Chapter One: The Wrong Whistle

There are things everybody knows, the way everybody knows that cats are not to be trusted near cream and that a red sky at night means fine weather is on its way, and one of the things everybody in Ninefold Orchard knew, without ever once having to be told, was that there were nine rows, and nine rows only.

Row One grew apples. Row Two grew pears. Row Three grew plums, Row Four grew quinces, and so on down to Row Nine, which grew a small, stubborn, purplish-black fruit that nobody outside the orchard had ever bothered to name and everybody inside it simply called a nubbin, on the grounds that it looked like one and nobody had come up with anything better in a hundred years of trying. At the head of each row stood a scarecrow, and each scarecrow had exactly one job in all the world, which was to whistle the row's count once every hour, so that anybody wandering past could tell at a glance whether everything that ought to be there still was.

This is a more sensible arrangement than it sounds. A whole town fed itself from nine rows of trees, which only works if nobody helps themselves early and nobody takes more than their fair share, and the only way to be sure of that, in an orchard too big for any one pair of eyes to watch, is to have something count for you, out loud, so everybody can hear whether the sums still add up. It is a rule that has kept a great many villages fed, in a great many worlds, and Ninefold Orchard was no exception, except insofar as its counting was done by scarecrows, which is not something you see everywhere.

Pip loved the whistles the way some children love a clock striking the hour, which is to say properly, with his whole small self. He was small even by the standards of a pip-sprite, about the size of a chestnut, pale gold-green all over like the flesh just under an apple's skin, with a little brown cap on his head shaped like the stalk he had grown from. He carried a hemp-string satchel over one shoulder with a tally-stick poking out of the top of it, and he counted things. Nobody had ever asked him to. It was simply, to Pip's mind, the single most satisfying thing a person could do with an afternoon, to know for an absolute, unarguable fact exactly how many of something there were.

"Forty-one," he said, mostly to himself, crouched at the foot of Row One with one finger tracing along a low branch. "Forty-one apples. Same as this morning."

"You already counted this morning," said Nine, dropping out of the branches above him in a scatter of black-blue feathers. He was a young crow, not yet properly grown into his own wings, with one stark white feather sitting amongst all the dark ones on his left side, which he called his tenth feather and could never quite explain why that made it special, only that it plainly did. "Why count again?"

"Because things change," said Pip. "That's rather the whole point of counting. If you only ever check the once, you don't know whether it's still true."

Nine considered this with the great seriousness of somebody who has just been handed a very large thought in a very small container and isn't sure yet whether it will fit. Before he could say anything else, a third voice cut in, somewhere down around their ankles.

"Forty-one is wrong," said Bramble.

Bramble was a round hedgehog with spines banded dark brown and cream, and two rings of darker fur round his eyes that gave him the permanent look of somebody faintly and pleasantly surprised by his own cleverness, which, in fairness, he generally had good reason to be. He held up a small flat slate with that morning's count scratched in the corner of it.

"It says forty-two here," he said. "From this morning. You wrote it yourself."

Pip counted the branch again. Forty-one.

He counted it a third time, more slowly, touching each apple as he went, the particular careful way you count a thing you would very much like to have got wrong the first time round. Forty-one.

"One's missing," he said, and something in his stomach went quite still, the way it does the first time a number refuses to come out the way it is meant to.

It did not take them long to find where it had gone. There was a core, neatly stripped, tucked under a fern at the foot of the tree, and a scatter of black feathers nearby that were not Nine's.

"That's not so terrible," said Nine, a shade too quickly, in the voice of somebody who badly wants a thing not to be terrible. "One apple. Crows eat fruit. It's practically the job description."

"It's not your turn, though," said Pip. Because that was the other rule of Ninefold Orchard, tucked in underneath the counting: everybody got their fair share, and fair shares only worked if nobody took more than theirs and nobody took it early. The orchard fed the whole town on that promise, and had done for a hundred years, and the scarecrows' whistles were not there for decoration. They were how the whole orchard remembered to be fair to itself, hour by hour, whether it felt like it or not.

The culprit was not hard to find, once they went looking. He was sitting in the crook of Row One's own scarecrow, of all the places to hide, a scruffy young crow with a guilty, hopeful sort of look about him and three more apple cores already lined up beside him, like a confession he had not quite worked up the nerve to say out loud.

"I was going to put them back," he said, which is not, strictly speaking, a thing that can be done with an apple once you've had it.

"Why didn't you wait for the count?" Pip asked. Not crossly. He had never been much good at cross. Just properly baffled, the way you're baffled watching somebody get their sums wrong when the right answer is sitting there in plain sight.

"Because I don't trust the whistle any more," the crow said, and looked, for the first time, properly miserable rather than merely caught out. "It's been going strange. Row One's scarecrow, Scratch, we call him, he's been whistling the wrong number for a week now. Sometimes he says forty when there's thirty-eight. Sometimes he just stops, halfway through, as though he's forgotten what comes after six. If the whistle can't be trusted, what's the good of waiting on it?"

Pip looked up at Scratch properly for the first time in his life. Scarecrows were just part of the orchard, the way fences were, the way the weather was, and you didn't generally look at them any more than you looked at the weather. But he looked now, and he could see it: one button eye sat very slightly lower than the other, and the painted mouth, meant for whistling, hung a little open and a little crooked, like a held breath that had forgotten what it was holding for.

"That's not a reason to steal," said Bramble. "That's a reason to tell somebody the whistle's broken."

"Tell who? A scarecrow that can't count right, telling everybody else the counting can't be trusted, is exactly the sort of thing nobody believes."

Pip crouched down until he was eye to eye with the young crow, which is a much easier thing to manage when you are the size of a chestnut than it sounds. "Here is what we are going to do," he said. "We are going to go back and count Row One properly. All of it, together, out loud, so there's no arguing over the number after. And then you're going to tell Scratch exactly what you told us. Because I don't think you're the trouble here. I think you're the first one brave enough to notice it."

It took the better part of an hour, and it was, by Pip's own private reckoning, one of the most satisfying hours he had ever spent. Thirty-eight apples still on the tree. Four cores under the fern, matching four apples that should have been there and were not, and thirty-eight and four made forty-two, exactly what Bramble's slate had said that morning, exactly what should have been true all along. The number had not lied. It had simply been waiting for somebody patient enough to add it up properly.

When they told Scratch, gently, the three of them together with the guilty crow among them, the old scarecrow's crooked painted mouth trembled, and for a moment Pip would have sworn that straw was not meant to be able to look quite that sad.

"I know," said Scratch, in a voice like wind going through dry leaves. "I've known for days. I just can't remember how it started going wrong. And if I can't remember that, how am I meant to put it right?"

Nine looked at Pip. Bramble looked at Pip. It appeared, without anybody actually saying so, that it was going to be Pip's job to have an idea, which he privately thought was fair enough, since ideas were rather like apples: it hardly mattered whose tree they grew on, so long as everybody got their share of what came off it.

"Then we don't put Row One right," said Pip slowly. "We find out why it went wrong. And I don't think Row One is where it starts."

Above them, quite unnoticed until that moment, an older crow dropped onto a branch and looked down at all four of them with an expression that was not quite surprise and not quite relief, streaked with grey through his black feathers, one leg banded with a carved ring of tally-marks.

"You're the first ones to ask why in a long time," said Cornix. "Row Two's scarecrow has been worse than Scratch for twice as long, and nobody's been out to look. I think you had better come with me."

Behind them, the young crow who had started the whole business, not looking nearly so young now that the worst of the secret was out, hopped down from the branch and fell into step beside Nine.

"Does this mean I'm not in trouble?" he asked.

"You're in some trouble," said Bramble. "Just rather less than everybody else is about to be."

And so they set off down the row together, Pip, Nine, Bramble and the crow who would, before the day was out, earn back his place in the queue and a proper name instead of "that thief from Row One", towards whatever was waiting at the second scarecrow, and the number that would not add up.

## Chapter Two: The Whistle That Meant "About"

There are two kinds of nearly, as anybody who has ever been promised nearly a whole slice of cake will tell you, and the difference between them is roughly the difference between weather and a wet sock, which is to say that both of them are damp but only one of them is any use to you.

Pip had been thinking about nearly all the way from Row One to Row Two, because Cornix set a brisk pace for an elder crow with a banded leg, and because the closer they got to the pear trees the louder the arguing became. It was not the good sort of arguing, the sort where two hedgehogs disagree cheerfully about whether a slug is best eaten head first or not at all. It was the tired, hot, going-nowhere sort, and it was coming from a knot of townsfolk gathered under the second scarecrow with baskets on their hips and thunder on their faces.

"He's an old fraud," said Sorrel the wood-mouse, to nobody and everybody, in the way of wood-mice everywhere. "Whistled 'about forty' this morning, he did, same as yesterday, same as the day before that, and there's never been an about in a pear tree in the whole history of the world."

The scarecrow at the head of Row Two was a great slouching thing of damp sacking and mildewed straw, with one button eye missing and the other hanging by a thread so that it seemed to be perpetually squinting at some distant, disappointing horizon. When the hour turned, he drew a long wheezing breath, and instead of a clean bright count he produced a whistle that sagged in the middle, a sound like a kettle giving up, and it said, as near as anybody could tell, roughly forty. Give or take.

"Old Roughweather," said Cornix, quietly, hopping onto a fence post. "That's what they call him now. He used to be Warp, on account of his weaving, but nobody remembers that any more, least of all him."

Bramble had already got his slate out. Bramble was the sort of hedgehog who, presented with a burning barn, would first want to know exactly how many buckets there were, and this was not a fault, whatever his aunts said, because more problems in this world are put right by counting the buckets than by running about shouting.

"Roughly forty," Bramble said, tapping his chalk. "Roughly is not a number. Roughly is a shrug wearing a number's coat."

They watched what happened next, and it was this. The pears were shared out by the whistle, because that was the whole point of the whistle, and Old Roughweather had whistled about forty, so Barrow the badger, who kept the ledger and had no more imagination than a boot, wrote forty in his book and divided forty pears amongst the eight families who worked Row Two, which came to five apiece with none left over, and everyone went home. Except there were not forty pears. There were forty-three. Pip counted them twice, out loud, the way they had counted the apples, and got forty-three both times, which is the good sort of nearly, the sort you can stand on.

"Three pears," said Nine, the young crow, ruffling his one white feather. "That's not so much."

"It's not so much today," said Pip. "But he rounds down when he's tired and up when he's cross, and he's tired or cross most days, and three pears a day is a basketful a fortnight, and a basketful a fortnight is a family going short by Michaelmas, and nobody ever notices, because everyone's only ever three short, which feels like nothing, which is exactly how a thing that is nothing at all adds up to a great deal of something."

This is worth understanding, because it is true of pears and also of a good many larger matters. A single rounding is a kindness you do to a difficult number to make it easier to carry. A thousand roundings, all leaning the same convenient way, is a hole in the bottom of the basket.

They went to the scarecrow. Up close he smelled of wet dog and old rain, and the hanging button eye turned slowly towards them.

"Count's about forty," he said, before they could speak, in a voice like a door that needed oiling. "Near enough. Always been near enough. Fussing over the odd pear, that's for young folk with nothing better to do."

"Why about?" asked Bramble, and it was a good question, delivered flat, the way you'd ask a sheep why it was standing in the pond.

Old Roughweather was quiet for a long moment. "Because the exact numbers stopped coming," he admitted, and there was something under the wheeze that was almost frightened. "One day I could see every pear on every branch, clear as counting your own toes. Then it went soft at the edges. Forty-one, forty-two, they slid about, wouldn't hold still. So I rounded. Rounding felt kind. Rounding felt like getting it near enough right, which is better than getting it exactly wrong, and I told myself near enough was good enough, and I have been telling myself that for a very long time."

And here Pip did the clever thing, which was not clever at all really, which is often the way of clever things. He did not tell him he was wrong. He got up on the fence beside his sagging shoulder, opened his satchel, took out his tally-stick, and said, "Then let's not carry the whole number in your head. Let's put it on the stick. You whistle what you can see, and when it goes soft, we count the soft bit together, one pear at a time, and I'll cut the tally, and the stick can remember for you."

So they did. Old Roughweather whistled the branches he could still count sharp, which was most of them, thirty-eight, and where his sight went soft, at the topmost fork where five pears hid amongst the leaves, Nine flew up and called them down one by one, and Bramble kept the running total on his slate, and Pip cut a clean notch for each, and it came to forty-three, and the stick did not shrug, because a stick cannot, which is the whole virtue of a stick.

When the hour turned again Old Roughweather drew his breath and whistled, and the whistle did not sag in the middle. It came out bright and exact and entirely without the word about in it, and Barrow the badger looked up from his ledger in surprise, crossed out forty, wrote forty-three, and the last three pears went to the family that had been quietly going shortest, who had not liked to complain, because who complains about nearly.

The townsfolk drifted off, mollified, and Sorrel found she had nothing to be cross about and looked mildly disappointed, which is the natural state of a wood-mouse.

"You'll want the stick back," said Old Roughweather, but Pip shook his head and left it hanging on his fence, because a scarecrow who is learning to count sharp again should not have to do it from memory, any more than you'd ask someone with a sore leg to run before they could walk.

It was Cornix, of course, who broke the good mood, the way elder crows are contractually obliged to. He was looking north, up towards Row Three, and his feathers had gone still in a way that had nothing to do with the wind, mostly because there was no wind, and that was precisely the trouble.

"That's wrong," he said. "Look at Barrow's ledger."

They looked. The page lay open on the fence, forty-three written fair and clear in badger-ink, and as they watched, with no breath of air to stir it and no hand anywhere near it, the ink shivered, and the three at the end of forty-three came slowly, deliberately, mischievously unwound itself off the paper like wool off a spool, and drifted away over the pear trees, leaving the tally reading forty, and a small cold spiralling gust going widdershins up the row towards the plums, laughing to itself in a language made entirely of tallies coming undone.

## Chapter Three: The Wind That Went the Wrong Way Round

Now, everyone in Ninefold Orchard knew that winds came in roughly two sorts, the warm ones that came up from the south smelling of hay and the cross ones that came down from the north smelling of nothing much and meaning business, and both of them, whatever their mood, went the way winds were supposed to go, which is to say onward, forward, off about their business like a dog that has just remembered where it buried something. This wind did not. This wind went backwards. It went widdershins, which is the old orchard word for anticlockwise, the wrong way round the sundial, the way you stir a pot if you want your granny to look at you sharply, and it spiralled up Row Three amongst the plum trees unpicking things as it went.

By the time Pip, Nine and Bramble had climbed up to the plums, the whole row was in a muddle of the quiet, expensive sort. Bramble set his flat slate down on a mossy root, licked his chalk, counted the plums on the nearest bough, wrote fourteen, and then all three of them watched the four peel itself off the slate letter by letter and go whistling off into the branches, leaving the slate saying ten and Bramble saying a word that hedgehogs are not, strictly, supposed to know.

"It did it again," said Nine, who was flustered, which for Nine was much the same as breathing. "It keeps doing the doing backwards. I counted these plums three times and every time I get to the end something reaches in and un-gets me."

"It's not un-getting the plums," said Pip carefully, because Pip was the sort who checked a thing twice before he was even properly worried about it. He looked at the bough. The plums were all there, fat and dusty-blue and perfectly countable, fourteen of them, no argument. "The plums are fine. It's the counting it's undoing. It's letting us do the sum and then taking the sum back off us the moment we finish, like a cat that lets you stroke it exactly once."

This turned out to be precisely and annoyingly true. Sorrel the wood-mouse, who had come up the row to be helpful in the way that gossip is helpful, reported that Barrow's ledger was now so unwound that it claimed the entire orchard contained minus six plums, which even Barrow, a badger not given to flights of fancy, agreed was unlikely, since he had eaten two of them himself that very morning and you cannot eat a fruit that isn't there, not even a badger can manage that.

They tracked it, in the end, the way you track anything that giggles. Cornix stood very still on his banded leg and pointed with his beak at the places where the leaves were shivering the wrong way, against the lie of the branch, and they followed the shivers up and up until they came to the topmost plum tree, and there, curled in the fork of the highest bough like a draught that had found somewhere cosy to sit, was Widdershins.

He was not much to look at, which is often the way with the ones that cause the most bother. He was a knot of moving air about the size of a startled kitten, visible only by the dust and plum-blossom he kept whirling round himself, and he was, at that moment, happily unspooling the number seven off a fencepost and eating it, or doing whatever it is winds do that looks like eating.

"OH," he said, delighted, spinning right way up and then wrong way up. "VISITORS. Nobody visits. Watch, watch, I can do a trick, you did a nine plus five and got fourteen, look, look, I take the five back OFF and now it's a nine again and it's like the sum NEVER HAPPENED, isn't that the best, isn't that the funniest thing you ever saw, I can un-happen ANYTHING."

"We noticed," said Bramble, drily, retrieving his four from where it had lodged in a spider's web belonging to Cobweb, who was not pleased.

"It is a bit funny," Pip admitted, because Pip was honest even when it was inconvenient, and it was, when you thought about it, a genuinely startling trick. "But you're doing it to sums that were finished. That's like undoing somebody's shoelaces after they've walked all the way to the mill. They still had to walk to the mill. You've just made them fall over."

Widdershins went still, which for him meant only slightly less spinny. "Nobody," he said, in a smaller breeze, "ever told me undoing was for anything. Everybody's always doing, doing, doing, adding and adding, and I thought, well, somebody ought to go the other way, keep it tidy, keep it even, and then everybody got cross, and I didn't know why, so I did it MORE, because at least when I did it people looked at me." Which is, if you have ever met a lonely creature or indeed a small child at half past bedtime, a speech you will recognise at once.

Now Pip could have said a great many stern things, but Pip had helped Old Roughweather only the day before by giving him a job rather than a telling-off, and Pip had noticed that this worked far better on the sad and the muddled than shouting ever did. So instead he sat down on the bough, took out his tally-stick, and said, "Do you want a real job? A proper one, with permission, that actually needs the thing you're good at?"

The wind edged closer. "A job," he said, as though tasting it.

"Watch," said Pip. He notched seven tallies, then five more, and counted the lot. "Twelve. Now, is that right? I think it's right, but thinking isn't knowing. So we go your way. We take the five back off." He rubbed out five notches. "And if we land exactly back on the seven we started with, then the sum was true all along, and if we don't, we've caught a mistake before it ever reached Barrow's ledger. Undoing isn't the opposite of counting. It's how counting checks itself. It's the mirror that tells you your hat's on straight."

There was a long, thoughtful gust. "So I'd be," said Widdershins slowly, "allowed to un-happen sums, but only the ones that ASK to be checked, and if it comes out wrong I get to make a fuss?"

"An enormous fuss," Pip agreed. "That's the whole point of you."

You have never seen a wind so pleased. He tore round the whole of Row Three in about four seconds flat, and where before he had left chaos he now left every tally checked twice, forwards by the counters and backwards by himself, and by teatime Barrow's ledger not only said fourteen plums again but said it with a small proud second fourteen written underneath in a hand made entirely of settling dust, which is the closest a wind can come to a signature.

"Verified," said Barrow, who did not hand out that word lightly. Widdershins glowed, insofar as moving air can glow, and settled at last into a gentle contented breeze that went round the plums the right way, mostly, except when he was working.

It was Cornix, naturally, who spoiled the tea. He was looking south this time, all the way back down the nine rows to the very first one, to Row One and the apples, and his feathers had gone still again.

"He's checking every sum in the orchard," Cornix said quietly, watching the little wind dart happily from row to row. "Forwards and backwards, all the way down the rows. And he says they all balance, every one, except the very first count of all. The oldest one. The one right at the beginning, in Row One." He turned his banded leg towards the apples, towards the crooked scarecrow they had left behind on that very first morning. "Whatever's forgetting itself, it doesn't start out here at the far end. It starts back at Scratch."

## Chapter Four: The Scarecrow Who Forgot

They came back down the rows the way you come back to a bed you have got out of too early, knowing the trouble was never really behind them but ahead, waiting patiently with its hands folded. Widdershins had gone whistling off to check the sums a fourth time, on the grounds that a job worth doing was worth doing until somebody stopped you, and so it was Pip, Nine and Bramble who walked the long track back to Row One, with Cornix hopping a few paces ahead on his banded leg and casting the occasional grey look over his shoulder to make sure none of them had wandered into a hedge.

Scratch stood where he had always stood, at the head of the apples, with his crooked painted mouth and his two mismatched button eyes, one bone and one horn, staring out over the orchard he could no longer count. He did not look like a monster. He looked, Pip thought, like a coat that somebody had loved very much and then left on a fence for a hundred winters, and the thought was so sad that Pip had to sit down on the cool grass and take out his tally-stick and hold it, the way some people hold a warm mug when there is nothing to be done but hold something.

"We've come to sit with you properly," said Pip. "Not to fix you in passing. To sit."

"That's kind," said Scratch, in a voice like wind getting into a barn. "Though I warn you, I'm poor company. I keep losing the thread of things. I had it a moment ago. I had a great many things a moment ago, and now I've a moment ago and not much else."

Nine, who three days past had been stealing this very scarecrow's fallen apples and calling it foraging, shuffled his glossy blue-black feathers and looked at his own feet, and the one white feather amongst all the dark ones caught the light like a mistake nobody had corrected. "We'll help," he said. "We're good at counting now. All of us. Even me."

Bramble had already laid his flat slate on the grass and licked the tip of his chalk, which was the hedgehog equivalent of rolling up one's sleeves. "The trouble with a memory," he said, in his methodical way, "is that it lives in your head, and heads are like bathwater, they go cold and they go out the plughole and you never see them again. So we don't keep it in your head. We put it out here, on the slate, where it can't leak."

"That's the same trick we did for Old Roughweather with his numbers," said Pip. "He kept trying to hold the count in his head and rounding it soft to make it fit. So we put the count on something that couldn't round. We'll do the same with your remembering. You tell me anything true, however small, and I write it down, and once it's written it's true forever whether you can hold it or not. You don't have to carry it any more. The slate carries it."

Scratch was quiet for a while, and the apples creaked on their branches like an old house settling.

"I know something's wrong," he said at last, which was exactly what he had said a week ago, and Pip nearly wrote that down and then didn't, because it was a true thing that led nowhere, and a scout learns to tell the difference. "Ask me small things. Big things frighten off, like sheep. Small things you can walk right up to."

So Pip asked small things. What did the frost taste of. Which button had come first, the bone or the horn. Whether he preferred the pears in Row Two, which was a trap, and Scratch fell into it laughing a dry laugh and said pears were show-offs and everyone knew it, and Bramble wrote *prefers apples, dislikes the airs of pears* on the slate, and something loosened.

"Who taught you to count?" asked Pip, very gently, the way you put your hand out flat to a nervous pony.

And there it was. The button eyes, which could not move, seemed somehow to be looking a very long way off, further than Row Nine, further than the nubbins, right into the green middle of everything.

"Not the same one who taught the others," said Scratch slowly, and Bramble's chalk went still, listening. "That's the thing I've had at the back of my mouth for a hundred years and couldn't spit out. The others, all eight of them, they were taught by the scarecrow in the next row along. Row Two taught Row Three. Row Three taught Row Four. Passed down the line, hand to hand to hand, like the counting was a bucket at a fire. But not me. There was no row before mine." He made a sound that was almost a whistle and not quite. "I was taught at the heart. By the first one. The old founding one, planted the very same morning as the great Ninefold Tree, when the orchard was nine saplings and a promise. I stood at the centre and it taught me my numbers straight from its own mouth, and then I came out here to the front, and I taught no one, because I was the beginning of the line and not the middle."

Pip looked at Cornix. Cornix looked at Pip. Neither of them said the thing they were both thinking, because it did not need saying, but Bramble said it anyway, because Bramble said true things whether they needed saying or not.

"Which is why you went first," said the hedgehog. "And worst. The forgetting doesn't start at the far end and creep in. It starts in the middle and creeps out. And you're the one standing closest to the middle by blood, so to speak. You caught it first because you were nearest the source."

"Like standing nearest the fire," said Nine faintly, "you feel the cold first when it goes out."

Scratch's crooked mouth did something that on a face made of sacking should not have been possible, and Pip realised the scarecrow was trying not to weep, and being very English about it, which is to say he had gone quiet and dignified and had a lump in his throat the size of a quince and was pretending very hard that a leaf had got in his eye. When he spoke again his barn-wind voice had gone thin.

"Then it's no good," he said. "It's no good you patching me up, kind as it is, and no good stitching the others one by one down the rows, because we're all just leaves off the one tree, and if the root of the thing that taught us all to count is forgetting itself, then we'll go on forgetting no matter how many times you sit with us. You have to go to the centre." He said it plainly, without drama, which made it land all the harder. "You have to find out what's become of the one that taught us. Go to the heart of the orchard, to the old Ninefold Tree, and see. Please. I'd go myself, but I've stood in this one spot since before your grandmothers, and I find I've quite forgotten how to walk."

Pip stood up and put the tally-stick back in his satchel, and then, because it seemed right, he took Bramble's slate and read the whole of Scratch back to him, all of it, the frost and the buttons and the show-off pears and the great true thing at the end, so that the scarecrow could hear his own self spoken aloud and know it was safe now, written down, kept.

"We'll go," said Pip. "And we'll come back and read you to yourself again, as often as you like, so you never have to hold it alone."

"That's the kindest arithmetic I ever heard," said Scratch.

They left him at the head of the apples with the slate propped against his post where he could see it, and they turned inward, away from the neat outer rows with their fixable little troubles, and towards the dark green tangle at the middle where the paths stopped being straight. Cornix came, on his banded leg, because a scout who leaves off scouting halfway is no scout at all. Nine came too, hopping close, one white feather bright against the deepening shade, because the crow who had once stolen from Scratch had decided he would rather spend the rest of his life giving things back.

Behind them the apples whispered. Ahead the orchard grew stranger, and older, and quieter, and none of them said so, but each of them felt the ground begin, very slightly, to lean towards the centre, the way a whole field of grass leans one way before the weather turns.

## Chapter Five: The Flock That Was Loads

The paths in the middle of Ninefold Orchard did not so much wind as dither, the way a puppy dithers when it has been called from two directions at once, and Pip noticed that the trees here had stopped standing in tidy rows and started standing about in loose knots, like folk at a market who have forgotten what they came to buy. Cornix hopped ahead along a fence-rail that had given up going straight some while ago, his banded leg clicking with each landing, and Nine kept close to Bramble, who kept close to his slate, because a hedgehog with a slate is a hedgehog who always knows at least one true thing, which is more than most manage on a bad day.

They heard the boss long before they saw him, and the boss was a great many.

It came as a sort of rushing hush, the noise a hundred small wings make when they cannot decide, and then over the dithery trees poured a river of finches, brown and gold and greenish, folding and unfolding against the sky the way water folds over a weir, and never once settling, and never once still.

"How many is that?" asked Nine, who found large numbers frightening in the way some grown-ups find spiders frightening, which is to say more than is strictly sensible.

"LOADS," sang the finches, all together and delighted. "We're LOADS. There's HEAPS of us. More than yesterday, probably."

"Probably," Bramble repeated, in the flat voice he kept for things he did not approve of, and he made a small mark on his slate that meant nothing yet, on principle.

The trouble with being loads, as the group discovered over the next dithery half-hour, was that loads is not a number you can share anything out amongst. The finches had crammed themselves forty-deep into one poor damson tree until it bowed like a washing-line on a Monday, while three fat plum trees beside it stood entirely bare and rather hurt about it. When a small finch called Siskin piped up that she thought her brother might be lost, the whole flock rose in a panic and swirled and counted the only way they knew, which was to shout "LOADS" at each other until they felt better, and since loads minus one brother is still loads, nobody could tell, and Siskin cried, and the flock felt worse, and settled nowhere.

"They cannot take turns at the roost," murmured Cornix, watching two hundred finches try to occupy a hawthorn built for thirty. "You cannot take turns if you don't know how many turns there are to take."

Pip took off his brown pip-shaped cap, which was what he did when he was about to think properly, and he looked at the swirling, folding, hushing loads of them, and he did not feel angry, because he was not very good at that, but he did feel the particular itch he got when something true was being left uncounted.

"You weren't always loads," he said, not unkindly. "Somebody taught you that."

The flock came down a little at that, the way a kettle comes off the boil, and an older finch with a bent tail feather landed on the fence by Cornix. "It got hard," she admitted. "Counting a moving thing is hard. Every time we started, we'd shuffle, and lose our place, and have to begin again, and the vagueness was coming through the trees from the middle anyway, all soft and easy, and one day it just seemed kinder to say loads and have done. Loads never loses its place."

"No," agreed Pip, "because it was never in one." He turned his cap round in his hands. "But here is the thing about loads. Loads cannot miss anybody. And you," he said gently to Siskin, "want to be the sort of flock that can miss a brother, and then find him."

Siskin sniffled and nodded, and that, Pip decided, was the whole of it settled, even if the flock did not know it yet.

He did not ask them to hold still, because you cannot ask a river to hold still and expect to be friends afterwards. Instead he crouched by the base of the bowed damson tree and drew, with the point of his tally-stick, one single ring in the dust, about a stride across.

"Nobody count everybody," he said. "Everybody is too much and it wriggles. We are only going to count the finches who settle inside this ring. Bramble?"

"Ready," said Bramble, chalk poised, because being ready was his favourite condition.

They coaxed the flock down to feed, and finches being finches they landed all over, and into the little dust-ring landed a jostling, ordinary, countable number of them, and Bramble counted that number the slow honest way, beak to beak, no shouting, and it was eight.

"Eight in one ring," said Pip. He paced out the bare ground and the bowed tree and the three sulking plums, and with Nine hopping the corners and Cornix calling the lines straight, they marked how many such rings the whole feeding-ground would make, which was nine, because it is that sort of orchard.

"Eight in a ring, and nine rings," said Pip, "is a guess of seventy-two. Not loads. Seventy-two. That is an estimate, which is a guess that has done its homework." He tapped the slate. "But an estimate is only a promise. Now we check it, or it doesn't count."

And here was the clever part, and it was Nine's, which surprised nobody more than Nine. "Make them go through a gap," he blurted. "When I used to, er, when I used to take fruit out of turn, I'd slip through the hole in the hedge one at a time because two won't fit. Count the hole, not the sky."

So they did. They shooed the whole dithery loads of them, gently, towards the one thin gap in the hawthorn, where finches must go through singly or not at all, and Bramble stood at the gap with his slate, and Cornix called a mark for every ten so the tens would not get lost, and the finches flew through one at a proper time, and it was the first orderly thing they had done in a year, and some of them wept to feel it.

The true number was seventy-four.

"Seventy-four," breathed the bent-tailed finch, as though it were a name, which in a way it was. "We're seventy-four."

"Your estimate said seventy-two," said Bramble, almost warmly. "Two out of seventy-four. That is a very good guess that did its homework."

And then Siskin, counting along the fence with her wing, went very still, and said in a tiny voice, "That's only seventy-three of us settled. One short," and for a heartbeat the old panic gathered, until a small draggled finch came puffing over the plums, cross and late and entirely findable, and the flock did not shout loads, the flock counted him back in, seventy-four, and that, everyone agreed afterwards, was the moment they stopped being a crowd and started being a number that could love its members one at a time.

They left the finches sorting themselves into the plum trees in tidy, boastful groups of ten, calling their total to each other like a song, and the group turned once more towards the soft grey vagueness at the orchard's heart. But Pip's careful eye caught something at the very edge of the dithery trees, snagged in the long grass where the paths gave out altogether: a scarecrow's straw, come loose. Not a wisp or two, but bundles, whole counted bundles, unravelled and scattered, their bindings slack, their shapes forgotten, as though somebody who had once known exactly how much a bundle was had woken up and could no longer remember what held it together at all.

## Chapter Six: The Scarecrow Who Came Undone

You could tell a great deal about a scarecrow, Cornix had once said, by the way he held his straw, and the scarecrow slumped at the edge of the dithery middle trees was holding his straw the way a sieve holds soup, which is to say not at all, and with a certain air of surprise about the whole business.

Pip stood very still and looked at the mess of him. It was not a small mess. Straw lay everywhere in loose drifts, some of it still faintly bundle-shaped, most of it not, so that the poor fellow had gone from being a scarecrow to being a rumour of one, a sort of straw-coloured sneeze frozen in the act of happening. His crossbar sagged. One sleeve had emptied itself entirely and hung like a windsock on a windless day.

"Oh," said the scarecrow, when he noticed them. "Have you come about the straw? Everyone comes about the straw. I keep meaning to sort it out, only I can't for the life of me remember how much of me there's supposed to be."

Beside him crouched a smaller scarecrow, an apprentice by the look of his half-stitched patchwork waistcoat and his button eyes that did not quite match, holding a great armful of loose straw and turning slowly on the spot with the expression of someone who has been sent to fetch water in a net.

"I'm Thistlewick," the little one said miserably. "I've been trying to put him back. I've been trying since the mid-morning whistle, only every time I stuff a handful in, another handful falls out the bottom, and I can't tell whether I'm making him more or less, and neither can he."

"That's Strawless, that is," offered Nine, who had landed on the crossbar and was peering down with the frank interest crows reserve for things that are not their fault for once. "Leastways that's what the wood-mice call him now. Sorrel's lot. On account of."

"On account of I keep losing the shape of myself," said Strawless, with the patience of a man who has explained this many times to many people. "It's not that the straw's gone. It's all here. It's that I've forgotten how it went together. I used to know exactly. Now it's just a heap, and you can't be a proper scarecrow made of a heap. A heap doesn't scare so much as one crow, and no offence to present company."

"None took," said Nine, who had once been the sort of crow a heap absolutely should have worried about, and knew it.

Bramble had already unrolled himself, set down his slate, and was regarding the straw the way he regarded most problems, which was as though it were being deliberately difficult and would come round eventually if stared at firmly enough. "How much straw does a scarecrow need?" he asked.

"Ah," said Strawless. "Now. That I do remember, because it's the only bit I've hung onto, the way you'll remember the tune of a song long after the words have upped and left. Ten bundles. A proper scarecrow is ten good bundles, no more, no less. That's the whole of me. That's what makes me stand up and whistle my count. But I've forgotten what a bundle is, you see, so knowing I want ten of them is no more use to me than knowing I want ten of your grandmother's smiles."

Pip took off his cap.

He did it quietly, the way he always did when there was thinking to be got through properly, and he turned the loose straw over in his mind. The trouble was plain once you said it out loud, and Pip rather liked troubles you could say out loud, because those were the ones that could be counted at. Strawless knew he needed ten bundles. He simply had a great flat heap of single straws and no notion of where one bundle ended and the next began, and you cannot make ten of a thing if you cannot make one of it.

"How big is a bundle?" Pip asked. "When you could still do it. Was it a great fat armful, or a little pinch, or somewhere in the middle?"

Strawless thought, and his button-and-stitch face creased. "A handful," he said at last, doubtfully. "A good handful. That feels right. About what young Thistlewick's carrying."

"Then we don't need you to remember," said Pip, and he felt the tidy click of it settle into place, the way a tally-stick notch settles under the thumb. "We only need to agree on one bundle, once, and then make all the others match it. If every bundle is the same size, you don't have to count straws at all. You count bundles. And ten bundles is you."

He knelt in the straw. "Thistlewick, your armful. That's our one true bundle. We'll tie it, and it's the size all the rest have to be, and from now on nobody has to guess ever again."

Bramble tied it off with a twist of hemp, neat and firm, and set it on the grass, and it sat there being unarguably one bundle, the founding member of a family that did not yet exist. "One," said Bramble, and chalked a stroke on his slate.

After that it was, as folk-wisdom has it, merely a matter of doing the thing you have decided to do, which is the hardest sort of matter there is and the most satisfying. Word went round the dithery trees the way word does, on the feet of wood-mice and the wings of finches, and before the next whistle a good part of the town had wandered over to help, because a scarecrow coming undone is everybody's business when the whole orchard eats by his count. They gathered the drifts. They matched each new bundle against the true one, laying them side by side, fatter ones losing a wisp, thinner ones gaining a pinch, until each was a twin of the first, and Bramble chalked a stroke for every one that passed.

"Two," went the slate. "Three. Four." And the marvellous thing, the thing that made Thistlewick's mismatched eyes go round, was how much faster it was to count ten fat bundles than a whole haystack of single straws, and how you could not lose your place, because a bundle was a bundle and stayed put and did not blow away between your fingers the way a straw would.

"Five. Six. Seven." They packed them into Strawless as they went, and with each one he stood a little straighter, his empty sleeve filling, his sag un-sagging, his shape coming back to him not all at once but in tens' worth of steady instalments.

"Eight. Nine."

There was a pause, and a certain holding of breath, and a wood-mouse who had appointed herself supervisor stood up on her back legs.

"Ten," said Bramble, and tied the last one in, and stepped back.

Strawless stood up.

He stood up properly, the way a scarecrow is meant to, tall and square and faintly ridiculous and entirely himself, and he looked down at his own two stuffed arms as though they were long-lost cousins, and then he did something none of them expected. He whistled. It was a rough, glad, wobbling note, the count of his own row rung out clear across the middle trees for the first time since the forgetting had reached him, and every soul in the orchard who heard it knew exactly, to the apple, how the sharing would go that evening.

"Ten bundles," Strawless said, wonderingly. "And I shall never need to count the straws again, only keep them equal, and match the one true bundle, and I'll always know it's me. Why, a body could rebuild anything that way. A body could rebuild the world in bundles of ten, if he had the patience and the string."

Thistlewick was practising already, gathering a fresh armful and holding it against the true bundle, his tin whistle forgotten in his waistcoat, learning at last the difference between a heap and a shape.

Pip put his cap back on. He was tired in the good way, the way you are after a thing has come right, and he might have stayed the evening in the glow of it, only that Nine, up on the crossbar, had gone very quiet, and a quiet crow is a thing worth noticing.

"Pip," said Nine, in a small voice that had lost all its earlier swagger. He was looking not at the straw, nor the town, nor his own clever work, but away towards the orchard's heart, where the trees thickened and the light went thin.

Pip followed his gaze, and Bramble's chalk stopped on the slate.

Along the whole dark rim of the sky above the central rows, where the Ninefold Tree stood a century deep in its forgetting, something was moving, and rising, and spreading, a great restless smoke of wings without any edge that Pip could find, no matter how he tried, and for the very first time since he had set out with his tally-stick and his careful small numbers, Pip looked at a thing in the orchard and understood, with a cold prickle down his gold-green spine, that he had absolutely no idea how to begin to count it.

## Chapter Seven: The Murder Who Could Not Count Themselves

Now, everyone in Ninefold Orchard knew that crows came in threes and fours, the way rain came in showers and grumbles came in Bramble, and if you had ever in your whole life seen as many as a dozen crows in one field at once you would have gone home and told your gran about it, and she would have said you had probably miscounted, because that was the sort of thing grans were for.

So the thing spreading across the sky above the central rows was not, strictly speaking, a thing anybody had a proper word for. It was crows, that much was certain, but it was crows the way the sea is water, which is to say technically true and entirely useless as a description. It poured and folded and turned itself inside out, a great restless smoke of wings that had no edge Pip could find no matter how he squinted, and Pip was a sprite who could find the edge of almost anything.

He took off his brown pip-shaped cap, which was what he did when a problem needed thinking about properly rather than merely worrying about, and he held it against his chest, and he stared, and for the first time in the whole long walk towards the orchard's heart he felt genuinely and completely small.

"Right," said Bramble, who had got his slate out from long habit, the way some people reach for a biscuit in a crisis. He looked at the sky. He looked at the slate. He put the chalk down again very quietly, the way you put something down when it has plainly become an insult to bring it out at all. "Well."

"We could count a ring," offered Nine, though even as he said it his one white feather drooped, because he had been there when they counted the finches, ringed off a little patch and multiplied it round, and this was not that, this was not that at all. You could count a ring of this and walk round it for a week and the ring would have moved, and doubled, and moved again, and eaten your slate.

"There's no round to it," Bramble agreed. "It hasn't got a shape. It's got a mood."

Cornix, the elder crow, landed on the tally-stick fence beside them, his banded leg clicking, and he did not look at the flock the way the others did, with the dizzy swimming feeling of a person leaning over a very deep well. He looked at it the way you look at family who have made a mess of things.

"They came for the forgetting," he said. "The heart-scarecrow is going out like a lamp, and every crow for a hundred miles felt the counting-song wobble, and a crow that can't trust the count gets frightened, and a frightened crow flies to where the others are, and this," he tipped his grey-streaked head at the sky, "is what you get when every last one of them has the same frightened idea on the same afternoon. They don't know why they're here. Worse than that, and this is the part that's turned them wild, they don't know how many they are. Ask a crow how many, and if he can't answer, he starts to think the number might be all of them, might be endless, might be a dark with no far side. And there is nothing, nothing in this world, that a living creature fears like a number with no bottom to it."

Nine had gone very still. Somewhere in that boiling dark were crows he had thieved beside, back when he was a thief and not yet a crow who was good at practical tricks, and he could hear it in the sound they made, not anger, not menace, just a vast frightened not-knowing, thousands of voices all asking the same question and none of them able to answer it.

"So we tell them," said Pip.

"You can't count that," said Bramble, gently, the way you correct a child who has offered to drink the pond.

"No," Pip agreed, and he turned his cap round in his hands, and something in his careful precise little soul was waking up and rolling its sleeves back. "No, you can't count that. Not one at a time. If you counted one crow every heartbeat, all day and all night and never stopped to sneeze, you'd still be counting come apple-blossom, and they'd all have flown off and had chicks and those chicks would have grown up while you were still on the low hundreds. Some numbers are too big to count. They're just too big, and that's not us being stupid, that's the number being honest about its size."

"Then how," said Nine, wretchedly.

"The same way you eat a whole orchard," said Pip, "which is one apple at a time, except cleverer, because you never actually eat the whole orchard, you just work out how much an orchard is. Watch." And he pointed at a single low branch of the nearest hawthorn, where the crows sat shoulder to shoulder like a row of grumpy black buttons. "Nine. Count me that one branch. Just the one. You can manage one branch."

Nine could manage one branch. Nine, who a fortnight ago could barely be trusted to count his own toes without one going missing, screwed up his eyes and counted, and said, "Nine. Ha. Nine crows," and even in the middle of everything a small unforgivable pride crept into his voice.

"Good," said Pip. "Now, Bramble, that tree. Not the crows. The branches. How many branches on that tree big enough to sit a row of crows on?"

Bramble was methodical, and methodical was exactly what the afternoon wanted. He walked round the hawthorn with his rings-round-the-eyes and his careful chalk, and he counted the fat crow-holding branches and ignored the twiggy useless ones, and he said, "Ten. Call it ten good branches."

"Ten branches," said Pip, "and near enough nine on each. So one tree is ten nines, which is ninety, and I never touched a single crow to know it." He put his cap back on, because now the thinking was turning into knowing, and knowing wanted a tidy head. "And Cornix. How many trees has the gathering settled on? Not the crows. The trees. You can see the whole heart from up high, you've flown it, how many trees are black with them?"

Cornix rose, and circled, and came back, and his old voice was steadier than it had been all day, because a scout loves a thing he can actually scout. "A hundred trees," he said. "Give or take a handful. A hundred trees wearing crows like a bad coat."

And Pip stood in the long shadow of the Ninefold Tree and did the sum out loud so that the crows could hear it, because that was the whole point, the sum was not for the sprites, it was for the flock.

"Nine on a branch," he said. "Ten branches to a tree, so ninety to a tree. A hundred trees, so ninety, a hundred times." He closed his eyes. "Ninety hundreds. Nine thousand. There are about nine thousand of you." He opened his eyes. "Nine thousand crows. That's a lot. That is an enormous, staggering, gran-won't-believe-you lot of crows. But it is nine thousand, and nine thousand has a far side, and a beginning, and an end, same as nine does, only bigger. You are not endless. You were never endless. You just hadn't been counted properly."

And a strange thing happened, the way it always did when a truth got said plainly in that orchard. The boiling stopped. Not all at once, but branch by branch, tree by tree, the great restless smoke of wings settled and grew quiet, because it turned out Cornix had been right, that the crows had not been afraid of being many, they had been afraid of being uncountable, and there is all the difference in the world between a number too big to reach one at a time and a number that has no size at all. Nine thousand was a weight you could set down. Endlessness was not. They had been carrying the wrong one.

Nine flew a little way out over them, his tenth feather bright against all that black, and he said something to them that Pip didn't catch and didn't need to, and the flock made a low sound back that was almost, if you were feeling generous, like relief.

They walked on after that, quieter themselves, into the very middle rows where the light went thin, and it was Bramble who stopped first, and pointed with his chalk at the great old bough of the Ninefold Tree itself, where by rights the richest harvest in the whole orchard should have hung heavy enough to bend the wood.

There was nothing there.

Not a wrong count. Not a lie. Not too few and not too many. Just a smooth grey bough with the shape of fruit still pressed into the air where fruit was meant to be, and nothing hanging in it at all, a hollow where a whole harvest should have been, and Pip felt the little hairs stand up under his cap, because he had spent the entire journey learning to count things that were there, and here, at last, at the orchard's forgetting heart, was the one number he had never once had to write on his tally-stick.

## Chapter Eight: The Bough That Was Meant To Be Empty

The heart of the orchard was the sort of quiet that has weight to it, the way a held breath has weight, and the four of them stood in it and said nothing for rather longer than any of them would later admit.

It was Bramble who had stopped them, his chalk pointing like a small accusing finger at the great grey bough of the Ninefold Tree, and now that they were all looking, none of them could stop. A bough that size, this near the centre, ought to have hung so heavy with fruit that it groaned. Pip had counted enough boughs by now to know what a good one looked like, and to know, in the particular ache of his counting-bones, what this one wasn't.

"Nothing," said Nine, in the small voice of a young crow who very much wanted an older crow to disagree with him.

"Nothing," agreed Cornix, who never softened a number, and whose banded leg shifted once against the moss.

Pip took off his cap.

He always took off his cap to think properly, on the sensible principle that a thing shaped like a pip did its best work in the open air, and he turned it round and round in his hands and looked at the bough the way you look at a sum that has come out wrong when you were absolutely certain you'd done it right.

The trouble was, it hadn't come out wrong. That was exactly the trouble.

"All the others," Pip said slowly, "everything we've met. Thistlewick counting too few. The Maybe-Flock that wouldn't settle to a number at all. The Murder, nine thousand of them, too many to bear until we bore it in layers. Every single one was a wrong count. Something that ought to have been one number, gone and been another." He put his cap back on, then took it off again. "This isn't a wrong count."

"It's empty," said Nine.

"It's exactly empty," said Pip, and something in the way he said exactly made Bramble lift his chalk. "That's different. That's the whole difference. Empty by mistake looks precisely, to the very last speck, like empty on purpose. And I don't know which one this is, and I don't know how to know, and that," he admitted, "is frightening me more than nine thousand crows did."

Bramble, who was methodical the way water is wet, right down through the middle of him, had already got down on his round front and was brushing moss from the foot of the bough with the flat of his slate. The dark rings round his eyes made him look permanently unsurprised, which was useful in a hedgehog, and he did not look up when he said, "There's a mark down here."

They crowded round. Under a century of green there was a plaque, though plaque was a grand word for what was really a slice of the tree's own bark gone hard as horn, and on it, in a whistle-script so old the curves had nearly closed up into circles, someone had carved a line of words. Bramble read them off with his chalk, transcribing each shape onto his slate before he trusted himself to say it aloud, because a hedgehog who has been fooled by a smudge once is never fooled twice.

"Here hung the first," he read. "Counted once. Given away on purpose. Kept empty in its honour."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Amberly, a single stray firefly who had drifted in from the outer rows on the strength of the quiet, settled on the bark and lit the last word up, honour, gold on grey, and then thought better of it and went dark again out of respect.

"The first fruit," Pip breathed. "The very first one the founding scarecrow ever counted. The seed of the whole orchard." He looked up at the nine rows falling away in every direction, every tree of them descended from one apple, one count, one careful whistle a hundred years gone. "It didn't rot. It didn't fall. It wasn't stolen and it wasn't forgotten." He turned the words over the way he turned his cap. "It was given away. Planted somewhere else, or handed to someone hungry, and this bough was kept bare ever since, not because the counting failed here but because the counting started here."

"So the number is nought," said Nine, trying it out, and flinching a little, because a young crow is taught early that nought is the number you get when you've done something wrong.

"The number is nought," said Pip firmly, "and it is the correct number, and it has been the correct number for a hundred years, and every autumn some tidy-minded soul must have looked at this bough and felt their stomach drop the way ours did, and been wrong to." He found he was smiling, which surprised him. "That's the thing about nought, Nine. It isn't the gap where an answer should go. It is an answer. It's the answer that says: I looked, and I counted honestly, and what is truly here is none. An empty basket you forgot to fill is a problem. An empty basket you kept empty on purpose, and know why, and would keep empty again, that's not a problem. That's a promise."

Bramble wrote it on his slate. Nought that means none went wrong, he wrote in one column, and Nought that means we meant it, in the other, and drew a firm line between them, because the whole art of the thing was telling the two apart, and you could not tell them apart by looking. You could only tell them apart by asking why.

Cornix, who had been very still, made a low sound in his throat that in an elder crow passes for being moved.

"There is a reason I brought you the long way to the middle," he said, "and it was not only the crows." He looked at the bough, and then, deliberately, past it, inward, to where the light went thinnest of all. "You will need this. What sits at the very heart, the one who taught all nine to count, the founding scarecrow itself. The forgetting started there and spread outward, and it has been spreading a long, long time." He let that settle. "When you find it, you may find there is very little left. You may find there is nearly nought. And you will have to decide, all over again and for something that matters far more than a bough, which kind of nought you are looking at."

Pip nodded slowly. A tragedy to be mended, or an honour to be kept. He was not sure, yet, that he would know. But he knew now that there was a question to ask, which was more than he'd had an hour ago, and in his experience the whole of counting was really just knowing which question to ask and then being brave enough to ask it out loud.

They gathered themselves to press on. And as they turned, Nine, whose eyes were sharp and whose white tenth feather always seemed to notice things a beat before the rest of him did, paused by a run of harvest baskets stacked ready at the inner path, nine to a row, all counted, all sealed, all correct.

All correct but one.

He couldn't have said how he knew. The stack looked perfect. The whistle-tallies matched. And yet one basket, the third from the end, sat with the faintest wrongness about it, the way a picture hung a hair off straight will nag at you across a whole room, and when Nine cocked his head and did the sum again in the careful layered way Pip had taught him, he came up, every time, with exactly one more apple than the tally swore was there.

"Pip," he said. "Pip, this one. It's off. Only by one, but it's off, and I can't make it not be."

Pip looked at the basket, and looked at the honest empty bough behind them, and felt the orchard tighten its held breath by one apple's worth, and said, very quietly, "Then that's where we go next. Bring your slate, Bramble. One is a small number to be wrong by. It is also, if you count it honestly, absolutely enormous."

## Chapter Nine: The Sum of Small Mistakes

Nine would not let it go, and this, if you knew anything at all about crows, was entirely the point of him.

"It's this one," he said, hopping along the top of the stack of nine harvest baskets, all of them counted, all of them sealed with the little twist of plaited grass that meant a scarecrow had whistled its number and been believed. "Third from the end. It says forty on its tally and it feels like thirty-nine, and don't ask me how a basket feels like anything, because I don't know, I only know it does."

Bramble, who had been trundling along at the back with his slate under one arm the way a churchwarden carries a hymn-book, stopped and put his nose in the air. "Then we count it," he said, as though this settled everything, which, in the world according to Bramble, it very nearly did.

Pip took off his cap. He always took off his cap to think properly, on the sensible grounds that a pip-shaped hat is a lovely thing but no help whatsoever to arithmetic, and he looked at the stack, and at the sealed lids, and at the little woven twists that meant a promise, and he felt the first cold prickle of something being wrong in a way that was much too quiet to be honest about it.

They broke the seal on the third basket, which felt rather like being rude at a funeral, and Bramble counted the apples out onto his slate one at a time, chalking a tidy tally-mark for each so that nobody, not even a crow, could argue. He counted thirty-nine.

"One out," said Nine, with the terrible satisfaction of a creature who has been proved right and rather wishes he hadn't.

"One out," agreed Bramble, and here is the thing about Bramble, he did not say it the way most people would, as though one out were practically nothing, a rounding, a shrug. He said it the way a doctor says a word he doesn't like the look of.

Cornix landed on the topmost basket, his banded leg clicking, and turned his grey-streaked head towards the heart of the orchard, where the rows drew close and the light went the colour of weak tea. "Break another," he said. "The next one in. Nearer the middle."

They broke the next, and it too was one out. And the one past that, one out. Not wildly wrong, not spilled or robbed or rotted, nothing you could point at and gasp over, only every single basket the nearer they got to the centre quietly, apologetically, maddeningly short by exactly one, the way a step you didn't know was there is only ever one step, and always enough to send you flat on your face.

"It's not a thief," said Pip slowly, turning his cap round and round in his hands. "A thief takes a lot from a few. This takes one from everything. It takes the smallest amount that anybody could possibly fail to notice." He looked up. "And near the heart, where the counting is oldest and the forgetting is deepest, nobody has noticed. For years."

That was when they saw it, or rather when they nearly saw it, because seeing it fully turned out to be beyond anybody.

It was sitting on the rim of the eighth basket, and it was very hard to look at, not because it was frightful but because it was so nearly not there, a shy shimmer in the shape of something small, apologising just by existing. When it spoke its voice was the softest thing in the orchard, softer than Amberly's wings had been the night before, if indeed there had been a night before, which near the heart you could never quite be sure of.

"I do help," it said, wretchedly. "I count them. I count them ever so carefully. I only ever miss one. One is hardly anything. One is so close to none that I can't see why it matters, I truly can't, and everyone is always so cross, and I don't mean it, I only ever miss one."

And you could tell it believed this with its whole almost-heart, that one out was the same as right, the way a tired child believes that bedtime and being carried up to bed are the same word.

Bramble put down his slate. This was Bramble's moment, and he knew it the way a careful creature always knows the one job in the whole wide world he was actually built for, and he did not raise his voice, because you do not need to raise your voice to say a true thing.

"Come here," he said, not unkindly. "Look at my slate. One out doesn't sound like much, and you're right, one apple is one apple, a child could eat it and no one would starve." He tapped the chalk. "But you didn't miss one apple. You missed one in this basket, and one in that one, and one in every basket in every row from here to the middle, over and over, the same tiny kindness to yourself each time, and small things that keep happening stop being small. That's not a missing apple any more. That's a missing tree."

The Nearly went very still, the way the almost-invisible go still, which is more of a held breath than a stopping.

"And here's the worse of it," said Bramble, gentler still. "The orchard doesn't share the harvest on how many apples there are. It shares it on the number the scarecrows whistle, because everybody trusts the number. That's the whole trick of the place. And a number that's one out isn't a small lie, it's just a lie that happens to be standing very close to the truth. There's no such thing as a little bit trustworthy. A promise is kept or it isn't. One out is isn't."

There was a long quiet, and then, in the smallest voice yet, the Nearly said, "Oh," and it was the sound of a thing understanding for the first time in a hundred years that close and correct are not neighbours but opposites.

So they did the only thing that ever mends a near-miss, which is not cleverness exactly but the patience that looks like cleverness from the outside. They went basket by basket, back down the rows towards the heart, Bramble chalking, Nine watching with his sharp black eye and his one white feather, Pip cap-in-hand checking every tally against every count and Cornix standing guard over the growing pile of corrected numbers, and one by one they found the little missing ones and put them back, forty where it had said thirty-nine, until every basket weighed exactly as much as its promise.

And the Nearly, who had crept along beside them the whole way, watching one true count follow another, said at last, "I could do that. I could be the one who looks again. I'm ever so good at noticing when something is nearly right, it's the only thing I've ever been good at, only I always thought nearly right meant leave it alone." It brightened, very faintly, which for the Nearly was practically fireworks. "It could mean look harder."

"That," said Pip, putting his cap back on, "is exactly what it means. The orchard has a hundred things that look fine. It has always needed somebody whose whole job is to distrust fine." And so the Nearly was given the one honest task in all the world for a creature made of almost, which is to walk behind the careful ones and check the things nobody thinks need checking, precisely because it can feel the difference of a single apple in the dark.

They pressed on then, into the last narrow row, where the trees stood so old and close that their branches had long since shaken hands and forgotten to let go, and the whistling had stopped altogether, and the quiet was not the Hollow Bough's honoured quiet but something frailer, thinner, nearly nought.

And there at the very centre, planted the same day as the ancient Ninefold Tree and about as tired, stood the founding scarecrow, the one who had taught all nine to count, its straw gone to grey mist, its stitched face turned up as though it had been trying, for a century, to remember a number it had known better than its own name, and as Pip stepped into the last of the tea-coloured light it moved, only barely, and breathed out a single sound that was not a whistle and not a word, and might, if you were being kind, have been the beginning of one.

*Boss: The Nearly, resolved by re-auditing every basket and giving "almost" the honest job of checking what looks fine. Beating him unlocks Chapter Ten.*

## Chapter Ten: The Number That Was Everyone

They came to the centre of the orchard in the last hour before dawn, when the mist lay so thick between the nine rows that even Cornix, who could find a single dropped seed in a ploughed field, walked with his banded leg testing the ground ahead like a blind fiddler feeling for the edge of a stage.

And there it stood. The first scarecrow. The one that had taught all the others.

Pip had imagined it a hundred times on the road, past the Nearly and the Hollow Bough, past Old Roughweather groaning on his pole, past the whole long argument with the Maybe-Flock about whether a bird you could not quite see still counted. He had imagined something grand. What he found was a small grey shape on a leaning stick, its straw gone to the colour of breath on a cold morning, its stitched face turned up to a sky it could no longer read, and it was trying, with everything it had left, to remember a number.

You could see it trying. That was the worst of it.

"One," it whispered, and stopped, as though the word were a stone it had carried up a hill and could carry no further. "One, and. One, and."

Bramble set down his slate very quietly, the way you set down a cup in a room where someone is sleeping.

Pip took off his cap.

He had learned to do that when he needed to think properly, and he needed to think properly now, because he had come all this way believing, in the private countinghouse of his heart, that there would be a trick. There was always a trick. Scratch had forgotten his tally, and the trick had been to write it down so your head did not have to hold what your hand could keep. Strawless had lost his count, and the trick had been to gather things in tens, so that nine hundred sheep became ninety little flocks and then nine tidy heaps and then something a person could actually hold. Even Hollow Bough, who had frightened Pip most, had turned out to be a lesson and not a monster: how to tell an honest nothing, a true and solid zero, from the empty place where a number had simply fallen out.

So Pip knelt in the wet grass in front of the first scarecrow and he tried the tricks. All of them.

"You don't have to hold it," he said gently, and held up his own tally-stick, the notches worn smooth by a whole journey of thumbs. "Look. You count it once, and you cut the mark, and then the stick remembers so you don't have to. Here. I'll cut it for you. Just tell me the number."

The scarecrow turned its soft grey face towards the sound of him. "The number," it agreed, warmly, as you might agree with a kind stranger about the weather. And then, with terrible courtesy: "Which number is that, small friend?"

Pip tried grouping. He gathered nine fallen apples into a neat row and touched each one. He tried the honest zero, cupping his empty hands so the old thing could see there was nothing in them and that the nothing was real. He tried, at the last, simply counting out loud himself, one two three, slow and clear, the way you lead a very old song hoping the other voice will find the tune and come in.

None of it worked. And Pip understood, kneeling there, that none of it was ever going to.

Because this was not a lost tally. This was the place tallies came from, wearing out.

"Nine," said Nine, very softly, from Pip's shoulder. Not the number. His own name, said the way you say a thing when you finally see why it was given to you.

Pip looked up. The young crow had shuffled to the very end of Pip's shoulder, and his one white feather, the tenth feather he had never been able to explain, that he had only ever insisted "plainly mattered" in the way children are certain of things adults have forgotten, caught the first grey hint of the coming light and shone.

"There were only ever nine," Pip said slowly. His voice did not feel entirely like his own. "Nine rows. Nine scarecrows. It taught nine." He looked at the little worn thing on its stick, and the truth arrived in him not like a solved sum but like water finding the bottom of a bowl, filling him up from the feet. "It taught them by counting where they could see. That's the only way it ever knew. You watch it count, and you learn to count, the way you learn a face. And it can't. Not any more. It can't show anyone anything, ever again."

Bramble, who had recorded every day of the journey on his slate in his small careful hand, wrote nothing. He simply said, "Then it can't be mended, Pip." And, because Bramble had never once softened a true thing into a kind lie, "Mending it would only teach it to forget again slower. That's not what an orchard needs."

"No," Pip whispered.

What an orchard needs, when its founder is wearing out, is not a founder made new. Things do not go backwards. The apple does not climb back onto the branch. What it needs is a tenth.

And Pip realised, with a lurch that was mostly fear, that he already knew who.

The scarecrow spoke then, and for one moment, one single clear moment like a gap torn in the mist, it was all there. You could hear a whole century of careful counting stand up straight inside its voice.

"You have a tally-stick," it said, and its stitched eyes were on Pip, truly on him, seeing him. "You take your cap off to think. You came the whole way round, did you not. Every row. The one that halves. The one that guesses honestly. The one that checks itself twice. The one too big to count that you counted anyway. The empty one that was truly empty." A sound came out of it that was very nearly the whistle it had been born knowing. "You know the near-enough is a liar. You know a real zero from a hole. Small friend, you did not come here to save me."

Pip's throat had gone tight and strange. "I don't know how to be a whole orchard's counting," he said, and it came out very young.

"No one does," said the scarecrow, "until the morning they are. I did not, once." The grey face was so gentle. "I have held the number that is all of them, every hour, for a hundred years, and I am so tired, small friend, in a way I have no notch to measure. I am not asking you to mend me. I am asking you to let me put it down."

The mist shifted. Out of it, unhurried, came a broad steady shape on a strong pole, the eldest of the nine row-scarecrows, the Warden, who had felt something changing in the grain of the dawn and walked in from his row to stand witness, saying nothing, because some things do not want words laid on them, only company.

And behind him, or perhaps within the great dark trunk of the Ninefold Tree itself, a voice like sap moving under bark said, "It has carried them all, child. It has been the number that was everyone. It only wants to rest now, and rest is not the same as ending. Ask any orchard in winter."

Ninebark. The keeper of the memory. Watching, as it had watched the whole slow forgetting, patient as roots.

Pip stood up. He was chestnut-small and the moment was enormous and the two facts simply had to exist together.

He thought about home, and about counting apples for the pure clean pleasure of the count, back before he understood that counting was how a whole town trusted each other, how the harvest got shared so the small and the far and the late still got their fair nine. He had wanted, all his life, to count for the joy of it. Now something was asking him to count so that everyone else could stop being afraid, forever, and never once be thanked, the way the little grey thing had never once been thanked.

He was afraid. He let himself be afraid for the space of three breaths, and Nine pressed warm against his neck, and Bramble stood at his one side and Cornix at his other, close enough to hold him up and not one inch closer, because they loved him too well to make the choice for him.

Then Pip stepped forward, and reached up, and laid his small hand flat against the founding scarecrow's worn grey breast, over the place where a hundred years of counting lived.

"All right," he said. "You can put it down now. I've got it."

And the first light came over the nine rows, and the white tenth feather on Nine's wing lifted in a wind that had not been there a moment before, and the tired grey face let go of the number at last, and something began, very quietly, to pass from the old straw into the small warm hand, like a whistle changing hands in the dark.

## Chapter Eleven: Nine, and One More

The change did not come the way Pip had feared, all at once and terrible. It came the way dawn does, which is to say so gently that you cannot point to the moment it happens, only notice afterwards that it has.

He felt the counting first. Not numbers exactly, but the shape of the whole orchard laid out inside him, nine rows and every tree upon them, every branch, every apple swelling quietly in the new light, all of it settling into him as easily as breath. It should have been too much. A century of it had been too much, in the end, for the one who carried it before him. But Pip had walked every row to get here, and learned, on each one, a little more of how to hold a great deal without dropping any of it, and so the weight came into him and found, to its own faint surprise, that there was room.

His cap moved. He felt it unfurl above his brow, the small brown pip-shape of it softening and greening and putting out, of all the cheek in the world, leaves. A little sapling-crown, no taller than a thumb, breeze-stirred. His arms, when he looked, were part twig now and part woven straw, gold and pale and jointed in ways that did not hurt, only felt new, the way a coat feels new before it feels like yours. And in the middle of his chest, where his hemp-string satchel had hung the whole long way, a single green shoot pushed up out of the straw and opened one leaf to the sun, which is the orchard's oldest way of saying: still growing. Not finished. Begun.

The tally-stick he kept. Some things you carry not because you need them any more but because they remember the road with you, and a scarecrow, of all creatures, is allowed a keepsake.

Beside him the old founding scarecrow gave the smallest sound, and it was not a sad one. It was the sound a person makes settling at last into a chair after standing a very long time. The ancient straw loosened. It did not fall so much as return, sifting down soft and unhurried amongst the roots of the Ninefold Tree, gold going brown, brown going to earth, until there was nothing left standing there at all and nothing lost either, only the ground a little richer and the Tree a little fuller, as though it had taken a long breath in and decided to keep it.

Thank you, said Ninebark from within the wood, and it was hard to tell, afterwards, whether the Tree said it to the old scarecrow or the old scarecrow said it, through the Tree, to everyone.

Warden, who had come at dawn precisely to witness this, bowed his straw head very low and stayed that way a good while. It is what the eldest do, when one of the old ones puts a great task down and walks home into the roots. Nobody hurried him.

Then Pip, because he was the Tenth Scarecrow now and the hour had come round whether he felt ready or not, drew in a breath through his new green chest and whistled.

It was not a triumphant blast. He had thought, if he ever got here, that it might be. Instead what came out was quiet and clear and exactly counted, and it counted the one thing he most wanted counting, which was not the trees and not the apples but the friends. He whistled them, every one who had walked any part of the road with him, the whole gathered company standing hushed in the rows below, and the number came out true and round and his voice did not shake on a single beat of it. And then, at the very end, when he had counted them all, he added one more note, soft, deliberate, a little bit sly. Nine, and one more. Because Ninefold Orchard had not been mended back to what it was. It had grown, by exactly one, into what it had become.

Down amongst the trees, the company let out its held breath all together, which sounded rather like the orchard laughing.

Scratch stood in the third row, straw and steady, no longer the one who had been taught differently and so was first to forget, just an ordinary contented scarecrow with nothing to prove and nothing to dread, and when the count reached him he closed his stitched eyes and looked, for the first time in anyone's memory, entirely at peace. Old Roughweather was already whistling his own row over, exact to the apple, grumbling that the young ones today made such a fuss about a thing that was simply the job. Widdershins had got hold of a basket and was checking its sum with both paws and enormous glee, announcing the total twice in case anyone had missed the pleasure of it. Away at the orchard edge the finches of the Maybe-Flock, seventy-four now and proud of knowing it, settled onto the fence in tidy proud groups of ten with four sensible ones left over, and did not once pretend to be more or fewer than they were. High above, a single ribbon of the Murder wheeled over the rows just once, nine thousand crows exactly, a known and honest number wheeling in salute, and were gone. The Nearly stood by the harvest table double-checking a full basket against the tally, and when it came out right, exactly right, not nearly right, it beamed so wide you could have warmed your hands at it.

Thistlewick was near the hedge, tongue between his teeth, practising a whistle of his own under Strawless's patient eye, and it wobbled, and cracked, and went flat, and then, on the fourth go, held. Strawless said nothing, which from Strawless was the highest praise there was.

Bramble came trundling up to the foot of the Ninefold Tree with his slate held out in both paws, dark rings round his eyes shining. Warden took the slate, looked at the neat columns of every count Bramble had kept the whole way, and declared, in front of everyone, that this slate would be the orchard's official record from that hour on, kept dry, kept safe, kept honoured. Bramble went so pink beneath his banded spines that he had to pretend to polish the chalk.

Cornix eased himself down onto a low branch, the carved tally-ring loose now upon his old banded leg, and let his wings settle in a way they had not settled in years. His long scouting was over. The count was whole. An elder crow is permitted, once the counting is safe again, to simply sit in the sun, and he did.

And Nine, young Nine, glossy black-blue with his one white tenth feather, flew up and landed on Pip's new sapling-crown as though it had been grown there for exactly that purpose, which perhaps it had. He looked at his own white feather, the odd one, the extra one, the one that had never fitted the count of nine and had quietly bothered him his whole short life. And all at once he understood why it had always plainly mattered, and had always plainly been right, and he laughed out loud in the way only crows and children can.

Now, everybody knows, the way everybody knows the sun comes up and apples fall down and a debt paid kindly is never truly gone, that Ninefold Orchard has nine rows and nine scarecrows, and always has, and always will.

Everybody, these days, is a little bit wrong about that, and could not be happier to be.
