# gf_delphine — "The Name in the Margin"

**tagline**
A strongbox has gone from the counting room, an Under runner boy is taking the blame for it, and Delphine Ashworth has never in her life let a good scandal go uninvestigated.

---

**intro**
Delphine Ashworth was, by her own cheerful estimate, the best-informed officer at Ashcombe Yard, and she had the duty room's full attention to prove it.

She was mid-anecdote about a Reeve's daughter and a borrowed hat when the sergeant came in at a pace that meant something had actually happened, which was rare enough to be worth noticing on its own.

The counting room strongbox had gone in the night. Forty-odd marks in collected conduct fines, out of a locked room with one officer meant to be watching the corridor outside it. Standing already accused, wedged between two constables twice his size, was an Under runner boy called Fossey, who carried post along that corridor most evenings and had, unhelpfully for himself, been seen doing exactly that the evening before.

"Convenient," murmured Delphine, not quite quietly enough.

Across the room, the new one, Corvain, two months in the coat and not yet twenty-one, looked up from her own paperwork without any particular expression.

"Something to add, Ashworth?"

"Probably," said Delphine, and drifted, with the unhurried confidence of somebody who has never once doubted she was allowed to be anywhere, over to the roster pinned by the door.

The roster was the sergeant's usual scrawl, name after name in a hand like a bad-tempered fence, right up to one line.

WYNDHAM, marked absent the night of the theft, in the sergeant's own ink.

Delphine had about as long as it took the sergeant to stop talking to Fossey and turn round.

Being quick, for once, was going to matter considerably more than being charming, which was not the way round she was used to and which she found she rather resented.

**crossroads**
Three routes, the way there always are once you start asking questions instead of merely enjoying other people's.

The Yard's own archive held the posting rosters going back years, tedious but exact, and a clerk who filed them.

The counting-house across the square kept the ledger of who signed in and out of every corridor in the building, minded at night by a man who did not care for visitors.

And the Brindle Chop-House three streets down was where junior officers went to lose money to each other on purpose, and where, if the gossip Delphine had spent a year cultivating was worth anything at all, somebody would eventually say too much.

**archive_intro**
The Yard's archive smelled of dust and other people's mistakes, and was minded by a Laachmi clerk called Ilsevet: tall, horned and entirely unmoved by rank.

"Rosters," said Ilsevet, when Delphine had explained what she wanted, "exist to be checked. Not gossiped about. Though I don't suppose that has ever stopped anybody."

The corridor watch outside the counting room went round four officers in the same order it had gone round for as long as anybody had kept the book. Colque, Pell, Wyndham, Ashe, and back to Colque.

"Which means," said Ilsevet, without being asked, "that his name being on that night is not a discovery. His name was always going to be on that night. Everybody in this building could have told you that, including him."

"Then the circle isn't correcting anything," said Delphine, slowly. "There was nothing to correct. He was down for it and he was marked absent, and both of those are right."

"There," said Ilsevet, with the closest thing to approval a Laachmi clerk permits itself, and turned the ledger round.

"Somebody wanted it to look decided," said Ilsevet. "It was already decided. That is the interesting part, and nobody in this building has been interested in it for two days."

**finale**
Reeve Ashcombe read the docket from the first page to the last without once looking up, while Wyndham stood very still and Fossey stood very still in an entirely different way, the way people stand when it has just occurred to them that the ground was ever going to hold.

"Dereliction," Ashcombe said at last. "Not theft. A distinction that will matter to you a great deal more than to anybody else in this room, Wyndham, and I would advise you to work out why on your own time."

He turned to Fossey, and something in his face changed.

"You're free to go. With the Yard's apology, such as it is worth."

Across the counting room the new one, Corvain, caught Delphine's eye and gave her the smallest possible nod, which from somebody two months in the coat ought to have been worth nothing at all and somehow was not.

Delphine allowed herself exactly one triumphant smile.

Then it occurred to her, entirely unwelcome, that Fossey had stood accused for two whole days with two constables' hands on him before one single person in that building had thought to check whether the paper protecting him was honest.

Two days. And the only reason anybody had checked was that a circle on a roster had happened to be in a hand she did not recognise, and she had happened to be standing near the door, and she had happened to be bored.

It was not a comfortable thought. She decided, with some reluctance, to keep it.

**end — goodText**
The story of how Delphine Ashworth had unpicked a cover-up with a torn chit, a wrong-coloured seal and a conversation in a chop-house went round the Yard by suppertime, gaining several flattering embellishments on the way, none of which she troubled to correct.

She was, after all, mid-anecdote about it before the ink on Ashcombe's report had dried.

What she kept back rather longer than the rest of the story was the last thing she had written, privately, on the docket's final page, in her own hand, where nobody was ever going to read it.

That the next time a name got quietly circled on a roster, innocent until proven otherwise ought to run both ways, Wrought and Under alike.

And that she was twenty-two years old and had never once, in her entire comfortable life, had cause to wonder whether it did.

**end — midText**
The case closed well enough. Wyndham was disciplined, Fossey was cleared, and Delphine's part in it was noted, filed and largely forgotten by everybody except Delphine, who made quite sure it was not forgotten by her.

She told the story rather more often than the facts strictly required.

Rather less often, though, without at least one honest aside about how close it had come to going the other way for an Under boy who had never had a circled name of his own to save him, and about how the only thing that had saved him was that an officer with nothing better to do that morning had happened to be standing near a door.
