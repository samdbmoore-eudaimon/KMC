# gf_nettle — "The Cold Pane"

**tagline**
Kept in by a cough that will not shift, Nettle has watched one window for six weeks. There is nobody on Ferrow Lane who knows the lane better, and tonight it is going to matter.

---

**intro**
She was meant to be asleep. Mother Halloway had said so twice, once at supper and once with the candle held up close to see whether the cough had gone sharp again, and Nettle had nodded both times and meant it both times, more or less.

But the room had gone cold in the particular way it went cold before the Turn knocked, and there was a stretch of bare glass over her bed that nobody else in the house had any reason to look at.

She got up on her knees on the boards, breathed a patch of warm onto the pane, and waited.

Six weeks she had been in this bed. Six weeks of one window, and what she had found out in that time was this: everybody in Ferrow Lane went out into the fog and waited it out and came in again, and not one of them had ever watched it arrive. They were in it. You cannot see a thing you are standing in.

She could see the whole lane from here. She could see the grate at the mouth of it, which nobody standing down there could.

Down at the end of the lane the outfall gave its first low knock, felt through the floor and the bedframe and her own chest, and Nettle put her face closer to the cold and started looking properly.

---

**skillcheck1**
The frost came up out of the corner of the pane the moment the fog began to rise outside, feathering across the glass ahead of her own breath.

That was the tell and she had learned it the hard way, in the third week, when she had told Tessa it would be a short one and it had not been. A hard vent laid the frost down thin and fast and right across the pane. A soft one crept, and it stopped short of the middle, and it went the colour of dishwater rather than white.

This one was going quickly. She thought. She pressed her sleeve over her mouth to keep her breath off the glass and made herself look at it properly instead of deciding what she wanted it to be.

---

**item1_found**
Thin, and fast, and right across. A hard one, then, and a long one after it.

She went to chalk it up on the underside of the sill where the candle never reached, and her fingers found the corner board loose, tipping up under her nail like something that wanted opening.

In the dusty hollow underneath was a stub of proper slate-chalk worn down to nearly nothing. And scored into the wood beneath it, small and careful and not in her hand at all, a row of marks somebody had kept for a while and then stopped keeping.

She knew the hand. Rooke had been ill in this bed too, four winters ago, and had got better, and had never once mentioned it.

Nettle held the chalk stub for a moment before she used it.

---

**item1_missed**
Her own breath got to the pane before she was sure, and the frost went to a smear, and by the time it cleared the thing had already made up its mind without telling her which it was.

She was crosser with her chest than with the glass. There was a bit of ordinary chalk in her apron pocket, not as good, and chalk is chalk.

Whatever was under the loose corner board stayed under it for another night.

**gate_fight** (scene intro)
Something scrabbled at the sill and Nettle nearly went off the bed.

Flattened against the outside ledge with her tail going was the lane's stray tabby, the one the children called nothing in particular and fed anyway. She had picked the one bad spot on the whole terrace to shelter in, directly above the outfall's mouth, and the knocking had her ears down and her claws into the rotten wood and no intention of moving anywhere sensible.

Nettle could not open the window. If she opened the window the cough would have her for two days and Mother Halloway would know exactly why.

So she put her palm flat on the inside of the glass, and tapped.

**Tuppence, in a Fright — hitText**
Nettle finds the beat the pipes are already making and taps it on the glass, slow and dull and the same every time, and one of the cat's ears comes half up.

**Tuppence, in a Fright — strikeText**
The ledge creaks and shifts and Tuppence goes flatter instead of calmer, close enough to the grate's mouth that Nettle's stomach goes with her.

**Tuppence, in a Fright — defeatText**
Tuppence gets both ears up, decides the whole business was beneath her, and shuffles along the ledge to the dry corner away from the mouth without once appearing to be doing what she was asked. She is still furious. She is also out of it.

---

**gate_win**
Tuppence settled into the dry corner with the air of a cat who had always been going there.

Nettle let her breath out slowly and put her forehead against the cold glass for a moment, which was the closest thing to going outside available to her.

Down the lane, muffled through the glass and the fog together, there were voices. Somebody else was up far too late, and by the sound of them, not pleased about it.

---

**gate_retreat**
Fright is not a thing that always listens to a good beat on a windowpane, and tonight it did not.

Nettle stopped rather than keep pestering a frightened animal, and after a minute Tuppence worked her own way along the ledge anyway, complaining the entire distance.

Down the lane, through the glass and the fog, voices carried, with that edge on them that people get when they have been up too long and something has gone wrong.

**crossroads**
There was a post-line strung down Ferrow Lane, window to sill to lamp-post, a good length of cord the children kept up so things could go along the terrace without anybody having to go out in the wet.

Somebody was tugging it. Three short pulls, which meant now.

Nettle reeled the tin basket in along the sill and found three separate pieces of trouble waiting in it, chalked in three different hands.

She could send one answer back down the line before the fog closed the lane. Not three. One.

---

**routeA_setup**
The first was in Cobb's blotchy careful hand.

He was the coal-man's boy and he did six houses on Ferrow Lane with a handcart heavier than he was, and he was also, and Nettle was the only person in the terrace who knew it, taking eight grist a week off the chalk at Marta's and putting it in his own pocket. She had watched him do it from this window eleven times and had never said.

Tonight his round took him straight past the mouth grate, and he did not know the mouth grate went first. Nobody down there knew. From the street it all looked like one fog arriving at once.

Get it wrong and he would be standing in it with a cart he could not leave and coal that could not get wet.

---

**routeA_good**
Nettle chalked one word, GO NOW, and sent the basket rattling away down the line.

She watched him read it. She watched him look up the lane at the mouth grate, which was still doing nothing at all, and she watched him decide to believe a windowpane anyway, and get the cart moving.

He was clear of it with room to spare when the mouth went.

The basket came back up a while later with a shard of lamp-glass off his cart lantern inside it, edges clean, not a crack in it. No note. Boys of that age do not write thank you to a window.

Nettle turned it over in the candlelight. Perfectly clear.

---

**routeA_mid**
She was not certain enough, fast enough, and while she was still deciding the basket went, tugged away by a Cobb who had not got the patience to wait on an answer he was not sure was coming.

He got past it. He got past it at a dead run with the cart bouncing and his shoulder into it, and arrived at the far end red in the face and dry, and it had nothing to do with her.

Nothing came back up the line. Some things you only get offered once.

---

**routeB_setup**
The second note was in a spidery elderly hand. Old Casser, the last lamplighter on Ferrow Lane, now that anybody young enough for it had gone to the forges or the outfall gangs where the money was.

She was three rungs up the post with the mantle unlit and the fog already round her ankles, and she needed both hands to strike it before the damp got into the gas and fouled it for the night.

Nettle had watched Old Casser light that lamp about two hundred times from this pillow. She knew something about it that Old Casser did not, because Old Casser was always at the bottom of the ladder looking up.

---

**routeB_good**
It depended which end the fog was coming from, and from up here you could see which end.

Off the sump it came low and wet and it fouled the gas every time. Off the outfall it came hot, and hot fog dried the mantle instead of soaking it, and you could strike into it all night.

Tonight it was coming off the outfall.

Nettle chalked STRIKE IT, and the basket went down, and the mantle caught with a soft pop about nine seconds later while the white was still coming up past Old Casser's knees.

Sarn came down the ladder and stood in the road looking up at Nettle's window for a moment. Later a twist of paper came up the line with a glass roundel in it out of her repair kit, clean and round and about the size of a thing that might sit in a frame.

---

**routeB_mid**
She could not tell which end it was coming from. The lane had already gone white at the middle and both ends looked the same and she was not going to send an answer she had made up.

She sent NOT SURE, which was the honest thing and did nobody any good at all.

Old Casser tried it anyway, because a dark street is a dark street, and the gas fouled, and she came down the ladder with a bruised palm and a lamp that stayed out until morning. She was not cross. She was tired, which was worse.

Nothing came up the line.

---

**routeC_setup**
The third note was not chalk. It was Tuppence, back at the sill, yowling now instead of frightened quiet, going up and down the ledge and refusing all comfort.

Down below, half under the grate cover where it had come loose from its frame, three small shapes were mewing in a huddle, too far in for a grown hand.

Tessa had come out onto the step in her nightclothes to find out what the noise was and was crouched at the grate with her fingers under the cold iron edge. The cover had rusted half into its seat. It would come, but only if it came in one heave, and only if the heave happened before the mouth of the lane went.

And Tessa, down on the step in it, could not see the mouth of the lane.

---

**routeC_challenge** (scene intro)
Nettle got the window open two inches, which she would pay for, and put her mouth to the gap.

She could see the whole of it from up here: the mouth grate, the middle of the lane, her sister on her knees on the step with both hands in the iron. Tessa could see about four feet of wet stone.

"Wait," said Nettle, through the gap. "Wait. Wait."

Tuppence went round the step in circles, beside herself, swiping at nothing, unable to help and unwilling to leave, and every time she went past Tessa's ankles she nearly had her over.

**Tuppence, Beside Herself — hitText**
Nettle gets her palm on the glass and finds the same dull beat she used on the ledge, and Tuppence hears it and stops going round in circles.

**Tuppence, Beside Herself — strikeText**
Tuppence goes between Tessa's ankles with a yowl and nearly puts her onto the stone with both hands still under the iron.

**Tuppence, Beside Herself — defeatText**
Tuppence sits down, tail wrapped tight, and waits instead of scrabbling. Nettle sees the mouth grate go white and says NOW, and Tessa takes the cover up in one heave, and three furious wet kittens come out into the light and straight into their mother's undivided attention.

---

**routeC_good**
The kittens went into a heap against Tuppence and the cover went back down level, and Tessa sat on the step in her nightclothes in the cold and got her breath.

Then she looked into the hole where the cover had been and reached in.

What came out was a shard of glass, long fallen down there and forgotten, rinsed clean by every vent that had ever gone over it and somehow never once cracked.

She climbed the step, wrapped it in a bit of her own shawl, and sent it up the post-line to the only person on Ferrow Lane who would know what to do with a clean piece of glass.

Then she came inside and shut Nettle's window without saying anything about it being open.

---

**routeC_mid**
Nettle said NOW half a breath late, or Tessa heard it half a breath late, and it came to the same thing: the cover jammed harder instead of coming clear.

Tessa got her fingers back out of the iron, which was the right choice and did not feel like it, and decided the kittens would have to sit out one more venting where they were, huddled and out of the worst of it, until somebody steadier could try again.

No glass came up the line. Only Tessa's hand raised through the murk, tired, and meaning thank you anyway.

**converge1**
Whatever had happened at the far end, the post-line went quiet eventually and the tin basket came to rest against the sill and stayed there.

Nettle sat back against the pillow with the chalk stub and whatever the lane had sent her, and found she was not finished.

Because the frost was the thing she was worst at. Everything else she could see. The frost she had to read in the four seconds before her own breathing wrecked it, and she had been getting it wrong about one time in three since the third week.

She wanted something she could hold flat against the cold glass. Something that would keep the shape where it was long enough to be looked at properly.

**dice2_setup**
Whatever she built would want something dry behind it, and there was a scrap of good waxed paper snagged on a nail across the lane, blown off somebody's parcel and left there.

The brass hook on the post-line could just about reach it if she swung the basket right. The nail was slick and the paper was thin enough to go through if she pulled at the wrong moment.

---

**comp2_good**
The basket swung out, caught, and came back with the whole scrap still on the hook, dry and sound and about the size to sit behind a small pane.

Nettle smoothed it flat on her knee.

Two pieces of whatever this was going to be, and nobody had given her either of them for being poorly.

---

**comp2_mid**
The hook went off the nail instead of onto it, and the paper, damp already, tore clean across and went down into the water below where it was not coming back from.

She wound the cord in and sat with it. A frame could manage without paper if it had to. It would just have to be the plainer sort of frame.

**skillcheck3_setup**
Downstairs, through the boards, Mother Halloway and Rooke were not arguing.

That was the thing about that house. Nobody argued. They went quiet, and the quiet went on for a while, and then somebody said a number.

Nettle lay absolutely still with her face turned away from the door and listened to the quiet, and then heard the number, which was five marks ten, and then heard Rooke say it twice.

Five marks ten was a bottle. Nettle knew what it was because she had asked Tessa in the fourth week and Tessa had told her rather than lie about it.

The quiet after that went on a long time. Then Rooke said something too low to make out, and Mother Halloway said, quite clearly, "You will not."

---

**comp3_good**
Nettle lay still and did not cough, which took some doing, because holding it in is worse than letting it out and she was not going to let them hear it just then.

After a while the stairs went.

A hand came round the door with a length of curtain wire in it, pulled straight out of the mending basket, and put it on the end of the bed.

"Seeing as you're building something up here instead of sleeping," said Mother Halloway.

She did not come in and she did not put the candle up, because if she had put the candle up she would have had to see Nettle's face and Nettle would have had to see hers.

The door went shut. Neither of them ever mentioned it.

---

**comp3_mid**
The cough got out.

It got out on the third one and it was a bad one and it went on, and downstairs the quiet stopped being the money kind and became the other kind, and somebody came up two at a time.

There was water, and a hand on her back, and Mother Halloway sitting on the edge of the bed for nine minutes saying nothing much.

No wire came up. Nettle would not have taken it that night anyway.

**forge** (scene intro)
Nettle laid out what the night had brought her on the blanket. A clean scrap of lamp-glass. A dry piece of waxed paper, if she had kept it whole. A length of bent curtain wire.

She turned them over by candlelight and worked out how somebody who could not get out of bed might still make a thing worth having.

**forge — forged**
The wire went round the glass easily enough to make a frame, and the waxed paper backed it soft so nothing scratched, and by the time she had the corners bound with thread the whole business sat light and neat in her palm.

A small hinged pane. Press it flush against the cold window and the frost came up behind clear glass instead of behind her own breathing, and stayed where it was, and could be looked at for as long as she liked.

She turned it over once and was pleased with it right down to her feet.

The Cold Pane is hers.

**forge — notforged**
Two out of three is not nothing, she told herself, and it was not, and it was also not a thing that worked.

A frame with no glass in it is a frame. A pane with nothing to hold it is a pane.

She wrapped what she had in her handkerchief and put it under the loose corner board where the chalk stub had been, next to somebody else's marks from four winters ago, for a night when the post-line was quieter and her hands were steadier.

---

**finale**
By the time the lane cleared properly Ferrow Lane had gone quiet in the way it went quiet just before the grey came up.

Nettle held the pane flat to the glass and watched the next frost come up behind it, clean and still and unsmudged, and she could see the whole of it, and she did not have to be quick about it.

Down the lane Cobb's cart stood empty outside the last house on his round. Old Casser's lamp was burning small and gold in the murk. On the step Tessa was asleep sitting up with three kittens in her lap and their mother watching all four of them with deep suspicion.

Somewhere below, Rooke came in. Nettle knew the sound of it; she knew all their feet by now. Rooke stopped at the bottom of the stairs for a while and then did not come up, which meant she had thought about it and decided not to wake anybody.

Five marks ten, thought Nettle, and put the pane under her pillow, and did not cough.

---

**end — goodText**
It got round Ferrow Lane the way useful things get round, which is quietly and without anybody deciding.

If your business wanted timing against the Turn, you sent word up the post-line to Halloway's youngest at the window, the one with the bit of glass and the cough, and she would tell you which end it was coming from and how long the mouth had.

Cobb took to knocking his cart-wheel twice on the cobbles before every round, which she could not hear and liked to imagine. He also stopped taking the eight grist off the chalk at Marta's, in the same week, and never knew that she had known, and she never told him.

Mother Halloway never said a word about the loose board or the wire out of the mending basket.

Nettle slept with the pane on the pillow beside her, and she was still ill, and she was worth something, and those two things turned out to be able to sit in the same bed.

---

**end — midText**
It was a scrappier night than she would have wanted. A torn scrap here, half a breath late there, and a pane held together with more thread than wire in places.

But Cobb got home dry, Old Casser's lamp got lit eventually, and there were three kittens asleep in Tessa's lap by the time the lane cleared, and that was the whole of what the night had asked.

Nettle put the unfinished thing under the loose board for another evening and pulled the blanket up over the cough that had not shifted yet.

Five marks ten, she thought, which was the only number she cared about and the only one nobody had asked her to work out.
