# gf_pip — "Given a Face on Purpose"

**tagline**
It is Chalk-Day in the Under, and small Pip has to find his family's place in a queue of hundreds, on his own, for the first time.

---

**intro**
Chalk-Day came round on Ferrow Lane every fortnight, rain or shine or vent-fog, and it was the day the corner shop's slate got settled and the coal ration got counted out at Cindergate Hall.

It had always been Rooke who took him, with a hand round his wrist through the worst of the crowd and an answer ready before the clerk had finished asking the question. Rooke was thirteen now and had four jobs and a household to hold up, and this fortnight there was simply nobody spare, and so it was Pip who had the family slate and the little cloth purse of grist and cogs, and Pip who had to bring the fortnight home.

He was seven, and small even for seven.

He had practised tying the purse-string the night before until his fingers ached, because a purse-string that comes loose in that yard is a purse-string that comes loose for good.

He went down Ferrow Lane buttoned to the chin with the whole weight of Number Twelve balanced in one fist.

"So you'll tell me what's on it," she said, "and I'll take your word, because you look like a careful sort of boy."

Pip stood on the step with the purse in his hand and understood, with a small cold clarity that a seven year old should not have available, that he could say almost anything.

He could say less than the slate said. She would take it. Nobody in the world would know, and there would be coins left in the purse afterwards, and there was coal at the end of this morning.

He put the purse on the counter and tipped the whole of it out.

**crossroads**
The yard was cut into three ragged lanes by a dry fountain nobody had seen work, and a chalked board nailed to its rim read FERROW, GULLIVER, ASHDOWN in letters that were supposed to sort several hundred people by street.

Nobody was standing where the board said. Nobody ever was. The crowd was thicker than Pip had seen it and there was vent-fog on the wind already, which meant everybody in that yard would be pushing before the hour was out.

He was going to have to find his own way to somewhere that made sense.

**finale**
The Turn vented exactly then, as it did four times a day whether the Under was ready or not, and a wall of hot white came down between the Hall and the yard, and the whole queue surged forward at once in the particular panic of people who cannot see where the front of a line has got to.

Coats and caps and satchels all went sideways together. Pip was lifted half off his feet and set down again facing entirely the wrong way, and before he had got one breath back he was inside a roped-off corner with perhaps forty other small children in identical charity grey, all of them somebody's, none of them at that moment anybody in particular.

A clerk somewhere in the fog, unable to see faces, had started counting coats.

"Forty-one." Not unkindly. Only tired, and ticking a slate. "Forty-one grey coats, small, various."

Pip stood very still, the way you do when you are trying not to become more lost than you already are, and shut his fist round the tag at his collar.

He was not crying. He was seven and he was going to, quite soon, and he knew it, and he was holding it off out of a stubbornness he had got from somebody in that house without ever being taught it.

It was a Laachmi who found him. Ledger-Keeper Vell, who kept the main book, tall and horned, working her way along the rope looking at faces instead of coats, the way almost nobody bothered to any more.

She stopped in front of him.

"I know you," she said, and it was not a question. "Halloway. The little one who used to come in holding his sister's wrist and correct my ledger from the queue. Cheeky article."

She got down to his height, which for a Laachmi meant folding a considerable distance, and read the brass tag at his collar without needing to be shown it.

"Pip Halloway," she said. "There. That's a good deal better than small, various."

He found he could breathe again, all at once, in the way you only notice once you had stopped.

**end — goodText**
Vell walked him back through it herself with one warm dry hand on his shoulder the whole way, and at the ledger desk she wrote HALLOWAY, NO. 12 FERROW LANE, HEARTH OF SIX against the coal ration in her own careful copperplate. Not a tally. A name, with a street and a hearth attached to it, the way it ought always to have been done.

She put the docket in his hand along with a peppermint out of a tin she evidently kept for exactly this, and told him to give her regards to his sister, since she rather thought Ferrow Lane was wasting a girl who could hold a whole ledger in her head at thirteen.

Pip walked home through the thinning fog with the docket in one fist and the tag warm at his collar, working out quite privately exactly how he was going to tell Rooke about all of it.

He told her that evening, on the back step, at enormous length, and she listened to the whole thing without once interrupting, which she had never done before, and afterwards she asked to see the tag and held it for a while and gave it back and did not say anything at all.

**end — midText**
It took a soaking on the steps, a chalk-stub or two gone for good, and one thoroughly frightening minute among forty strangers in identical grey coats, but Vell found him anyway, and walked him to the desk, and wrote his name against the family's coal instead of leaving him a number in a headcount.

Some of the finer things stayed lost that morning. A peg down a gap. A weight kept by a tired countkeeper. They would keep for a steadier day.

The docket was fair and the name was his own. He walked home through the last of the fog turning that over, and decided it had been, on the whole, a good sort of terrifying.

He told Rooke about it that evening and left out the part in the roped corner, because he did not have the words for that part yet, and because he had a feeling she would work it out anyway.
