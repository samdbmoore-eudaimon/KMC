# gf_colworth — "The Interest That Was Never Owed"

**tagline**
A widow's chalk debt has grown teeth over eleven unwitnessed years, and someone patient had better read the statute all the way to the bottom.

---

**intro**
Magistrate Colworth's court sat in the back half of the Sump Bench, a low brick room in the Under with one window too grimed to let much through and a stove that argued with itself all winter. On a good day it heard boundary squabbles, unpaid writs and the odd dispute over a stolen barrow. On a bad day it heard the same, only slower.

Colworth liked it that way. Liking is perhaps too warm a word. Colworth found it tolerable, which for Colworth amounted to the same thing. He worked through the morning's stack of dockets the way a mill works through grain: steadily, without curiosity and without much caring what came out the other end, provided it came out in the right shape.

The stack that morning held the usual: two rent disputes, a chalk debt and a complaint about a goat. He was three dockets in and entirely unmoved by any of them when the chalk debt came round again, this time with a Distress Writ pinned to its corner and a number on it that made even his tired eye pause for exactly one second longer than habit allowed.

One second. That was where this whole business started.

---

**docket_check**
The writ was Strick versus Loom: an old grocer's tab against Sarn Loom, widow of a lamplighter, for a debt that had begun, eleven years back, at three sovereigns and had somehow grown into something Strick's covering note called 'compounded per the usual custom, five in the hundred, every year, as is proper.'

'As is proper' was doing a great deal of work for four words. Colworth had read ten thousand dockets in his time and had learned, the slow way, that whenever a phrase worked that hard, it was usually carrying something it did not want carried openly.

He did not reach for his gavel. He reached for the drawer where the old clerk before him had kept a magnifying loupe, on the grounds that if a phrase was going to lie to him, it could at least do so in large print.

---

**loupe_found**
The loupe was still there, wedged behind a box of nib cases nobody had thrown out in a decade, its brass gone dull but its glass true. Through it, the covering note gave up its small print without complaint: a footnote, half a line, citing 'the Common Floor' as the fallback rate for any tab not properly renewed.

Colworth did not know yet what the Common Floor was. He knew only that a footnote citing it had been included, and that nobody in the room seemed to want him reading it. In his experience, that was usually the whole case, sitting quietly in six-point type, waiting to be noticed.

**dispute_won**

**crossroads**
Three days, or a week: either way, the question was the same. Somewhere behind Strick's tidy compounding sat the truth of what Sarn Loom actually owed, and somewhere behind that sat the reason nobody had wanted Colworth reading footnotes in the first place.

There were three ways to find it. He could walk to Strick's shop himself and go through the ledger line by line. He could send to the Roll Office for whatever original paperwork had survived eleven years of filing. Or he could have Strick's own clerk brought in and asked, plainly, to explain himself.

None of the three appealed to him more than the others. Colworth did not, as a rule, have preferences. He picked the one that seemed most likely to produce an answer that would hold up.

**shop_route**

'Magistrate. If this is about the Loom account, I've done nothing but what's customary. Five in the hundred, every year, same as everyone charges.'

'Custom is not the same word as lawful,' said Colworth, 'though I grant they are often seen together. Show me the docket. The original, if you still have it.'

**shop_good**

'Everyone,' said Colworth, 'is not, on this evidence, doing it lawfully.' He took the single signed sheet, wrapped it with the care he gave to anything that might later be needed in a hurry, and left Strick standing behind his own counter looking, for the first time in the conversation, rather smaller than his shop.

**finale**
The corrected writ went out under Colworth's own seal: four sovereigns and sixty-five hundredths, payable at a rate Sarn Loom could actually meet without losing her rooms over it, and a note appended to Strick's shop licence reminding him, in the driest language the Act allowed, that custom is not law and never has been, however comfortably the two sit together when nobody checks.

It was, in the grand ledger of the Sump Bench, a small case. Three sovereigns, some interest, a widow, a grocer and a clerk who would think rather more carefully about strongboxes from now on. Nobody outside the Under would ever hear of it. Colworth filed it away himself, in the ordinary manner, and found, somewhat to his own surprise, that he minded rather less than usual doing so.

He would hear larger cases before the year was out, cases with rather more at stake than four sovereigns and a widow's rooms, cases that would take considerably longer than an afternoon and matter considerably more than chalk. One of them, when it finally reached his lectern, would concern a young Sirrath and a Calibration result that did not add up either, though that was a docket for another day, and today's was smaller, and simpler, and entirely his own.

For now, Colworth closed the Loom file, set it on the shelf where closed files went, and reached, without any particular feeling one way or the other, for the next docket in the stack. It was, he noted, considerably more legible than the last one. Small mercies. He had learned to take them where the stack allowed.

The Sump Bench's clerks spoke of the Loom ruling for months afterward, in the careful, approving way clerks speak of paperwork that finally came out right. Sarn Loom kept her rooms, paid down the true debt at a rate that left her enough for coal and sent Colworth, entirely against custom, a small parcel of dried lavender with no note attached, which he kept in his desk drawer and never once explained to anyone who asked.

Strick amended his practice, grudgingly at first and then, as the months passed, rather more thoroughly than the law strictly required, on the grounds that a second visit from the Sump Bench was not an experience he cared to repeat.
