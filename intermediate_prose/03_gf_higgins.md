# gf_higgins — "The Seven and a Half Rule"

**tagline**
Sump Gate Seven vents four times a day, on the tick, without exception. Corporal Higgins has never once needed to wonder what an exception would look like.

---

**intro**
Sump Gate Seven sat where Tallow Street stopped pretending the Rise and the Under were the same city, and admitted it outright. Oxblood brick above the seam, soot brick below it, and between the two a grate the size of a dining table, bolted into the road like something that expected trouble and intended to be ready for it.

Corporal Higgins had stood this post for six years. In six years he had never once been late for the click.

The click came first, a knock felt up through the boots, the Turn clearing its throat before it spoke. Then the count of forty, while the fog gathered itself in the culvert below. Then the seal, both grates down, nobody crossing for love, money or a superior's writ. Then the long count, one hundred and fifty, while the fog got on with its scalding, iron-eating business and thought better of coming up any further. Then the reopening, and the docket check, and the next lawful soul across the seam.

It was written down. It had always been written down. Higgins had read the Vent Order so many times he could recite it walking backwards in the dark, which he had in fact done once, for a bet he did not remember losing, to a colleague he did not much like.

'Correct in every particular,' his old training sergeant used to say, tapping the card, 'and you will never once have to decide anything for yourself.' Higgins had found this the single most restful sentence in the language, and had built six years of a career on it.

This particular morning the eight o'clock queue for the boiler sheds was backed up past the second bollard, because the Rise-side clatterlift had thrown a cable in the night, and eight o'clock was also klaxon time, and the crowd did not know that yet. Which was, in its entirety, Higgins's job to fix before the click.

**count_check**
Forty workers to a lane and a click already ringing up through the cobbles. Higgins had exactly as long as the count of forty to clear the crowd behind the yellow line, and the counting had to be exact, because 'about forty' is a fine number for a poem and a hanging offence for a gatekeeper.

He called it aloud, the way the manual said to, one number to a step, the crowd shuffling back on his voice like a tide that had finally met somebody more stubborn than it was.

**whistle_found**
The count landed on thirty-nine, the lane cleared with a full second to spare, and the grate housing gave a small metallic cough as the seal dropped. Something rattled loose inside it and skittered to Higgins's boot.

A whistle. Brass, dented, the cord long since rotted away, with DOONE scratched into the barrel in a hand that had clearly done it in a hurry, forty years before Higgins was born. Old Gatekeeper Doone had stood this exact post once, by the look of it, and had also, at some point, stopped needing the whistle.

Higgins blew it once, out of professional curiosity. It worked perfectly. He put it on his own cord and did not consider the matter further, which was the correct amount of consideration for a Wednesday.

The seal dropped anyway, in time, because the seal did not care how the count had felt about it. Whatever had been rattling loose in the grate housing stayed lost in there for another day.

**gate_fight** (scene intro)
Something in the sealed culvert did not want to stay sealed. A lump of hardened slag and old fog, about the size of a coal sack, had wedged itself sideways in the outfall throat, and the pressure building behind it was making the whole grate hum a note Higgins did not care for.

The manual had a name for these. A Clinker: cooled fog and grit, fused by heat into something that could think just enough to be stubborn about doorways. Left alone it would either blow the seal early or crack the grate outright, and neither of those was going to happen on Higgins's watch, on Higgins's grate, on a Wednesday.

'Move along,' said Higgins, in the flat tone the manual recommended for uncooperative masonry. The Clinker did not move along. The Clinker glowed a shade angrier and pushed.

**gate_win**
The Clinker's remains were swept into a bucket for the cinder cart, and the grate went back to humming its usual, entirely acceptable hum.

'Third one this month,' said Higgins, to nobody, because there was nobody else on duty to say it to. Third Clinker, third grate, third little fault the Turn hadn't quite managed to correct on its own. It always managed the first ninety-nine steps by itself, the tinkers said. It was the hundredth step it couldn't do without a person going first. Higgins found this a reasonable division of labour and did not lose any sleep over which of the two of them was doing the dangerous part.

**gate_retreat**
The Clinker got the better of the exchange, vented a last spiteful gust through the bars, and Higgins pulled the party back to the yellow line to reconsider the approach with slightly less exposed skin.

They went round by the footbridge instead, which added the better part of an hour and a great deal of standing in a queue that had opinions about queue-jumpers. Behind them the grate kept humming its unhappy note, and would go on doing so until somebody came back to finish the job properly.

**crossroads**
The morning's real trouble, once the Clinker was dealt with, turned out to be entirely human, and arrived in triplicate.

At the head of the boiler-shed queue, foreman Crick was doing arithmetic under his breath and not liking the answer. Further up the lane, a Rise widow by the name of Anstruther was explaining, at volume, exactly whose grate this was going to stop being if somebody didn't move faster. And down by the old culvert stile, a Laachmi courier stood very still, the way people stand when they have noticed they are being followed and are hoping it isn't true.

Somewhere in that lot, tinkers' gossip said, a spare Sump-Glass Lens was going to change hands today. Higgins couldn't be in three places, so he went where the day sent him first.

**queue_setup**
Foreman Crick had ninety-five boiler-shed hands queued behind the yellow line and a clatterlift that wasn't coming, which meant every one of them had to go through Higgins's grate on foot, in groups, dockets checked, before the klaxon.

'I've counted it four times,' Crick said, showing Higgins a slate covered in crossings-out. 'I keep getting a different number of poor souls left standing in the cold, and none of the numbers is zero.'

Higgins took the slate. Ten counts to unlock the gate mechanism properly, always. Groups of five through the docket desk, eight counts a group, no faster, because faster is how dockets get missed and missed dockets are how the wrong people end up on the wrong side of a fog vent. One hundred and fifty counts until the klaxon. It was, Higgins thought, quite a tidy little problem, for a Wednesday.

**wrought_setup**
Widow Anstruther had a writ appointment on the Rise side, a lawyer who charged by the quarter-hour, and absolutely no patience for a grate that thought it outranked her.

'There is an override valve,' she said, tapping the manual's cover with one gloved finger, as though she had written it herself. 'I have seen it used. My late husband had it used for him twice.' Which was true, and also, Higgins reflected, exactly the sort of true that got people killed eventually, because a valve used twice safely is not a valve proven safe, it is a valve that has been lucky twice.

The override cracked the seal ten counts early, venting the last of the built-up fog through a side stack instead of the main one, provided the residual pressure had already dropped low enough. If it hadn't, the side stack gave everyone nearby a very bad afternoon. Higgins had never once needed to make this particular call. He supposed a Wednesday had to be the first time for something.

**courier_setup**
The Laachmi courier by the culvert stile gave her name, reluctantly, as Ondine Rell, and her business, more reluctantly still, as carrying a sealed docket for the Turn's overseers that she would very much like to still be carrying in five minutes.

'There's a man been matching my pace since the Tallow Street corner,' she said, not looking round, in the disciplined way of someone who has been taught exactly how to watch a threat without watching it. 'Ordinary coat. Extraordinary interest in exactly what I'm holding.'

Higgins had seen the type before. Docket forgers didn't usually want your docket to keep. They wanted ten quiet minutes with it, a steady hand and a very good pen, and to hand you back something that looked identical and read completely differently three signatures down. It was the sort of small, patient theft that never made the broadsheets, and ruined exactly the sort of person nobody important went looking for.

**chain_check**
The equipment store behind the gatehouse held nine tally chains on a single hook, every one of them near identical, and exactly one of them calibrated to mark Gate Seven's counts in true tens up to a hundred and fifty. The other eight belonged to other gates entirely, with other rhythms, and would seal Gate Seven's mechanism a shade fast or a shade slow depending on which liar's chain you trusted.

Each chain's notches told a story if you were patient enough to read it: wear patterns from years of the same count, over and over, worn deeper at the tens than the ones and deeper still at the fifties, where a gate's cycle habitually pauses to check itself. Somebody with a proper eye for that sort of pattern could tell Gate Seven's true chain from the other eight without a single label.

**finale**
It was Higgins's own count that caught her, not the manual's: a Rustbound girl of perhaps twelve, standing dead still at the yellow line exactly as the klaxon sounded, a chemist's chit clutched in one fist gone white at the knuckle.

'Please,' she said, in the flat voice of somebody who has already been told no several times today and is saving her tears for later, on the grounds that they wouldn't help now. 'My mother's chest. The only draught that'll shift it is at the Rise apothecary, and the counter shuts the minute the fog clears, and the fog won't clear for a hundred and fifty counts, and by then—' She stopped, because the arithmetic finished the sentence for her, the way arithmetic does, without any interest in how it landed.

The Vent Order was quite clear. The lane was sealed. Nobody crosses for love, money or a superior's writ, and Higgins had built six years on that sentence being the whole of the job.

But Higgins had also spent the last several hours with his nose in Gate Seven's mechanism, and a man who has just relearned every joint of a thing sometimes remembers, entirely by accident, a fact that isn't strictly his to know. The old culvert stile past the courier's post, the one nobody used for foot traffic any more, had never been renumbered when the two lanes merged forty years back. On paper, in the Vent Order, in the only document that mattered, it was still Gate Seven and a Half, fed by a smaller secondary valve, venting on its own offset schedule roughly twenty-five counts behind the main stack. And the Vent Order, read exactly as written, forbade crossing THE LANE. It said nothing whatsoever about a lane it didn't know existed.

Higgins looked at the chit. He looked at the girl. He looked, for slightly longer than strictly necessary, at absolutely nothing at all, which was, for once, doing quite a lot of work.

'What's your name,' he said, already moving.

'Tansy Vail,' she said, half a step behind him and closing the gap fast.

'Any relation to the Instrument-Sergeant?'

'My great-uncle.'

'Convenient,' said Higgins, in a tone that was almost, but not quite, dry. 'Keep up. We've got about a hundred and twenty counts before the second valve fires, and I intend to use every one of them counting, not talking.'

They made the apothecary counter with eleven counts to spare, Tansy's chit stamped, the draught paid for out of a coin purse the apothecary clearly kept for exactly this sort of morning, and Higgins escorted her back through the same unlisted culvert before its own valve so much as thought about firing.

He did not mention it to anyone that evening. What he did do was open the logbook, in his own careful block capitals, and add a line that nobody had asked him to add and the manual did not require: SUMP GATE SEVEN AND A HALF, OFFSET SCHEDULE, UNLISTED. FOR THE NEXT MAN'S BENEFIT.

It was not a rebellion. Higgins would have been genuinely offended by the suggestion. It was, as far as he was concerned, simply a correction to an incomplete record, made by a man who happened to notice the record was incomplete, on a Wednesday when noticing had, for once, mattered rather a lot.
