# gf_fenn — "The Sixth Board"

**tagline**
Fenn Okonkwo came out sixth of eleven at the Calibration, no triumph and no disaster, and now has four days to work out in cold arithmetic what that is actually worth.

---

**intro**
Fenn Okonkwo had sat the Calibration four days ago, in a hall built for six hundred and lit like a courtroom, and had come out of it the way everyone came out of it: quiet, hungry and unable to remember a single sum he had actually written down.

Now it was posting day. Tallow Street was already three deep in children who had not slept properly in a week, all of them looking up at a lintel with eleven boards under it, waiting for men in Council grey to climb the ladders and pin up the lists.

Fenn's mother had wanted to come. Fenn had told her no, gently, because if it went badly he did not want an audience for the part where his face did whatever it was going to do, and if it went well he wanted to be the one who told her, in his own words, at his own kitchen table.

Sootfall was starting, fine and black, the way it always did around the fourth bell. Fenn turned his collar up and waited with the rest of the crowd for the ladders to come out.

---

**crowd_check**
The boards weren't posted by name. They were posted by docket number, in strict order, first stratum at the top of the leftmost board and running down and across, and six hundred children trying to read six hundred numbers off eleven boards at once made for a crush that had already put one boy on the ground twice.

Beside Fenn a small girl in a coat two sizes too big was reciting her own docket number under her breath like a prayer and getting no further with finding it than sheer volume was going to take her. She had the look of someone who had never had to count past a hundred in a hurry and was extremely unhappy to be starting now.

Somebody was going to have to work out where on eleven crowded boards a four-digit docket number actually sat, fast, before the crush got any worse. It might as well be the boy who was good at that sort of thing.

**help_found**
Fenn did the sum properly: dockets ran in blocks of sixty per board, so a number in the four thousands sat, near enough, six boards along and forty-odd rows down, and no amount of crowd noise changes long division. He got the girl to her own board inside a minute and found her stratum (ninth, and she went white, then oddly relieved, because ninth was not what she had feared).

'You did that fast,' she said, still catching her breath.

'Practice,' said Fenn, which was not quite true. What was true was that he had spent the walk over doing the same sum for himself, forty different ways, and none of the answers had changed anything.

The girl's older brother, hovering behind her with the specific gratitude of someone who has been made to feel useless in public, pressed something into Fenn's hand before being towed off. It was a slate, half rubbed clean, chalk-dust still ghosting the last sum somebody had worked on it. 'Go on,' the brother said. 'You'll want it more than I will today.'

**sixth_board**
Fenn found board six before he found himself on it, which was its own small mercy: enough time to get his breathing steady before his eyes landed on the number.

There it was. Not near the top, where the gilt frame caught what little gaslight the lintel offered and the crowd stood doing the particular hushed, dangerous smiling that success does to people. Not near the bottom either, where the eleventh board hung nailed flat and nobody stood near it long, because standing near it too long invited comment. Dead centre. Sixth of eleven, in a hand so ordinary the clerk had clearly stopped caring by row forty.

Around him the morning did what it always did on posting day: someone shrieked with joy three boards along, someone else sat down hard on the cobbles as if the news had gone straight through their knees, and a boy near the eleventh board was quietly, thoroughly sick against the wall while his friends pretended not to notice, which was the kindest thing available.

Fenn stood with his finger on the board a full ten seconds after he'd found the number, not because he needed to check it again, but because it seemed, for a moment, like a thing that deserved ten seconds. Sixth of eleven. Not the top. Not the bottom. The exact, unglamorous middle, and somewhere under the sootfall a voice in him that sounded uncomfortably like his own was already asking the only question that mattered: what was that actually worth, in real coin, over a real life, starting Monday?

He had four days before the placement offices closed their books for the season. He set off to find out.

**crossroads**
Four days, three real leads, and a mother who would ask him at supper whether he had a plan, in the tone of someone who already suspected the honest answer was not yet.

The Engine Yard on Founder's Row was taking a handful of sixth-to-eighth-stratum boys onto its millwright apprenticeship this season: slow, respectable, four years to a proper writ. The Signal Office by the clatterlift terminus had more applicants than posts and was settling the matter by lot, which paid at once if it paid at all. And the vent-warden crews down in the Under were short-handed again after last winter, training being short and the work being exactly as dangerous as everybody already knew.

Fenn had four days to find out what each of them was actually worth, in coin, before he could tell his mother anything true.

**engineyard_setup**

'Millwright's apprenticeship. Four years. First two, you're paid three marks a week and you're worth every penny less than that. Second two, once you're trusted near anything that turns, it's four marks a week. Come out the other end with your writ, and a qualified fitter earns nine marks a week for as long as his hands hold out, which round here is generally a good long while.'

She tapped the sheet. 'Everyone asks the same question at this point, so I'll ask it for you: is it worth it. That's not for me to say. That's arithmetic, and you'll want to do it properly before you sign anything.'

**finale**
That night Fenn laid it all out on the kitchen table for his mother the way Marta had taught him to, side by side instead of one at a time: the Engine Yard, slow and safe, four years to nine marks a week and a writ nobody could take off him afterwards. The vent-warden crews, faster money and a real hand on the household's needs from week one, and a foreman who paid Bosk to thin his queue because the work cost more than wages some winters.

'The vent yard pays quicker,' Fenn said at last. 'I worked that properly. It's not close, the first two years, it's genuinely not close.'

Fenn signed the Engine Yard's papers on the last morning the books were open, three marks a week starting Monday and a writ waiting for him four slow years off, and walked home past the Signal Office queue (still three deep, still hoping, still one in fourteen) without a flicker of regret he could locate. It was not the fast money. It was the sum he'd checked most carefully, and the one that stopped being an emergency and started being a life. His mother put the Counted Half-Hour on the mantel where the stopped watch used to sit, and it kept, from that day, extremely good time.

Some Calibrations end in gilt frames and some end nailed flat under the eleventh board, and most, like Fenn's, end somewhere in the ordinary, unglamorous middle, which turns out, if you reckon it properly and refuse to be ashamed of it, to be worth rather more than the boards ever bothered to post.
