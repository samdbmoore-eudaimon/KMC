# gf_higgins — "The Seven and a Half Rule"

**tagline**
Sump Gate Seven vents four times a day without exception. Corporal Higgins has stood it for six years and has never once had to decide anything, which is exactly how he likes it.

---

**intro**
Sump Gate Seven sat where Tallow Street stopped pretending the Rise and the Under were the same city. Oxblood brick above the seam, soot brick below, and between them a grate the size of a dining table bolted into the road like something that expected trouble and meant to be ready for it.

Higgins had stood this post for six years. In six years he had never once been late for the click.

The click came first, felt up through the boots, the Turn clearing its throat before it spoke. Then the seal, both grates down, nobody crossing for love, money or a superior's writ. Then the long wait while the fog got on with its scalding iron-eating business below. Then the reopening, and the docket check, and the next lawful soul across the seam.

It was written down. It had always been written down. Higgins had read the Vent Order so many times he could recite whole pages of it walking backwards in the dark, which he had in fact done once, for a bet he did not remember agreeing to, against a colleague he did not much like.

"Correct in every particular," his old training sergeant used to say, tapping the card, "and you will never have to decide anything for yourself."

Higgins had found that the most restful sentence in the language and had built six years on it.

This morning the boiler-shed queue was backed up past the second bollard, because the Rise-side clatterlift had thrown a cable in the night. The crowd did not know the seal was coming. Getting them behind the line before it did was, in its entirety, the job.

---

**count_check**
Ninety-odd men in a lane and a click already coming up through the cobbles.

Higgins did not shout. He had learned in his first month that shouting at a queue makes a queue, and that what moves men is a flat voice saying an ordinary thing as though it had already happened.

"Behind the line, please. Behind the line."

He said it the way the manual said to say it, once, and then again, and started walking at them at the pace of a man with nowhere else to be. It worked because in six years on this grate he had never once told that queue something that turned out not to be true, and a crowd can smell that on a man.

---

**whistle_found**
The lane cleared with a second to spare and the seal came down, and the grate housing gave a small metallic cough, and something rattled loose inside it and skittered to Higgins's boot.

A whistle. Brass, dented, cord long rotted away, with DOONE scratched into the barrel in a hand that had clearly been in a hurry about it forty years before Higgins was born.

Old Gatekeeper Doone had stood this exact post. Higgins had seen the name in the back of the log a dozen times without ever once wondering about the man.

He blew it, out of professional curiosity. It worked perfectly. He put it on his own cord and did not consider the matter any further, which was the correct amount of consideration for a Wednesday.

The Watchman's Whistle is yours. (+1 Speed, kept forever.)

---

**count_miss**
It came apart near the end, when a boiler-shed foreman tried to push one last apprentice over the line for luck.

Higgins put a hand flat on the man's chest, which was the whole of the argument, and the lane cleared two seconds late and considerably less politely than usual.

The seal came down anyway, because the seal did not care how it had felt. Whatever had been rattling loose in the housing stayed lost in there for another day.

---

**gate_fight** (scene intro)
Something in the sealed culvert did not want to stay sealed.

A lump of hardened slag and cooled fog about the size of a coal sack had wedged itself sideways in the outfall throat, and the pressure stacking up behind it had the whole grate humming a note Higgins did not care for.

The manual had a name for these. A clinker: grit and fog fused hard by the heat, and the trouble with a clinker is not that it fights back, it is that it does not do anything at all while the pressure behind it goes on building. Left alone it would either blow the seal early or take the grate out of the road, and neither of those was happening on Higgins's watch.

He got the bar off the wall and set his feet.

**The Grate Clinker — hitText**
The bar found the seam and a fist-sized piece sheared off with a sound like a kettle giving up an argument.

**The Grate Clinker — strikeText**
The bar slipped, the pressure found the gap, and a gout of scalding fog came up through the bars into somebody who was standing too close.

**The Grate Clinker — defeatText**
It cracked down the middle and slumped into ordinary slag, and the grate stopped humming that note. Higgins wrote his own note, in the log, in block capitals: CULVERT THROAT, CLEAR.

---

**gate_win**
The remains went into a bucket for the cinder cart and the grate went back to its usual entirely acceptable hum.

"Third one this month," said Higgins, to nobody, because there was nobody else on duty to say it to.

Third clinker, third grate, third small fault that had been sitting in that throat for God knew how long without anybody upstream noticing. He did not have a theory about it. Having theories was somebody else's grade.

He wrote it in the log, which was his grade, and which in his considered professional opinion is the only thing that makes a fault into a fact.

---

**gate_retreat**
The pressure got the better of the exchange, put a last spiteful gust up through the bars, and Higgins pulled everybody back behind the line to think about it again with slightly less exposed skin.

They went round by the footbridge instead, which cost the best part of an hour and a queue with strong opinions about queue-jumpers.

Behind them the grate went on humming its unhappy note and would go on humming it until somebody came back and finished the job.

---

**crossroads**
The morning's real trouble was entirely human and arrived in triplicate.

At the head of the boiler-shed queue, foreman Crick was working something out on a slate and not liking it. Further up the lane a Rise widow by the name of Anstruther was explaining at volume whose grate this was shortly going to stop being. And down by the old culvert stile a Laachmi courier stood very still, in the way people stand when they have noticed they are being followed and are hoping to be wrong.

Somewhere in that lot, according to the tinkers, a spare sump-glass lens was going to change hands today.

Higgins could not be in three places. He went where the day sent him first.

---

**queue_setup**
Crick had ninety-five boiler-shed hands behind the line and a clatterlift that was not coming, which meant every one of them had to come through Higgins's grate on foot, in groups, dockets checked, before the seal.

"I've had four goes at it," Crick said, showing him a slate covered in crossings-out. "However I do it there's a dozen of them left standing out here in the wet."

Higgins did not take the slate. He had never been quick with a slate and had stopped pretending about it around his second year.

What he had instead was the Vent Order, all four hundred pages of it, most of which nobody read.

---

**queue_good**
"Your dockets are wrong," said Higgins.

"My dockets are in order."

"Your dockets are in order and they're wrong. You've got ninety-five individual chits." He held one up. "Order says a shed foreman may present a single gang docket for a working party under one signature. Section nine. Nobody's used it since before I came on because nobody reads section nine."

Crick stared at him.

"One docket," said Higgins. "One check. Not ninety-five."

They came through in four gangs and the last man was over with room in hand.

Crick, in the sort of gratitude that embarrasses both parties, pushed something wrapped in oilcloth into Higgins's hand. "Doone's old kit. Cleared the shed store last spring and found it. Didn't know who else it'd fit."

Inside was a sump-glass lens, ground thick and clear, exactly the sort of thing a careful man keeps for looking into fog and seeing what is actually in it.

Component found: Sump-Glass Lens.

---

**queue_mid**
He got them through by doing it the ordinary way, five at a time at the docket desk, and he got most of them through.

The seal caught the last group half processed and the desk clerk had to shut the book on an undignified scramble, and eleven men went home in the wet to try again tomorrow.

"Close enough," said Crick, who did not mean it as a compliment and was not offering one.

The oilcloth parcel stayed in the boiler-shed store, unclaimed and unmentioned.

---

**wrought_setup**
Widow Anstruther had a writ appointment on the Rise side, a lawyer who charged by the quarter hour, and no patience whatever for a grate that thought it outranked her.

"There is an override valve," she said, tapping the manual's cover with one glove as though she had written it. "I have seen it used. My late husband had it used for him twice."

Which was true, and which was exactly the sort of true that gets people killed eventually, because a valve used twice safely is not a valve proven safe, it is a valve that has been lucky twice.

The override cracked the seal early and put the last of the fog out through a side stack instead of the main one, and it was safe if the pressure below had already dropped and appalling if it had not.

There was a listening tube for telling the difference. Higgins had never once had cause to use it.

---

**wrought_good**
He put his ear to the tube and listened for longer than she liked.

Low, and even, and steady. That was the note the manual called safe, in a single line, on page two hundred and eleven, and Higgins had read page two hundred and eleven perhaps forty times without ever expecting to need it.

He cracked the valve. The side stack took the last of it quietly. Widow Anstruther went through eight minutes ahead of her lawyer's clock and did not thank him, which he had not expected.

What she did do, going past, was put a small felt case into his hand. "He kept that by the valve. Man who made up rules for himself." She was already walking. "You'll know what to do with it."

A second sump-glass lens.

Component found: Sump-Glass Lens.

---

**wrought_mid**
The tube came back uneven, which the manual filed under wait, and Higgins said so, and was overruled by a woman who did not have the authority to overrule him and did it anyway by simply standing there until he moved.

He cracked it. The side stack gave back a short ugly cough that took the paint off the housing and the temper out of everybody.

She got through ten minutes later than promised and a great deal less gracious about it, having watched the whole business from a safe distance with a handkerchief over her nose.

Whatever her husband had kept by the valve stayed in her pocket. She did not offer it to a man who had just demonstrated he might drop it.

---

**courier_setup**
The Laachmi courier gave her name, reluctantly, as Ondine Rell, and her business more reluctantly still: a sealed docket for the Engine houses that she would very much like to still be carrying in five minutes.

"There's a man been matching me since the Tallow Street corner." She did not look round. She had been taught how to watch a thing without watching it. "Ordinary coat. Extraordinary interest in what I'm holding."

Higgins had seen the type. Docket forgers did not want your docket to keep. They wanted ten quiet minutes with it, a steady hand and a very good pen, and to hand you back something that looked identical and read differently three signatures down.

It was the sort of patient little theft that never reached a broadsheet and ruined exactly the sort of person nobody important went looking for.

---

**courier_fight** (scene intro)
The ordinary coat stopped being ordinary the moment it produced a knife, angled not at Ondine but at the satchel strap on her shoulder. Cut the strap, take the bag, be three streets gone before anybody worked out it was not a mugging.

"Fenno Grale," the man said, as though the name should mean something.

It meant one thing to Higgins, which was that it appeared three lines down a list of persons the Under watch would like a quiet word with, and that Higgins had read that list on Monday.

"Off duty, are we, Corporal?"

"Never off duty at my own gate," said Higgins, which was, strictly, in the manual.

**The Docket-Snatcher — hitText**
Higgins got a hand on the knife wrist and turned it out and away from the strap, and it stayed uncut.

**The Docket-Snatcher — strikeText**
Grale feinted low and put a boot into a shin and bought himself the half step of room he had wanted from the start.

**The Docket-Snatcher — defeatText**
Grale went down over his own feet as much as anything, let go of the knife, and put both hands up with the weary speed of a man who has been here before and knows how it finishes.

---

**courier_good**
Grale went into custody still complaining, mostly about the unfairness of finding a gate officer actually standing at his gate, which Higgins felt was rather the point of gates.

Ondine Rell checked the satchel twice, found the seal unbroken, and allowed herself one breath before the professional face came back down like a shutter.

"You'll want this for the trouble." She pressed a lens into his palm, thick glass in a plain brass ring. "The houses issue them to couriers on the fog routes. I've a spare. You work a fog route too."

Component found: Sump-Glass Lens.

---

**courier_mid**
Grale was quicker than he looked, went wide of the whole business, and was gone down a soot alley with the strap half severed and Ondine's shoulder badly wrenched from hanging onto it anyway.

The docket was sewn into her coat lining and not in the satchel, which Grale would find out with some irritation once he had emptied his prize of a spare pair of gloves and a cold meat sandwich.

She did not offer Higgins anything for the trouble. She had kept the only thing that mattered by not trusting the satchel in the first place, and she knew it, and so did he.

---

**converge1**
The three small fires of the morning were out one way or another, and Higgins logged all three in his block capitals, because a thing that is not written down, in his considered professional opinion, has not finished happening yet.

Filling in the log he noticed that a young Rise officer named Corvain had signed the seam-gate register twice already that month, asking about seal timings on a grate that was not, strictly, her patch.

Officers did not usually spend afternoons on other people's grates. Higgins wrote the dates down and made no further comment, on the grounds that noticing was his job and having opinions about it was somebody else's grade.

What concerned him more was the mechanism itself, which had been sticking on its return stroke since the clinker jammed it, and a sticking gate on Sump Seven on a day when everything else had already gone slightly sideways was not a coincidence he felt inclined to trust.

---

**spring_good**
He did not open the housing to look at it. He shut his eyes and listened to it.

Six years of that tick. He knew it the way a man knows his own stairs in the dark, and what he could hear was that the return was arriving a hair late and dragging on its way, which is a spring going soft and not a spring going wrong.

He backed the tension off by feel until the tick came back to the one he had in his head, and then left it alone, which is the harder half.

The old spring came out clean once the new setting took the load off it: a verge spring worn thin and not yet worn through, the sort of part a careful man keeps rather than scraps, on the grounds that something which has kept honest time for eleven years has earned a second job somewhere.

Component found: Verge Spring.

---

**spring_mid**
He went at it the other way, with the housing open and the dial in his fingers, and chased the tick round the dial in both directions for the better part of an hour.

He got there in the end. The gate ran right by evening.

But the old spring had been wrung well past its patience on the way, and came out in two pieces instead of one, fit for nothing but the scrap tin.

---

**chain_check**
The equipment store behind the gatehouse held nine tally chains on a single hook, near enough identical, and exactly one of them was set for Gate Seven.

The other eight belonged to other gates with other rhythms and would seal Seven a shade fast or a shade slow depending which of them you trusted.

None of them was labelled. Nobody had labelled anything in that store since Doone.

---

**chain_good**
Higgins did not compare them. He remembered.

Gate Seven's chain had been off that hook and back on it perhaps two hundred times in six years, and every one of those times he had been the man who put it back, and it hung fourth along, and it had a nick in the third link where somebody long before him had dropped it on stone.

He found the nick with his thumb without looking, which is the sort of thing six years on one post gives you and nothing else does.

He unhooked it, coiled it properly and pocketed it, and pinned a note to the hook for whichever colleague came looking, because a man who finds the right chain and does not say so has solved half a problem.

---

**chain_mid**
He took the sixth chain, which hung about where he thought and looked about right, and did not find out otherwise until the mechanism ran fast on the next cycle and sealed four seconds before the lane behind the line had actually cleared.

Nobody was hurt. Four seconds is not much to lose and it is not nothing either.

Higgins filed an incident note that was correct in every particular and considerably too late to matter to the people who had felt those four seconds happen to them.

---

**forge** (scene intro)
The gatehouse workshop belonged to old Instrument-Sergeant Vail, who had fitted more dials into more brass housings than any three younger men together, and who looked over what Higgins tipped onto the bench with the satisfaction of a man about to be usefully occupied.

"A gatekeeper's glass, is it." Vail turned the lens, the spring and the chain over one at a time. "Not built one of those since the Order was redrawn. Lens, spring, chain, all three, and I'll set you something that tells the truth on a bad morning."

**forge — forged**
The forge ran hot and steady while Vail set the sump-glass into a brass face, wound the verge spring into a housing ground smooth as a promise, and hung the whole thing off the tally chain so it could sit against a watchman's coat and be trusted at a glance.

It told him what he wanted to know exactly, immediately, and without a single opinion on the matter, which was more than could be said for anybody else at that gate.

**finale**
It was not the manual that caught her. It was Higgins.

A Rustbound girl of about twelve, standing dead still at the yellow line with a chemist's chit in one fist gone white at the knuckle, at the exact moment the klaxon went.

"Please." She said it flat, in the voice of somebody who has been told no several times already today and is keeping her crying for later on the grounds that it will not help now. "It's my mother's chest. The draught that shifts it's only at the Rise apothecary and the counter shuts when the fog clears, and the fog won't clear for an hour, and by then it's shut."

The Vent Order was quite clear. The lane was sealed. Nobody crosses for love, money or a superior's writ, and Higgins had built six years on that sentence being the whole of the job.

But Higgins had spent the last several hours with his hands and his ear in Gate Seven's mechanism, and a man who has just relearned every joint of a thing sometimes remembers something he was never really required to know.

The old culvert stile past the courier's post, the one nobody used on foot any more, had never been renumbered when the two lanes were merged forty years back. On paper, in the Order, in the only document that actually mattered, it was still Gate Seven and a Half, fed off a smaller secondary valve on its own schedule, well behind the main stack.

And the Order, read exactly as written, forbade the crossing of THE LANE.

It said nothing whatever about a lane it did not know existed.

Higgins looked at the chit. He looked at the girl. He looked, for slightly longer than was strictly necessary, at nothing at all, which was for once doing a great deal of work.

"Name," he said, already moving.

"Tansy Vail."

"Any relation to the Instrument-Sergeant?"

"Great-uncle."

"Convenient," said Higgins, in a tone that was almost dry. "Keep up, and don't talk to me on the way. I want to hear that valve before I hear you."

---

**end — goodText**
They made the apothecary counter with time in hand, the chit stamped, the draught paid for out of a purse the apothecary evidently kept for exactly this sort of morning, and Higgins had her back through the same unlisted culvert before its valve so much as thought about firing.

He did not mention it to anybody that evening.

What he did was open the log and add a line nobody had asked him for and the manual did not require, in his own careful block capitals.

SUMP GATE SEVEN AND A HALF. OFFSET SCHEDULE. UNLISTED. FOR THE NEXT MAN.

It was not a rebellion. Higgins would have been genuinely offended by the suggestion. It was a correction to an incomplete record, made by a man who had happened to notice the record was incomplete, on a Wednesday when noticing had for once mattered rather a lot.

**end — midText**
They made the counter with almost nothing to spare, both of them singed at the cuffs from a morning that had already asked more of the mechanism and of Higgins than a Wednesday generally does. The draught got bought. The chit got stamped. Nobody official ever found out quite how.

He added the line to the log regardless, later than he would have liked and less steady in the hand than usual.

SUMP GATE SEVEN AND A HALF. OFFSET SCHEDULE. UNLISTED. FOR THE NEXT MAN.

It had been a rough morning by any measure. He found, filing it away, that he minded rather less than he had expected to about the parts of it that had not gone by the book.
