# gf_jory — "The Wager of the Unmarked Chalk"

**tagline**
Jory Fenwick has never lost an argument he cared enough to finish.

---

**intro**
Jory Fenwick's proof came back from the Reckoning Hall with a single word chalked in the margin: FLAWED. Not WRONG, which he could have argued with. FLAWED, which meant somebody had read it all the way through.

He had proved, to his own satisfaction and his tutor's signature, that the sum of two odd numbers is always even. He had shown it for three and five. He had shown it for seven and nine. Both times the answer had come out even, obligingly, and Jory had considered the matter settled the way the Rise generally considered matters settled, which was to say without much further checking.

The clerk's desk at the foot of the Reckoning Hall stairs was not where he expected to find the person who had written FLAWED. It was smaller than his family's boot cupboard, and behind it sat a girl in a darned Under coat, copying figures into a ledger with the unhurried patience of someone who has never once been in a hurry on somebody else's behalf.

---

**dispute**
'You wrote this,' Jory said, laying the proof on her desk as though it might be contagious.

'I read it,' said the clerk, not looking up. 'Lark Hollis. I check the disputed marks, the ones the tutors sign without finishing.' She turned the page towards him and tapped two lines with a pencil worn down to a stub. 'Three and five. Seven and nine. That's two sums, Mr Fenwick, not a proof. A proof has to hold for every odd number there is, not just the two you were bothered enough to try.'

'It's obviously true for all of them.'

'Obviously isn't a word that appears anywhere on your statute,' said Lark, 'and it certainly isn't one I'm paid to accept from a Wrought boy who tried two examples and got tired.' She said it without heat, which somehow made it worse. 'Write it general, or the mark stands.'

**sc1_success**
'2n+1 plus 2m+1,' Jory said slowly, writing as he spoke, 'is 2n+2m+2, which is 2 times the whole quantity n+m+1. Whatever n and m are, that's two times a whole number. Which is even. Always.' He looked up, faintly startled by his own working.

Lark studied it, unhurried, and gave the smallest possible nod, the kind that costs nothing and means something anyway. 'That's a proof,' she said. 'Pity it took a clerk's desk to get one out of you.' She slid a cracked brass loupe across the desk, the kind the Hall used for checking smudged workings line by line. 'Keep it. You'll want to read your own writing more carefully than you have been.'

**finale**
The Slate Court filled early, mostly with people who had come to watch a Fenwick lose money in public, which was rarer entertainment than the Rise liked to admit. Lark Hollis stood at her slate in the same darned coat, chalk already in hand, and did not look nervous, which Jory found more unsettling than if she had.

The Steward read the statute, named the stakes aloud (twenty marks a side, pocket change to Aster Row and, Jory now understood, close to a term's rent to a Reckoning clerk), and set the question: prove, generally, that the product of any two consecutive odd numbers is one less than a perfect square.

Jory wrote fast and clean, using every general trick he'd bled for that week: odd numbers as 2n+1, full expansion, no shortcuts, no examples standing in for proof. It was, he knew as he wrote it, the best working he had ever produced in his life.

Lark's slate, when the Steward called time, was cleaner still: the same substitution, one fewer line, one elegant step he hadn't thought to take, arrived at with the unhurried certainty of someone who did this for a living because she had to, not because it was expected of her.

The Steward examined both slates for a long time, turned them side by side, and ruled that the flag stood: Jory's original proof had indeed been flawed, and Lark Hollis had been right to say so, then and now.

Jory paid the twenty marks without a word of complaint, which surprised the room and surprised him rather more. Outside the Slate Court, he found Lark counting the stake into a purse that clearly had further to travel than his ever had.
