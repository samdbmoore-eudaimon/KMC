# gf_jory — "The Wager of the Unmarked Chalk"

**tagline**
Jory Fenwick has never lost an argument he cared enough to finish. He has also never had one marked by somebody who read it all the way through.

---

**intro**
Jory Fenwick's proof came back from the Reckoning Hall with one word chalked in the margin.

FLAWED.

Not WRONG. Wrong he could have argued with. Wrong meant somebody had glanced at the answer. FLAWED meant somebody had gone through the whole thing line by line and found the place where he had stopped bothering.

He had shown it twice. It had come out right twice. He had signed it and his tutor had signed it and that had been the end of the matter, in the way things generally are the end of the matter on the Rise, which is to say without much further checking by anybody who might have minded.

The desk at the foot of the Reckoning Hall stairs was not where he expected to find the person who had written it. The desk was smaller than the boot cupboard at Aster Row. Behind it sat a girl of about nineteen in a darned Under coat, copying figures into a ledger with the unhurried patience of somebody who has never once been in a hurry on anybody else's behalf.

---

**dispute**
"You wrote this," said Jory, putting the sheet down on her desk as though it might be catching.

"I read it." She did not look up. "Lark Hollis. I check the disputed marks. The ones the tutors sign without finishing."

She turned the page round and tapped two lines with a pencil worn to a stub.

"You've shown it twice. Two cases, Mr Fenwick. That is not a proof of anything, that is two cases and a decision to stop."

"It's obviously true in general."

"Obviously." She said the word back to him without any particular emphasis, which was worse than if she had leaned on it. "Obviously is not in the statute anywhere. It is not in my instructions and it is not a thing I am paid to accept off a Wrought boy who tried two and got tired."

"You don't know that I got tired."

"No," Lark agreed. "I know you stopped. Getting tired was me guessing, and I'll withdraw it if you can tell me why else a man stops at two."

He could not, on the spot, and she went back to her ledger and let him not be able to.

---

**skillcheck1**
He reached for his pencil and was irritated to find his hand less steady than his opinion of himself.

She was not asking him to do the thing again. She was asking him to say why it had to hold every time, out loud, in general, standing up, at a clerk's desk nine inches wide, with no examples to hide behind and nobody in the room to be impressed.

He had never once in his life been asked to do that. He realised, with a small cold drop somewhere under the ribs, that he had been through two years of the finest teaching in Gifford and had never been asked to do that.

---

**sc1_success**
He got there, slowly, out loud, and he did it by talking about the *shape* of the thing instead of any particular instance of it, and about halfway through he heard his own voice change because he had stopped performing and started working.

When he finished he looked up, faintly startled at himself.

Lark studied it for a moment and gave the smallest possible nod, the kind that costs nothing and means something anyway.

"That's a proof," she said. "Pity it took a clerk's desk to get one out of you."

She slid a cracked brass loupe across the wood, the sort the Hall kept for reading smudged working line by line. "Keep it. You want to read your own writing rather more carefully than you have been."

**sc1_fail**

"The mark stands," said Lark, not unkindly, which was its own sort of insult. "Come back when you've done the working instead of the sulking."

**gate_fight** (scene intro)
Stung, Jory did the one thing a Fenwick can always do. He invoked statute.

Any disputed mark, pressed far enough, becomes a Contention: tried in public, before the Steward of Reckoning, two slates back to back so neither party can see the other's working. He filed it before he had finished being angry, which is generally when people file the worst decisions of their lives.

He also filed it, and he was careful about this and rather proud of the care, as a forfeit Contention and not a staked one. Nobody had put money on a Contention in forty years. His grandfather had opinions about people who did.

Statute wanted a qualifying viva first, set by the Steward's clerk of entries, to establish the challenger was not wasting the Court's chalk.

**The Clerk of Entries — hitText**
Jory's answer arrived whole, in order, and general, and the clerk's pen moved on without comment, which from a clerk of entries is very nearly applause.

**The Clerk of Entries — strikeText**
Jory's answer wandered, hedged, and got to roughly the right place by roughly the wrong road, and the clerk noted the wobble in a hand that would be read by the Steward later.

**The Clerk of Entries — defeatText**
She shut the entry book with a small final snap. "Qualified," she said, in the tone of a woman recording a fact rather than awarding a prize. "Whether that's good news is between you and your forfeit, Mr Fenwick."

**gate_win**
The docket was stamped and the date set for the following market day.

Jory came out of the Reckoning Hall feeling entirely in control of events for the first time that week, which is generally how these things begin to go wrong.

**gate_retreat**
She qualified him in the end anyway, on the grounds that turning away a Fenwick was more paperwork than letting one through, and she made sure he heard her say so.

The docket was stamped all the same. He left with the distinct sense of having scraped it rather than sailed it, and with the further sense that everybody behind that desk had watched him do it.

**crossroads**

**routeA_setup**
Master Quill arrived inside the hour, because that is what retainers are for, and unrolled what he called the family method with the pride of a man who has never had to justify it to anybody poorer than himself.

"Half these so-called proofs come apart the moment you work them properly instead of assuming. Take it in the order the statute wants it taken. Mind you *do* it. Don't assume it and then write down that you did it."

Which was, Jory noticed, the same thing the clerk had said, in a coat that cost eleven times as much.

**routeC_setup**
Fettle Grey worked out of a laundry doorway two streets below the cliff and dealt in things that were true and not necessarily kind.

"You want dirt on a Reckoning clerk," Fettle said, turning Jory's coin over twice, checking the mint mark rather than the amount. "Or you want to know whether she'll flinch before she has to prove anything. Those are different orders, Mr Fenwick, and only one of them is for sale here."

"Which one?"

"Neither, as it happens." Fettle's eyes had already begun measuring him back. "But I'll sell you something else if you turn out to be worth the risk of being seen with."

None of it was about proofs. It was about whether Jory would answer a straight question straight, and whether he could be got to say the family name when he was under any pressure at all, and how long it took him to notice he was being handled.

**lemma_good**

**second_seek**
Statute wanted one more thing before the Court would hear him: a second, somebody of standing to vouch that the challenge was made in good faith and not merely because a Fenwick could afford the docket.

Most of the Rise wanted no part of a Contention against a Reckoning clerk, on the grounds that clerks occasionally won and nobody wished to be seen having backed the other side.

Which left, to Jory's considerable irritation, Antony Vex, who was a year above him, immaculate about everything, and took the statute more seriously than the Steward did.

**second_check**
Vex read the docket twice and then looked at him over the top of it.

"You've filed it as a forfeit Contention."

"Yes."

"Good. My grandfather would have had something to say if you hadn't." He put the docket down. "Now tell me why I should stand up in the Slate Court next to a boy who has spent four days trying to find a way round a clerk instead of a way through a proof, because I have heard about the laundry doorway, Fenwick, everybody has heard about the laundry doorway."

The docket office shut at noon. Jory had perhaps four sentences left in which to make this worth Antony Vex's afternoon.

**second_success**
"The flag was fair," said Jory, before he could talk himself out of it.

Vex waited.

"She found a real hole. I'm not contesting that she was right, I'm contesting whether I can do better in front of the Steward than I did the first time, which is a different question and I want it asked in public because in private I'll lie to myself about the answer."

Vex considered him for a long moment with an expression Jory could not read at all.

"That," he said, "is the first interesting thing you have ever said." He signed. "I'll stand. Not because I like you. Because a Contention where the challenger concedes the flag is the only kind worth watching, and there hasn't been one in this building since before either of us came up."

**finale**
The Slate Court filled early, mostly with people who had come to watch a Fenwick lose in public, which was rarer entertainment than the Rise liked to admit.

Lark Hollis stood at her slate in the same darned coat with the chalk already in her hand and did not look nervous, which Jory found more unsettling than if she had.

The Steward read the statute, named the forfeit aloud, and set the proposition: one general claim, to be argued whole, no cases admitted.

Jory wrote fast and clean and did not once reach for an example. It was, he knew while he was doing it, the best thing he had ever produced.

Lark's slate, when time was called, was cleaner still. The same road, one line shorter, and one turn in the middle he had not thought to take, arrived at with the unhurried certainty of somebody who does this for a living because she has to rather than because it is expected of her.

The Steward looked at the two slates for a long time and turned them side by side and ruled that the flag stood. Jory's first proof had been flawed. Lark Hollis had been right to say so, then and now.

Jory took the forfeit standing up, which the room noticed.

**end — goodText**
The forfeit was that the flawed proof stayed on the public slate for a full term, in his own hand, with FLAWED left on it, and the corrected working underneath, also in his own hand.

He wrote it up that afternoon rather than make anybody ask him twice.

Outside the Court he found Lark on the step with her coat collar up.

"Where did you learn to write it that clean?"

"Reading other people's mistakes for a living," she said. "You see every way there is of getting it wrong. After a few years you notice there's usually one way of getting it right."

"You should be teaching."

Lark looked at him then, properly, for the first time all week, and something in her face closed over.

"I sat the Calibration eleven years ago," she said. "I came out ninth. There's no writ in this city for a ninth, so I check the marks of boys who came out first, and I'm good at it, and that's the whole of the arrangement." She pulled her collar up further. "You did better than I expected. That's worth having, so have it."

She went off down the hill and Jory stood on the step for a while.

He did not apologise for Quill, or the laundry doorway, or the family name, because that would have been a different story than the one he was in.

But he walked back up to Aster Row turning her proof over in his head instead of his own, and for a Fenwick that is very nearly an earthquake.
