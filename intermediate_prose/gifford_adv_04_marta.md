# gf_marta — "The Quarter-Day Reckoning"

**tagline**
Four marks have gone off the chalk, and a Rise debt-factor is already at the door with the ribbon untied. If Marta's book goes, the whole of Ferrow Lane has to borrow off somebody worse.

---

**intro**
The chalk had balanced every quarter-day for forty years, and then on a Tuesday it did not.

Marta found it by candlelight after the shutters went up. Four marks off the slate, in a column she had been over twice, which meant either she had lost her grip on her own counter or somebody on this street had been busy.

She did not believe the first. She rarely did.

Outside the outfall grate coughed its white into the gutter and Ferrow Lane held its breath the way it did four times a day, and the knock came up through the boards, and the whole business passed off exactly as it had passed off every day of her life.

Four marks is two days' bread for a family. Somewhere out there tonight somebody was going without it and did not know why.

She latched the book, put the candle out to save it, and sat in the dark thinking about who had been standing near that till on collection days.

---

**skillcheck1**
She did not add the column again. She had added it twice and adding a thing a third time is what people do instead of thinking.

What she did was go back through the last quarter in her head, name by name, the way you go through a drawer.

Forty years behind that counter. She knew what every household on Ferrow Lane bought and when they bought it and what they bought instead when there was no money. She knew who came in on a Friday and who sent a child. She knew which of them would take a short weight rather than say anything and which would have the shop down about her ears.

Somewhere in that, and not in the column, was a habit that had changed.

---

**skillcheck1_success**
Dill Corrigan.

Dill had come in on a Thursday for three weeks running, which Dill had never done in eleven years, because Dill was a Saturday man and had been since his wife died. And on all three Thursdays he had settled part of his chalk in coin, in the middle of a quarter, which nobody on this street does unless something has changed at home.

Nothing had changed at Dill's home. She would have heard. Ferrow Lane is not a place where things change quietly.

Marta sat back. That was not a theft. That was a man doing something he did not want discussed at a counter.

She got the brass rule out of the drawer under the till, the one her old master had left her, notched along its edge so that nobody, including her, could give short measure by accident. She weighed it in her hand and felt steadier for it.

---

**skillcheck1_fail**
Nothing came. Forty years of habits and not one of them out of place, which unsettled her considerably more than finding something would have.

It meant the fault was not in the column and not in anybody's routine either, and that left the goods themselves, or a hand she had not thought to doubt.

She rubbed the ache out from between her eyes, put the book away for the queue's sake, and told herself she would have it before quarter-day.

**afterskill**
The bell over the door coughed, a Rise fitting she had put in herself, the Under's own doorbells having gone for scrap two winters back.

The man who came in under it wore a coat the colour of unpaid rent and carried a folder tied with green ribbon, which in her experience was never good, ribbon being cheaper than honesty and rather more common.

Old Custom and Sons. Debt-factors of good repute on the Rise and none whatever on Ferrow Lane.

"Notice of inspection," he said, before she had finished with the woman in front of him. "Quarterly reckoning. We hear your book don't balance, Mistress Marta."

She knew what that meant and she knew it inside a second, because she had watched it done to a shop on the Wick four years ago.

They did not want four marks. They wanted the chalk. Every household on this lane ran on her slate, and if the slate was declared unsound, Old Custom took the debt and the terms with it, and the ones who would not have Old Custom's terms would go to the Ashlight instead, and the Ashlight's terms are worse in a way that does not show up for nine years.

Her book was the only thing standing between Ferrow Lane and either of them.

Fessup did not shout. Men like Fessup never need to.

He laid the folder on her counter, untied the ribbon with theatrical patience, and began reading clauses about unsound accounts and provisional receivership in a voice built for exactly that.

Marta had one quarter-day. Fessup intended to make it as expensive as possible before he left, and he had the whole of the Rise behind him, and he was standing in a shop nine feet deep.

**gate_victory**
He met every clause with a name and a date, and she had a great many more names than he had clauses.

"Quarter-day," he said again on his way out, as if repeating it made it more his own idea. "We'll be back."

He left without buying anything, which she counted as a second small victory, and she stood behind her counter for a while afterwards with both hands flat on it, because her hands had started going and she did not want the queue to see.

**gate_retreat**
She could not spare it. Three customers waiting, the fog due inside the hour, and a man in front of her who was being paid by the hour to stand there.

So she let him have the last clause and agreed to have the book ready on quarter-day.

Fessup looked satisfied with that, which told her more about his actual instructions than anything he had read out.

He left the ribbon's mark pressed into her counter like a bruise.

**crossroads**
Four marks does not lose itself. Somebody puts it somewhere.

Three places to look before quarter-day, and not time for all three.

Dill Corrigan, who was suddenly a Thursday man. The delivery register, which Ossa the carter filled in by lamplight and the eye of faith. And Perren, her sister's boy, who had worked that counter eight months and had lately taken to standing nearer the till than a boy with no interest in money has any business standing.

**routeA_setup**
Dill Corrigan wove cloth for half the Under and owed her for most of the thread in it, paid down slow on the terms she gave anybody steady enough to be trusted with them.

He came in looking like a man who had already lost an argument with himself and put his own tally slate on the counter, which was the single most useful thing any customer had done for her all month.

"Your book says I owe less than I do," he said.

Which was the opposite of what any man had ever said to her at that counter in forty years.

---

**routeA_good**
She got it out of him in about four minutes and it took her the rest of the evening to stop being angry about it, and the anger was not at Dill.

The Ochre family two doors up had gone four marks past what Marta would ordinarily carry, and Marta had carried it anyway and said nothing, and Dill had worked that out because Dill was not stupid. So Dill had been coming in on Thursdays, when the Ochres never came, and paying four marks off their chalk under his own name, and asking her to put it against his.

Except she had never put it against his, because he had never said, and he had never said because saying it out loud would have meant the Ochres knowing.

So the four marks were on her slate twice. Once as owed by the Ochres. Once as paid by a man who would not have his name on it.

"It's not missing," said Dill. "It's just in two places at once."

She corrected it in front of him. Then she wrote out a page, in her own hand, and made him sign it, and he did not want to and she made him anyway.

**routeA_mid**
He would not say. She could see there was something and he sat on it, and a man of fifty who has decided not to tell you a thing will not be got at across a counter with a queue building outside.

She corrected what she could see and apologised for the trouble, which he waved off, which was worse.

Whatever Dill Corrigan was doing on Thursdays stayed his business for another quarter.

**routeB_setup**
The carter was a broad silent woman called Ossa who never wrote a word she could avoid writing, and who filled in the delivery register each outfall-day off memory and a stub of chalk before handing it over for Marta to make sense of.

Six carts that quarter. Any one of them might have been miscounted at the weighbridge, over-counted at the door, or guessed at outright when the fog made the numbers on the sacks unreadable.

There was one weighbridge slip for each of the six, and the slips lived in a tin on the cart, and the tin had been open in the fog all quarter.

Whether any of them were still readable was nobody's decision to make.

---

**routeB_good**
Three of the six had gone to mush. The fourth came out of the tin stiff and stained and legible, and it was the one that mattered.

Ossa's tally for that delivery ran four sacks short of what had gone onto the shop floor, and four sacks had gone somewhere between the outfall grate and the storeroom door, and it was not Ossa.

Ossa swore for some time, none of it at Marta, and re-signed the register there and then with a great deal more care than the first time, and put her thumbprint on it as well without being asked.

**routeB_mid**
Five of the six had gone to mush and the sixth was for a delivery nobody had ever queried.

She went over the register with Ossa anyway, out of thoroughness, and found nothing, which told her only that the fault had not been careless enough to catch it today.

Ossa signed the register again regardless and went back out into the white looking faintly insulted on the horses' behalf.

**routeC_setup**
Perren had worked the counter on collection days for eight months. He was her sister's boy and he was sharp enough to make change without moving his lips, and he had until recently taken no interest whatever in money, which for a boy that sharp was itself worth noticing.

She had watched him stand near that till twice this month with no reason to be near it.

She also knew what he was saving for, because she knew his mother, and she knew his mother had not been able to pay the fee.

She sent the shop empty on a pretext, latched the door and asked him straight out.

---

**routeC_combat** (scene intro)
He denied it before she had finished the question, which told her most of it, and then went on denying it louder while she laid the book open between them and asked him quite calmly about specific days.

A boy telling the truth slows down when you press him. A boy telling a lie speeds up.

She was sixty years old and she had all night and she had raised two of her own, and she did not raise her voice once.

Marta puts a date in front of him and waits, and does not fill the silence, and watches him fill it instead.

**Perren — defeatText**

**routeC_good**
He cracked on the third date and not the first, which was almost admirable.

He had not been taking from the till. He had been rounding his own wages up when he totted it at close, a few grist here and there, on the theory that nobody checks a boy's figures as carefully as they check a customer's.

He was right about that, which was rather the problem.

And then, because the totals had started not to sit right, he had written in an extra purchase against a name to cover it. One line. In her book. In a hand doing its best to look like hers.

That was the line Fessup had found. That was the whole of the unsound accounts.

Marta did not shout. She made him write out every entry he had touched, in his own hand, at that counter, while she watched. She told him the money was coming out of his wages until it was square, and that she would be checking behind him every night for as long as it took her to want to stop.

Then she asked him what the fee was, and he told her, and she wrote it down and did not say anything else about it.

---

**routeC_mid**
He held the denial all the way to the door and out into the lane, and she let him go rather than have it on Ferrow Lane where every window would have it by morning.

She was not certain. She was suspicious, and suspicion is not proof, whatever the Rise thinks about people like her and people like him.

She would watch the till. That would have to do.

**route_converge**
Between Dill's four marks sitting in two places, whatever had walked off the cart and whatever Perren had or had not put in her book, Marta had something now that was worth more than a balanced column.

She had paper with other people's names on it.

That was the thing Old Custom and Sons could not answer and did not expect. A debt-factor's case rests on the book being the only record of the street. It is not. The street is the record, and the street can sign.

She still had the scale to settle, and the figure Fessup had quoted at her, but this was a start and it was a start with signatures behind it.

**comp2_setup**
The scale came next, because if they meant to argue her weights as well as her sums she wanted to know before they did.

Her brass weights had come with the shop and had been in daily use for forty years, and brass wears. A weight that has been picked up and put down a hundred thousand times is not the weight that was stamped on it, and a light weight in a grocer's shop is a hanging matter on the Rise and a cheat on Ferrow Lane, and she did not intend to be either.

There was a sealed test weight at the weighbridge on the seam. Getting at it meant asking the weighmaster's clerk a favour on the busiest morning of his quarter.

---

**comp2_good**
Coop was Laachmi and seven feet of him and had been on that counter nine years, and he heard her out and then went and got the sealed weight himself rather than send anybody.

Her half-mark weight was light. Not much. Enough.

Which meant she had been giving short measure to the whole of Ferrow Lane for something like two years without knowing it, and that was worse than the four marks, and she said so out loud, in the weighbridge office, and Coop had the decency to let her.

Then she had the set filed true against the seal, and the certificate stamped, and walked it home in her apron pocket.

She marked the query in her own margin, honestly, and left it standing.

It was not the cleanest choice she had made all week. It was not the worst either.

**comp3_setup**
One thing left: the figure Fessup had actually quoted. The arrears his whole argument hung off.

If she could not account for that exactly, then signed slates and stamped certificates would count for nothing on quarter-day, because he would simply read the number out again and let the number do the work.

She lit a second candle, which she resented on principle, and got out the fair copy.

The fair copy was in Rooke's hand. The Halloway girl had been doing Marta's book for two years, for four grist an hour and whatever was bruised, and she was thirteen and she had never once made an error Marta had been able to find.

Marta laid the two books side by side.

**comp3_skillcheck**
Two hands, two nights, two books, and somewhere between them a number a debt-factor had built a buyout on.

She did not add anything. She went down the two pages together with a finger on each, looking for the place where they stopped agreeing, which is a different job from arithmetic and a great deal more like reading.

The candle went down. The Turn knocked, and the lane went white outside the shutters, and she kept going.

**comp3_success**
There.

Three lines from the bottom of her own page there was an entry that was not on Rooke's page at all.

Not a wrong figure. An extra line. Somebody had written a purchase into her book after the fair copy had been taken, and the hand was doing its best to be hers and was not quite managing the R.

She sat and looked at it for a while.

Then she got Rooke round, at half past nine at night, and put both pages in front of her without saying which she thought was which, and watched a thirteen year old find it in under a minute and then say, "That's not your hand," and then say nothing else at all, because Rooke had already worked out whose it was and had decided that was Marta's business.

Marta had her initial the page. Two hands, one number, and the odd line out in the open where anybody could look at it.

**comp3_fail**
She went down the two pages four times and came out with a total she trusted well enough, close to Fessup's and not the same as it, and could not find where the difference lived.

So she copied out her own figure and wrote plainly underneath where it departed from his and that she could not yet say why.

Showing the working would have to serve where certainty would not. It was, she reflected, roughly the position most of Ferrow Lane occupied most of the time, and she was no stranger to making do.

Quarter-day was hours off. Marta laid out what she had on the counter by the last of the candle: a slate with Dill's name on it, a slip with Ossa's thumbprint, a stamped certificate off the weighbridge, and a fair copy with a thirteen year old's initials in the margin.

Four names. Three of them could not read the page they had signed and had signed it anyway because she asked.

That was not a set of accounts. That was a witnessed page, and a witnessed page is a thing a Rise clerk has to answer in front of people, which is precisely what Old Custom and Sons are paid to avoid.

**finale**
Fessup came back on quarter-day with the ribbon and all, and found a woman with a ledger open at the front rather than the back.

He read the signatures. He read them twice, looking for the seam he was used to finding in books like hers, and there was not one left to find, because the answer to his question was not a number, it was four people who would stand in a room and say so.

He retied the ribbon for the second time that month with the air of a man doing sums of his own about how much trouble a street this well kept was actually worth to his employers.

He left without the word receivership.

Outside, the Turn knocked and the lane went white on schedule, entirely indifferent to any of it, and the queue at Marta's counter reformed itself as though nothing had happened, which on Ferrow Lane is what happens after a thing very nearly does.

**end — goodText**
The chalk carried on exactly as it always had, which is to say precariously, honestly and entirely on Marta's own terms.

Old Custom and Sons did not come back that year. When they came back the winter after, they found the same woman with the same rule notched along its edge, a set of weights filed true against the seal, and rather less patience for ribbon than before.

Perren paid down what he owed out of his wages over five months and never once tried to argue about it, and in the fifth month she gave him the fee out of the till and told him if he ever mentioned it she would take it back.

And the Halloway girl went on doing the fair copy for four grist an hour, and Marta went on watching her do it, and thinking, more and more often, that the sums were not the remarkable part.

**end — midText**
The chalk got through quarter-day, which was the main thing, with a query still standing in the margin and a boy she was not certain about still working the counter on collection days.

Marta kept the page pinned inside the front cover where she could see it. Not as a trophy. As a reminder that a book only stays sound as long as somebody keeps checking it, and that she had not stopped in forty years and did not intend to start.

What she thought about, on the bad nights, was the shop on the Wick four years ago, and how it had gone in a fortnight, and how nobody on that street had had a page with four names on it.
