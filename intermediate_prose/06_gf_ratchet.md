# gf_ratchet — "The Ninth-Bell Run"

**tagline**
A sealed quittance, a debt of his own he can't shake, and the ninth bell closing in fast.

---

**intro**
The back room over the Anchor smelled of oil, spilled gin and paper going soft with damp. Silas Vane sat where he always sat, one boot up on the table among the strata sheets, reading something upside down with the ease of a man who had long since stopped needing to hide what he already knew.

'Ratchet,' he said, without looking up, which was somehow worse than if he had. 'Quick job. You're quick.'

He set a small wax-sealed chit on the table between them: a quittance, the kind that says a debt is settled and done, not owing, not shamed, finished. On the front, in a clerk's careful hand, one name: Etta Coombe.

'She scraped it together over eight months,' Vane said. 'Every mark of it, honest. I want it logged at the Sixmark counter before ninth bell. After ninth bell the Long Lane vent seals the counter's window till morning, and her name sits on tomorrow's strata list still marked owing, when it isn't. One more day of the street looking at that list and thinking what it thinks.'

He did look up then, and there was nothing kind in his face, and nothing cruel either. Just a man doing arithmetic with someone else's dignity, and knowing exactly what he was doing.

'You've got until the ninth bell vents Long Lane,' he said. 'That's the whole of the job. Don't be clever. Be quick.'

**briefing**
Ratchet pocketed the chit before Vane had finished the sentence, because that was the trick of the whole business: move first, feel it later, if you had time left over for feeling.

He knew what he was, working for a man who lent money nobody was ever really pressed to repay quickly, which was worse, not better, because it meant you never finished owing. Ratchet still owed nine marks himself, taken on at sixteen for a debt that had long since stopped being about the marks. He knew the shape of what he was carrying in his pocket, both directions at once: relief for Etta Coombe, and one more small, useful kindness that kept the whole rotten machine looking almost decent.

'Ninth bell,' Vane said again, to his back. 'Not a count after.'

Outside, Gifford's clocks were already arguing about how close to ninth bell they actually were, the way they always did. Ratchet didn't wait to hear who won.

**finale**
The counter window was still lit when Ratchet finally slapped the wax-sealed chit down in front of the Exchange clerk, breathing like a man who had personally argued with the whole of Sixmark Street and mostly won.

The clerk unfolded it, read the name, checked the seal against the ledger, and logged it in a hand as unhurried as if there were no such thing as a ninth bell anywhere in the world. Etta Coombe, quit and settled, struck off tomorrow's strata list before it had even been drawn up.

Somewhere out over Long Lane, right on cue, the outfall let go with a long scalding sigh, and the fog came up thick and orange under the gaslight, sealing the route Ratchet had just spent the whole evening racing. He watched it through the counter window, in no hurry now at all, and let himself feel, just for a moment, exactly what he'd been putting off feeling since the back room over the Anchor: that he'd done one decent thing today, on behalf of an organisation that mostly didn't deal in those, and that the two facts sat together in his chest without cancelling each other out, the way they always did, the way he'd stopped expecting them not to.
