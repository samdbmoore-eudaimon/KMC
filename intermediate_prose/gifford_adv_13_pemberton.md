# gf_pemberton — "The Transposed Row"

**tagline**
A tired hand swapped two digits on two dockets, and swapped two futures along with them. Pemberton has until the six o'clock bell to swap them back.

---

**intro**
Friday reconciliation. Pemberton liked Fridays the way a man likes the smaller of two available headaches. The boards came down off the wall, the carbons came up out of the drawers, and for two quiet hours nobody wanted anything from him except correct figures, which he could reliably provide.

Five years at the Tallow Street records office. Eleven marks a week, a chalk at three houses on the Wick, a folded periodical in his ledger he had stopped pretending he was going to finish, and a working life whose great consolation was that a column either added up or it did not.

Thoroughness was, as it happened, how he found it.

Board Nine, second column: SLACK, P. Twelve out of thirty.

Pemberton frowned at that the way you frown at a coat on the wrong peg. He had stamped that girl's paper himself three weeks back, and he remembered thinking, with the small private pleasure of a man who genuinely likes a clean piece of work, that whoever P. Slack was, she wrote like somebody who had never once in her life been allowed to be lazy about anything.

Twelve did not sound like that girl.

Twelve sounded like a mistake wearing her name.

---

**arithmetic_check**
The master tally ran to nine hundred rows in a clerk's hand that had gone ragged somewhere around row six hundred, which is the point in any triple shift where numbers stop meaning anything and start being shapes you are owed home for.

Pemberton laid two dockets side by side under the lamp.

SLACK, P., number 4179.

Two rows below it, DOSS, C., number 4197.

The same four digits. Different order, near the end. The kind of thing a wide-awake clerk catches in a blink and an exhausted one copies faithfully, twice, onto two separate boards, and never thinks of again.

Somebody careful had better check every digit against the carbon stubs before deciding this was worth the trouble it was about to become.

---

**slate_found**
Digit by digit, column by column, it held. Four one seven nine against four one nine seven, the seven and the nine sitting in each other's chairs like two men who swapped coats in a dark cloakroom and went home wearing the wrong one without either of them noticing.

Which meant Peony Slack was posted on Board Nine with Cutter Doss's marks against her name, and Cutter Doss was posted three boards up with hers.

Wedged behind the tally sheet, forgotten by whoever had last used that drawer, Pemberton found a nine-column slate with chalk dust worn into every groove. The sort of thing a truly careful predecessor keeps for exactly this.

It sat in his hand as though it had been waiting for somebody who would actually use it properly.

The Nine-Column Slate is his. (+1 Arithmetic, kept forever.)

---

**slate_missed**
The columns blurred rather than resolved. He went over the digits three times, got three faintly different readings, and had to admit quietly to an empty room that his own hand was not entirely steady, which is not a condition conducive to careful work.

He was almost certain it was a transposition.

Almost certain is not a phrase that survives contact with a Petition fee. He was going to have to be surer than this, quickly, and without the slate he would have liked to have found.

---

**gate_fight** (scene intro)
The Long Gallery hatch was manned that hour by Under-Clerk Vance, a thin correct man three years Pemberton's junior who had memorised the whole of the Procedures for the Handling of Calibration Returns and quoted it the way frightened people quote scripture.

"Boards are collated for the Bindery, Clerk Pemberton." Not unkindly, already reaching for the requisition ledger. "Nothing comes off that wall without a form eleven-C and a countersignature. I haven't got your countersignature. It's gone four. And I am not," his voice climbed half a register, "having today be the day I get this wrong."

He was not being difficult. He was being exactly as frightened of that office as Pemberton was, from the other side of the same hatch, which made him no easier to get past whatsoever.

**Under-Clerk Vance — hitText**
Pemberton gives him the docket numbers and the row references in order, plainly, and Vance's certainty wobbles a visible inch.

**Under-Clerk Vance — strikeText**
Vance recites a sub-clause at speed, word-perfect and entirely correct, and it is Pemberton's nerve that takes the hit rather than his argument.

**Under-Clerk Vance — defeatText**
Vance runs out of clauses before Pemberton runs out of answers, and stands back from the hatch with the stunned faintly grateful look of a man who has just been allowed to stop being right about the wrong thing.

---

**gate_win**
"You'll minute this," Vance said, already reaching for a fresh form, because some men make peace with a crisis by giving it paperwork. "Every step. If it goes wrong I want it written down whose idea it was."

"It'll be written down," said Pemberton, and meant it as comfort, and heard as it left his mouth exactly how little comfort it was.

He was through. Board Nine hung ahead of him under its brass frame with SLACK, P. still reading twelve, still wrong, still going down to the Bindery at six the same as everything else in that building.

---

**gate_retreat**
He backed off the hatch rather than argue a frightened colleague to a standstill in front of the whole Gallery queue, which did him some credit and cost him nine minutes he did not have.

He went round by the side stair instead, past the pigeonholes, past a Laachmi clerk three heads taller than him who did not look up from her filing, and came out on Board Nine's landing with dust on his cuffs and considerably less patience than he had started the afternoon with.

---

**crossroads**
Suspicion is not proof, and Pemberton had watched enough Petitions of Error die in committee to know the difference intimately.

He needed something that would still be true after somebody hostile had looked at it twice, which meant Peony Slack's actual marked script, recovered whole from wherever the archive had put it.

Three ways down to where the old scripts slept. The Archive proper, three floors under the Gallery, where nothing was ever thrown away and almost nothing was ever findable. The service stair, steep and dim, that the runners used to move dockets between floors at speed. Or straight down through the Bindery floor itself, past whoever was on shift starting the day's binding early.

---

**archive_setup**
The Archive smelled of dust and old glue, and the Archivist, a stooped Sirrath woman called Coll who had catalogued every scrap in that building for thirty years and trusted precisely none of it, made him sign for the key before she had even asked what he wanted.

"Slack, P. This season." She did not move. "Original script, not the carbon. That's a proper request, that is. Most people want the carbon, because a carbon's easier to lose quietly."

She found the box in under a minute, which told Pemberton everything about how little she trusted the rest of the building to manage the same.

---

**archive_good**
Coll unfolded her arms, which for Coll was roughly a standing ovation.

"Twenty-six out of thirty," she read aloud, tapping the scorched corner where ink had run in some old spill. "Same hand all the way through and no hesitation anywhere in it. That's the girl."

She let him take the scorched carbon of the script itself, corner burned, marks perfectly legible, and stamped his requisition without being asked twice.

"Don't lose that. It's the only version of the truth this building's going to believe."

Component found: the Scorched Carbon Copy.

---

**archive_mid**
He got there in the end and got there slowly, reading the same three rows over twice on the back of a requisition slip while Coll watched with the patient faintly disappointed expression of a woman who has seen a great many people arrive at the truth by the scenic route.

"You'll want to be quicker than that downstairs," she said, not cruelly, and handed him the scorched carbon anyway. "Go on. The bell won't wait for you to feel clever about it."

Component found: the Scorched Carbon Copy.

---

**stair_setup**
The runners' stair was steep, dim and built for people half Pemberton's age moving twice his speed, which described precisely nobody currently on it.

He could hear a boy's boots going down three floors below, fearless in the way only people who have not yet broken anything important can afford to be.

Speed was not Pemberton's strength. He knew that about himself the way you know your own handwriting: without pride, without much hope of it changing, and with the settled certainty that today was going to ask for it anyway.

---

**stair_good**
He took the loose flag at a dead run and somehow stayed upright, and arrived on the Archive landing with his heart going like a badly wound clock and his ledger held so tight against his chest it had left a mark through his shirt.

Coll had the box already half open, drawn by the noise.

"Slack, P.?"

He nodded, wheezing. She found the scorched script inside a minute and handed it across without commentary, the way you hand a drowning man a rope.

Component found: the Scorched Carbon Copy.

---

**stair_mid**
The loose flag did exactly what loose flags do. He went down it rather than over it, landed hard on the next step, and spent an ugly undignified minute establishing that nothing was broken except his composure.

He finished the descent at a limp, considerably slower and considerably more honest with himself about his own speed than he had been at the top.

Coll took one look at him, said nothing about it at all, and fetched the scorched script anyway.

Component found: the Scorched Carbon Copy.

---

**floor_setup**
The Bindery floor was heat and leather and the low iron smell of hot wax, and it was already busy: two binders working the day's first ledgers into their covers and, standing over them checking the seals as they went, Overseer Marsh, a broad weary woman who had run that floor for eleven years and had never once let a document leave it improperly bound.

She clocked him the second he came through the door, uninvited and unannounced, and read him correctly in about four seconds. A records clerk with a problem and no business standing where he was standing.

"This floor's for binding, not for browsing." She planted herself between him and the archive door beyond. "You want something off it, you go through the front like everybody else. Rules are rules, especially on a day when everything else is coming loose."

---

**floor_fight** (scene intro)
Marsh was not going to hit anybody and neither was Pemberton, whatever the tension in the room suggested.

What she was going to do was ask him, precisely and repeatedly, to justify every single thing about his presence on her floor, in the flat relentless manner of a woman who has spent eleven years being the only obstacle between chaos and a properly bound ledger.

"Authorisation. Purpose. Time of departure."

Each one landed like a closing door, and he had to answer every one of them correctly and fast or lose the argument entire.

**Overseer Marsh — hitText**
Pemberton answers clean and exact and in the right order, and Marsh's flat expression cracks very slightly toward something like respect.

**Overseer Marsh — strikeText**
A question lands that he has no ready answer for, and the silence Marsh leaves afterwards does considerably more damage than shouting would have.

**Overseer Marsh — defeatText**
Marsh runs out of questions before Pemberton runs out of answers, folds her arms, and steps deliberately one pace out of the doorway.

---

**floor_good**
"Archive's through there." She jerked a thumb at the door she had been blocking. "Coll'll have what you want."

And then, as he went past her: "Clerk Pemberton. If this is what I think it is, you had better be right, because I am not unsealing anything twice for the same mistake."

Coll had the scorched script waiting before he had finished explaining himself, having heard the whole exchange through the wall with the professional interest of a woman who missed nothing that happened anywhere near her boxes.

Component found: the Scorched Carbon Copy.

---

**floor_retreat**
Marsh did not move, did not shout and did not need to.

Pemberton retreated up the corridor with his dignity in worse condition than his prospects, and had to go round through the Archive's front counter after all, ten precious minutes poorer for the detour.

Coll took one look at his face and did not ask. She fetched the scorched script, because whatever had happened on the Bindery floor, the truth in the box had not changed.

Component found: the Scorched Carbon Copy.

---

**converge**
Whichever way he had come, Pemberton stood in the Gallery with the scorched carbon of Peony Slack's true script in his ledger, next to the folded periodical he had long since stopped pretending he would read.

Proof, at least, he had.

Proof was not enough. Correcting a collated board without proper standing invited exactly the sort of question he could least afford, and the boards went down to the Bindery at the six o'clock bell whether he was ready or not.

He needed a runner sent ahead to hold the binding on Board Nine and Board Three, and he needed it immediately.

---

**chit_good**
The boy caught the coin, caught the instruction, and was gone down the corridor at a speed Pemberton had not managed on his best day as a child, let alone this one.

He came back four minutes later with a scrawled chit from the Bindery clerk on duty holding both boards for exactly twenty minutes, no more, signed and countersigned.

It was real, it was legal, and it bought him time he had not had five minutes earlier.

Component found: the Runner's Chit.

---

**chit_bad**
The boy had already gone, off down some other corridor on some other errand, and the next runner Pemberton found was slower, less certain, and came back nine minutes later with an apologetic shrug and the news that the Bindery clerk wanted it in writing, properly, through channels.

Which was precisely the thing there was no time left for.

He was going to have to move a great deal faster on his own account now, with no chit and no minutes bought.

---

**stamp_good**
The stamp drawer held seven and only one of them was current, and Pemberton had never once in five years had cause to work out which.

It came out the way careful elimination does. The retired stamps all carried a strike-through date on the log page. The reissued ones carried last season's header mark. That left exactly one drawer, second from the left, whose code matched this season's ledger header.

He drew it out, checked it a third time out of pure habit, and found it uncompromised. Clean, current, entirely his to use.

The Uncompromised Stamp is his to carry. Component found: the Uncompromised Stamp.

---

**stamp_bad**
The codes swam. Two drawers looked equally plausible and Pemberton, sweating properly now, did not trust himself to choose between them with a Petition fee riding on the answer.

He would have to go on without a stamp he could be certain of, which meant that the amendment, if it happened at all, would happen the harder way: countersigned in person by whoever was overseeing the Bindery, on the strength of the proof alone.

---

**risk_good**
He arrived on the Bindery floor with a stitch in his side, dust on his knees from one bad step near the pipe gallery and, astonishingly, five whole minutes in hand, the wax cart still two boards away from Nine and Three.

He had never in his working life moved that fast for anything.

The small unfamiliar feeling sitting under his ribs alongside the stitch took him a moment to identify, and when he did identify it he was rather embarrassed about it, and it was something close to pride.

Bonus: +2 ⭐ at the end of the adventure.

---

**risk_bad**
His legs gave out on the second flight, not dramatically, just completely, and he came onto the Bindery floor two minutes later than he had hoped, wheezing, having lost his hat somewhere near the pipe gallery and most of his composure with it.

Two minutes is not nothing on a day measured in them.

He was going to have rather less room to argue than he had wanted.

---

**safe_route**
The Deputy Registrar's office was three corridors out of the way and the Deputy Registrar himself was, as ever, correct, unhurried and entirely unmoved by the phrase *the bell's about to go*.

He read the proof twice, asked one sharp question Pemberton was extremely grateful he could actually answer, and signed.

Pemberton walked down to the Bindery at a steady sensible pace with a countersignature in hand, arriving with rather less margin than he would have liked and rather more paper behind him than the running would have bought. Different risk, same clock.

---

**final_fight** (scene intro)
Bindery-Master Coyle stood over Board Nine with the wax pot already hot, watching the clock rather than the ledger, the way a man watches something he would rather not be responsible for.

He was not unkind. He was tired, it was six minutes to the bell, and a records clerk he barely knew was asking him to unpick two hours of careful work on the strength of a scorched piece of carbon.

"Everything you're telling me might be true." He did not move the wax pot an inch. "But I bind what's put in front of me, correctly, and I've bound thousands of these, and I am not stopping the whole floor on a hunch."

He was not wrong to be careful. That was, infuriatingly, rather the point of him.

**Bindery-Master Coyle — hitText**
Pemberton lays the proof out clean and exact and in the order it needs to be in, and Coyle's caution gives an inch, and then another.

**Bindery-Master Coyle — strikeText**
Coyle raises a fair procedural objection Pemberton has not quite prepared for, and the clock goes on moving while he finds an answer.

**Bindery-Master Coyle — defeatText**
Coyle looks at the last document, then at the clock, and sets the wax pot down with the air of a man who has just decided, against a great deal of professional caution, to be persuaded.

---

**final_win**
"Right," said Coyle, and it visibly cost him, a man giving up eleven years of careful habit in a single syllable. "Show me the stamp. Show me the chit. And God help the both of us if you're wrong."

He was not wrong.

Board Nine and Board Three sat waiting on the counter in front of him, six minutes from being unfixable, and entirely fixable still.

---

**final_retreat**
Coyle would not be moved on less than Pemberton had brought, and the argument ran down to nothing with the bell four minutes off.

Pemberton stepped back, breathing hard, and made himself think rather than plead, which had never once in his life been the wrong order to do those two things in.

Whatever he had left, proof or chit or stamp or whichever of the three he had actually managed to gather, was going to have to stand on its own, laid out plainly and fast, because there was no more time to win Coyle over by argument.

---

**forge** (scene intro)
Pemberton spread what he had across the Bindery counter under Coyle's eye: the scorched carbon of Peony's true script, the runner's chit holding the boards, the uncompromised stamp still warm from his own pocket.

Three things, each one proof of very little on its own, and together the whole shape of the truth.

"Well," said Coyle, looking at the row of them. "Get on with it. Bell's not getting any further off."

**forge — forged**
He worked faster than he had ever worked in his life and cleaner than he had any right to at that speed.

Struck through the wrong total on Board Three. Struck through the wrong total on Board Nine. Wrote the true figures in his own careful hand, and brought the stamp down on both corrections with the chit as his authority, one minute inside the borrowed twenty and four minutes ahead of the bell.

Coyle checked every line twice, found nothing wrong with any of it, and reached for the wax with something that was almost, cautiously, respect.

The stamp went back into Pemberton's pocket still warm, and stayed there.

The Quick-Set Stamp is his. (+1 Speed, kept forever.)

**forge — notforged**
Coyle looked at the gap on the counter where a third thing should have been, and did not soften.

"Two of three. I bind what's complete and correct and this isn't, not yet." He picked the wax pot back up. "Bell's going, Pemberton. Come back with the whole set, or come back next season."

**finale**
The bell went at six exactly, the same as it had every evening for as long as anybody in that building could remember, entirely indifferent to what had or had not been settled underneath it.

Coyle sealed Board Nine and Board Three into the bound ledger with SLACK, P. and DOSS, C. carrying the marks that actually belonged to them, and locked the Bindery for the night without another word about any of it.

Pemberton walked home the long way, for once, past the tannery district, where a girl he had never met and would probably never meet was at that moment entirely unaware that a thin damp anxious clerk had spent his whole afternoon being braver than he felt on her behalf, over four digits nobody else in the building had thought worth the trouble.

He did not think of it as bravery. He thought of it as the job, done properly, all the way to the bottom of the page, which was not a thing he had often had the chance to do.

He slept extremely well, and did not know yet that it would be a year before he had another afternoon like it, and that when the next one came it would arrive in the shape of a young officer in a grey coat asking him four polite questions at his own hatch, and that he would answer the fourth one badly.

---

**end — goodText**
By Monday the word had come back through the ordinary channels, which is to say slowly and without fuss. The Instrument-Makers' Guild had confirmed Peony Slack's apprenticeship on the strength of a third stratum return, properly bound and properly true.

Nobody at Tallow Street made anything of it. Pemberton preferred it that way and told himself so rather firmly, twice.

Cutter Doss was quietly moved to the stratum his own marks supported, and nobody explained to him exactly why, which Pemberton privately thought was its own small unkindness, if a gentler one than the alternative.

He ate his lunch alone that day, as usual, and got four pages further into the folded periodical in his ledger, which was three more than he had managed all year.

**end — midText**
The correction went through in the end, though it took a Petition of Error lodged the following week to finish what the Bindery floor had not quite settled outright, and a longer and more anxious wait than anybody involved would have chosen.

It came out true all the same. Peony Slack's marks were hers, properly, on the record, for good.

Pemberton spent the intervening week doing his ordinary work with his ordinary caution, said nothing to anybody about any of it, and found that the waiting suited him a great deal less well than the running had.

When the Petition finally cleared he allowed himself exactly one small private moment of satisfaction, standing alone at his counter, before going back to the next row of figures.

There was always a next row of figures. That, on the whole, was the part he liked.
