# gf_maude — "Five Parts Water"

**tagline**
The medicine came in short and the queue came in long, and somebody has to decide who that falls on.

---

**intro**
Sister Maude had run the house on Sallow Row for eleven years, which was long enough to have settled opinions about weather, water and the general untrustworthiness of anything shipped down from the Rise, in that order.

The fog had come down twice in the night instead of the usual once, which meant the Turn was working harder than it had any business working in early autumn, which meant the alleys had gone scalding and wet by turns for six hours straight.

By morning half of Sallow Row was coughing the particular wet rattling cough Maude had a private name for and never used in front of a patient, because nobody needs to hear their doctor making a joke about kettles.

The crate from the Rise apothecaries' guild arrived at eight, three hours late, and when she got the lid off with the flat of a bread knife it was plainly wrong. Forty vials of prepared feverbane tincture had been ordered and paid for. What was in there, packed in straw between two cracked flasks, was concentrate. Raw, unmixed, dangerous at full strength, and nowhere near enough of it.

Ivy Coles was on the second cot from the door, six years old, breathing like a door with a bad hinge.

Maude looked at her, and looked at the crate, and stopped being angry about it considerably sooner than she wanted to, because the anger was not going to be any use to the child and the morning was already going.

---

**dose_found**
The proof mark on the flask shoulder was faint, half scraped away by careless handling, and it was there, and it did not match what the manifest claimed.

The guild had sent last season's stock. Stronger than this season's standard mix and labelled as though it were not.

Caught now, that was a nuisance. Caught in twenty minutes, in a hurried dose for a six year old, it would have been an entirely different sort of morning and one Maude had had before.

Tucked in the straw where somebody had checked the true mark against the false one and evidently thought better of the whole consignment, she found a fine glass dropper, graduated along its side in a careful hand.

Its last owner had been a dispensary clerk who plainly cared about exactly this sort of mistake, and had no further use for it. Maude did.

Item found: The Steady Dropper.

---

**dose_miss**
The first test measure went in cloudier than it had any right to, and by the time she had worked out why, a good ounce of concentrate had gone down the drain instead of into anybody's chest.

Last season's stock, stronger than the label admitted, had very nearly caught her the way it would certainly catch somebody less careful.

She did not waste any time being cross with herself. There was nothing in the straw worth keeping and there was a room full of coughing people who did not care in the slightest whose fault the crate was.

---

**factor_fight** (scene intro)
Factor Grieve came at ten with a docket, for her signature on the delivery and the balance of the account.

He was a grey tired man who had signed a great many dockets in a great many wards that did not have enough of anything, and he did not enjoy this part of his work so much as endure it, which is how a good deal of the Rise's business gets done in the Under: politely, and at the poor end's expense.

"Forty vials, prepared, as invoiced." He tapped it. "Sign here and we're square."

"There are two flasks of raw concentrate in that crate and a great deal of straw," said Maude. "We are not square. We are considerably short, and I should like that written down before you leave rather than after."

**Factor Grieve — hitText**
Maude has the crate open on the table between them and goes through what is actually in it, item by item, out loud, until Grieve's pencil stops defending the invoice and starts correcting it.

**Factor Grieve — strikeText**
Grieve produces a second docket, older and messier, and for a moment its tangle of adjusted totals very nearly buries the plain fact that the crate is short. "These things average out over the season," he says, and it very nearly sounds reasonable.

**Factor Grieve — defeatText**
Grieve runs out of forms to produce. He looks at the crate, and then at Ivy Coles's cot, and writes SHORT DELIVERY, CONFIRMED across the docket in a hand rather less steady than the one he arrived with.

---

**factor_win**
He signed the correction without further argument, which was in its small grey way the most honest thing Factor Grieve had done all morning.

It would not put a single extra vial in her hands today. It would mean a proper credit against the next crate, and next time is a thing worth planning for even when this time is already going wrong.

"For what it's worth," Grieve said, not quite meeting her eye, "the guild board doesn't ask where the shortfalls land. Only that their own side comes out even."

"They land here," said Maude. "You may tell them that as well, if you like. I shan't hold my breath."

He left. She turned back to the crate and to a morning that still had to be got through whether the guild's paperwork balanced or not.

---

**factor_retreat**
Ivy coughed from the second cot, wet and long, and Maude decided the docket could wait and Grieve's conscience could keep.

She signed what he put in front of her without reading it twice, because arguing with a company man about a manifest while a child fights for breath ten feet away is an argument for a calmer day.

Grieve took the signature and went, faster than was strictly dignified, plainly relieved not to have to defend anybody's numbers after all.

No credit, then, and nothing admitted on paper. Only the crate, exactly as short as it had been an hour ago, and rather less of the day left to do anything clever about it.

---

**crossroads**
By noon the list ran to sixty-three names and the crate had not grown.

Maude set the pen down and thought about it with the flat unsentimental patience of a woman who had done this before and expected to do it again.

There were three places in Sallow Row that might have something she was short of, and time to try one.

---

**apoth_intro**
Drip-Row ran two streets over: a narrow dispensary kept by Widow Prudhoe, who bought up what broke elsewhere and sold it on for less than the Rise ever would, provided you could prove you deserved the discount.

"Everybody wants my cupboard today." She did not look up from her ledger. "I don't open it for a sad story, Sister. Never have. Give me a reason and I'll show you the shelf."

---

**apoth_good**
Maude did not give her a reason. She gave her eleven years.

Everything Prudhoe had ever let her have below price had gone into somebody on Sallow Row, and not one drop of it had ever been sold on, traded, or quietly found its way to the Ashlight's people at a markup, which was more than Prudhoe could say for two other houses in this district and both of them knew exactly which two.

Prudhoe listened to the whole of it without interrupting, which was rare, and then got up.

"Aye," she said. "That'll do. That's the only reason worth having and you're the only one who's ever brought it."

From under the counter came a true brass beam, salvaged whole out of a scale some careless apprentice had dropped years ago, and she set it down between them.

"Somebody'll need that more than my dust will."

Component found: A True Brass Beam.

---

**apoth_mid**
Maude led with the child, because the child was what was in her head, and Prudhoe heard about thirty seconds of it before her face closed like a shop at six.

"No. I said no sad stories and I meant it, and you know why I meant it, because half this street's got a sad story and I've one cupboard."

Maude got there in the end, by the longer road, and it took her a good deal more of the afternoon than the shorter one would have.

Prudhoe handed the brass beam over anyway, muttering that wasted breath was still better spent than wasted argument.

"Come at me properly next time, Sister. I shan't always be in the mood."

Component found: A True Brass Beam.

---

**cistern_intro**
Coop kept a still on the cistern stair and a reputation for stock that had fallen off the back of somewhere respectable. He dealt in flasks nobody official would admit existed, at prices that assumed you were desperate, which you generally were.

"Got a fresh case in." He thumbed a crate that had certainly not come through any guild docket. "Roads being what they are this time of year, a fair few of them arrive cracked and there's no telling which from outside. Open one blind and take what you get, or walk away with nothing. Your call, Sister."

---

**cistern_good**
The seal held. The flask inside was whole.

Better than whole: wedged in the same crate under a layer of old rag, Coop turned up a brass beam off some scale he claimed to have come by honestly, a claim Maude did not examine too closely, because a house that asks too many questions about the Ashlight's supply lines tends not to remain a house for long.

"Call it a courtesy," said Coop, which in his dialect meant it was not, quite, and nobody in that stairwell was going to say so.

Component found: A True Brass Beam.

---

**cistern_fail**
The flask came out cracked at the neck with its contents already sour in the straw.

Coop shrugged, entirely unbothered by another person's bad luck.

"Some days it doesn't run your way. No refunds on a blind case, Sister. You knew that going in."

She left with nothing to show for the visit but a wasted hour and a rather firmer opinion of Coop's business practices than she had arrived with.

---

**fen_intro**
Warrick Coles was still at the door when Maude got back, exactly where she had left him, wearing the particular grey look of a man who has been thinking about his own daughter for four hours and has not liked any of it.

"She's fifty-third on that list." He said it before Maude had her coat off. "Fifty-third, and you've medicine for forty if you're lucky. I've watched you work through other people's children all morning and I want to know where mine comes, and I want her moved while there's still time for it to matter."

---

**fen_fight** (scene intro)
It was not a fight, not really, though it had every shape of one: a man frightened past politeness, and a woman with exactly one fair way to run a room who intended to go on running it that way even now.

"I'm not moving her because you shouted loudest," said Maude. "I'll move her if she needs moving, same as anybody. Sit down and let me show you the chart, or stand there and waste what's left of the afternoon."

**Warrick Coles — hitText**
Maude puts Ivy's fever chart flat on the table beside the others and Coles's anger has nowhere left to stand once he can see it.

**Warrick Coles — strikeText**
"You don't know what it's like," he says, and for one bad moment the whole queue behind him takes up the same fear at once, loud enough that Maude nearly loses the thread of her own argument.

**Warrick Coles — defeatText**
Coles runs out of things to be angry at that are not the fog, the Turn or a guild three streets up, none of which are in the room to take it. He sits down hard on the step, all at once a great deal more tired than dangerous.

---

**fen_win**
"She's eleventh," Maude told him, and turned the chart round so he could see it himself. "Not fifty-third. Eleventh, on her fever and her breathing, same as I'd place anybody's child, and she moved there at eleven o'clock this morning when I looked at her chest, three hours before you started shouting at me about it.

"You'll have her seen inside the hour. And you'll have it because of what's on that chart and not because you frightened a queue, and I'd thank you to hold on to the difference the next time you're this frightened, because you will be."

Something in him gave way, quietly, the way it does once fright has somewhere useful to go.

He dug in his coat and pressed a brass fitting into her hand, a beam off his own mother's dispensary scales, kept back against harder days and rent he could not cover.

"Take it. You'll want it more than I'd get for it. Just get her seen."

Component found: A True Brass Beam.

---

**fen_retreat**
He would not sit, would not look at the chart and would not be talked down that afternoon, and Maude, who had spent a great deal of patience over eleven years on frightened parents, knew the point at which arguing further only burns daylight.

She let him empty himself out on the step instead of into the room.

He went eventually, no calmer and no better off for it, and Ivy stayed exactly where her chest had put her that morning, eleventh, whether her father had heard it or not.

Maude made a private note to look in on the Coles house before dark regardless. Some things you settle later and off the page.

---

**converge**
By the time she got back to the cots the list stood at sixty-three and showed no sign of stopping, and there was enough mixture in that room for forty of them.

This was the part nobody on the Rise ever had to do, and Maude had done it eleven times in eleven years and had never once got used to it.

There were two ways. She could make forty full doses at proper strength, treat the worst forty properly and give twenty-three people nothing at all, which is what the guild's own dosing sheet assumed and what every hospital above the seam did as a matter of course.

Or she could pool the lot and thin it, and give all sixty-three something weaker than the sheet allowed, and hope that weaker and in time beat full strength and too late.

The first was defensible. She could write it in a ledger and no inspector in Gifford would query a line of it.

The second was the one she believed in, and she could not prove it, and if it went wrong it would go wrong on her name.

She sat down with a very short pencil and chose the second.

---

**share_good**
Hollis, the ward monitor, was a stooped old Rustbound clerk who checked Maude's decisions the way some men check a bridge before they will walk on it, and he read the whole of it through twice before he said anything.

"You're going under the guild strength."

"I am."

"On sixty-three of them. Where any inspector could ask you why."

"Yes."

He put the sheet down and looked at her for a moment.

"Most of them do the forty," he said. "Every house I've clerked in for thirty years does the forty, because the forty's what you can defend at an inquiry, and the twenty-three don't come to inquiries." He got up and unlocked a drawer. "That's the whole trade, that is. Nobody teaches it and everybody learns it."

Out of the drawer came a small set of true grain-weights he had been keeping back for emergencies, and he allowed that this, finally, counted as one.

Component found: The Grain Counterweight.

---

**share_mid**
She got to the same place and got there slowly, and she went round the houses on the way, and twice she nearly talked herself into the forty because the forty was the one she could write down without her hand shaking.

Hollis watched her do it and did not help, which was deliberate and which she understood.

"You'd have got there," he said at the end, without much warmth. "You went the long way round a thing you already knew, and the long way round costs an hour, and you had one."

He kept his grain-weights locked in the drawer that round. Not out of meanness. Out of a clerk's plain refusal to reward the same afternoon twice.

---

**bottle_found**
Two flasks on the shelf, near enough identical, and only one of them was what the label claimed.

The wax told her, once she looked properly. One seal sat a shade paler than the manifest described with the dust undisturbed all round it, months of it. The other matched exactly and had plainly been handled inside the week.

Nothing mystical about that. The true bottle was the one somebody had actually been using.

Clearing the dead flask off the shelf to make room, she found behind it a small steady glass dish, precisely the shape a proper beam scale wants for weighing out grains. Somebody had shelved it years ago and forgotten it, the way useful things get forgotten right up to the day they are needed.

Component found: A Steady Glass Pan.

---

**bottle_miss**
In the end she went by the smell and the shape of the cork, which happened to be right.

She told herself firmly, on the way back down the ward, that happening to be right is not the same thing as knowing, and that she would not be trying that particular shortcut again on a day with less luck in it.

Nothing turned up behind the wrong bottle. Some shelves keep their secrets whether you guess correctly or not.

---

**forge** (scene intro)
Bramwell's workshop was over in Kettle Yard behind the dye works, cluttered with the innards of machines nobody else in the Under could still name, and Bramwell himself grinned at the sight of a true brass beam the way other men grin at good news.

"A scale, is it." He was already clearing bench space with one forearm. "Bring me the makings and I'll true it up proper. Grains don't lie, Sister. Not once the beam's honest. It's people that lie. Machines just get built wrong."

**forge — forged**
He set the true brass beam across its fulcrum, hung the steady glass pan off one arm and weighed it dead level against the grain counterweight on the other, adjusting with shavings of brass no thicker than a hair until the beam held perfectly still on its own.

"There. Honest to the grain. Weigh anything on that and you'll know exactly what you're giving, which is the whole point of the exercise when you think about it."

Maude took it in both hands. It felt, for the first time all day, like something she could actually stand behind.

**forge — notforged**
He worked with what she had brought him, which was not quite everything the job wanted, and said so plainly rather than pretend otherwise.

"It'll weigh true enough for government work." He tapped the beam so it settled a fraction off level and stayed there. "I'll not tell you it's honest to the last grain, because it isn't, and you'd find that out at the worst possible moment. Mind the difference and you'll be all right."

Maude took it anyway. An imperfect scale beats no scale, and she had never once in eleven years had the luxury of only using good tools.

---

**finale**
By evening the room had its rhythm back. Measured, given, name crossed off in a hand too tired to be neat about it.

Ivy Coles took her feverbane at the strength Maude had decided on rather than the strength the sheet allowed, slept through until morning without the rattle, and woke up asking, with the particular outrage of the very young, why nobody had brought her anything to eat.

Warrick Coles came back at dusk, sober-faced, and did not apologise, because Maude did not deal in apologies and had made that clear to him some years before this morning ever happened. He sat by his daughter's cot until the shift changed, which was the same thing said differently.

On the empty crate by the door, half hidden under straw nobody had bothered to clear, there was a small chalked mark that Maude recognised and did not mention to anybody.

The sort of mark that meant a certain person's money had quietly settled the guild's shortfall invoice, the same as it settled a good deal else on Sallow Row that everybody in the district knew about and nobody said aloud.

She scrubbed it off before the next delivery boy could see it and start thinking, and went back to the cots, because the work needed doing whoever, in the end, had paid for it. She had stopped pretending to herself, some years ago, that she was going to find a way to stop taking it.

---

**end — goodText**
By the week's turn the Kettle Cough had gone through Sallow Row without one name added to the churchyard's own ledger, which in a season like that one was not nothing.

Bramwell's beam proved so exact that the parish surveyor came round twice to borrow it for his own work, and Maude let him, on the understanding that it came straight back.

She held one whole flask's worth of concentrate back against the next crate's certain shortfall, because there would be a next crate, and there would be a next shortfall, and a woman who has done this often enough stops being surprised by either.

Nobody ever asked her whether she had dosed under guild strength. She had written it down anyway, in her own hand, in the ledger, where anybody who wanted to could find it.

**end — midText**
Most of Sallow Row came through it.

That is the honest way to say it, and Maude had no patience for any of the others. A handful of the later names spent two bad feverish nights waiting their turn, and one of them was seventy-one and had a hard week of it afterwards, and none of that stops being true because the churchyard stayed empty.

Everyone being fine had never been on offer. Not with a crate that short. What was on offer was that everybody on the list got something and got it in the order their chests put them in, and she had done that, and she had signed her name against it.

She sat down at the end of it with the ledger and a cold cup of tea and thought, not for the first time, that the worst part of the job is not the nights. It is that the choice she had made that afternoon was the right one and she could not have proved it to a single soul on the Rise.
