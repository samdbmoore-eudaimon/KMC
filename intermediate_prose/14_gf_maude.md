# gf_maude — "Five Parts Water"

**tagline**
The medicine came in short and the queue came in long, and someone has to make the numbers hold.

---

**intro**
Sister Maude had been running the infirmary on Sallow Row for eleven years, which was long enough to have opinions about weather, water and the general untrustworthiness of anything shipped in from the Rise, in that order.

The fog had come down twice in the night instead of the usual once, which meant the Turn was working harder than it should for early autumn, which meant the alleys had gone scalding and wet by turns for six hours straight. By morning half of Sallow Row was coughing the particular wet, rattling cough that Maude had a private name for and never used it in front of patients, because patients did not need to know their doctor thought of their illness as a joke about kettles.

The crate from the Rise apothecaries' guild arrived at eight, three hours late and, when she got the lid off with the flat of a bread knife, plainly wrong. Forty vials of prepared feverbane tincture had been ordered and paid for. What she found, packed in straw between two cracked flasks, was concentrate: raw, unmixed, dangerous at full strength and nowhere near enough of it.

Little Ivy Coles was already on the second cot from the door, six years old and breathing like a door with a bad hinge. Maude looked at her, looked at the crate, and did the sum in her head before she had properly finished being angry about it, because the sum mattered more than the anger and always would.

---

**dose_found**
The proof mark was faint, half-scraped by careless handling, but it was there, and it did not match the manifest's claim. The guild had sent last season's stronger stock, not this season's standard mix, a full third more potent than the label pretended.

Caught early, that was nothing. Caught late, in a hurried dose for a six-year-old, that would have been a different sort of morning entirely.

Tucked in the straw where the true mark had been checked against the false one, someone before her had left a fine glass dropper, graduated in careful grains along its side. Its last owner, a dispensary clerk who had clearly cared about exactly this kind of mistake, had no more use for it. Maude did.

Item found: The Steady Dropper.

---

**dose_miss**
The first test measure went in cloudier than it should have, and by the time Maude worked out why, a good ounce of concentrate had gone down the drain rather than into anyone's chest. Last season's stock, stronger than the label claimed, nearly caught her the way it would have caught someone less careful.

She wasted no time being cross with herself. There was no dropper in the straw worth keeping, and there was a ward full of coughing people who did not care whose fault the crate was.

---

**factor_fight** (scene intro)
Factor Grieve came at ten, docket in hand, to collect Maude's signature on delivery and the balance of the account. He was a grey, tired man who had clearly signed a great many dockets in a great many wards that did not have enough of anything, and he did not enjoy this part of his work so much as endure it, the way a lot of the Rise's business got done in the Under: politely, and at the poor end's expense.

'Forty vials, prepared, as invoiced,' he said, tapping the docket. 'Sign here and we're square.'

'There are two flasks of raw concentrate in that crate and a great deal of straw,' said Maude. 'We are not square. We are considerably short, and I'd like that written down before you leave, not after.'

**Factor Grieve — hitText**
Maude set the manifest weight against what the crate actually held and read the shortfall aloud, number by number, until Grieve's pencil stopped defending the invoice and started correcting it.

**Factor Grieve — strikeText**
Grieve produced a second docket, older and messier, and for a moment its tangle of adjusted totals nearly buried the plain fact that the crate was short. 'These things average out over the season,' he said, and it very nearly sounded reasonable.

**Factor Grieve — defeatText**
Grieve ran out of forms to produce. He looked at the crate, looked at Ivy Coles's cot, and wrote SHORT DELIVERY, CONFIRMED across the docket in a hand rather less steady than his first one.

---

**factor_win**
He signed the correction without further argument, which was, in its small grey way, the most honest thing Factor Grieve had done all morning. It would not put a single extra vial in Maude's hands today, but it would mean a proper credit against the next crate, and next time was a thing worth planning for even when this time was already going wrong.

'For what it's worth,' Grieve said, not quite meeting her eye, 'the guild board doesn't ask where the shortfalls land. Only that the numbers on their side come out even.'

'They usually land here,' said Maude. 'You may tell them that too, if you like. I shan't hold my breath.'

He left. She turned back to the crate, and to the numbers that still needed to work whether the guild's did or not.

---

**factor_retreat**
Ivy coughed, wet and long, from the second cot, and Maude decided the docket could wait and Grieve's conscience could keep. She signed what he put in front of her without reading it twice, because arguing arithmetic with a company man while a child fought for breath ten feet away was an argument for a calmer day.

Grieve took the signature and went, faster than was strictly dignified, plainly relieved not to have to defend the guild's numbers after all.

No credit, then, and no admission on paper. Only the crate, exactly as short as it had been an hour ago, and rather less time than there had been an hour ago to do anything clever about it.

**crossroads**
By noon the list had grown to sixty-three names and the crate had not grown at all. Maude set down her pen and considered, with the flat unsentimental patience of a woman who had done this particular sum before and would do it again: there were three places in Sallow Row that might have what she was short of, and no time to try all three.

**apoth_intro**
Drip-Row ran two streets over, a narrow dispensary kept by Widow Prudhoe, who salvaged what broke elsewhere and sold it on for less than the Rise ever would, provided you could prove you deserved the discount.

'Everybody wants my cupboard today,' she said, not looking up from her ledger. 'I don't give it away for a sad story, Sister. Never have. Show me the sum's right and I'll show you the shelf.'

**apoth_good**
Prudhoe checked the working twice against her own slate, found nothing to correct, and allowed herself something close to approval.

'Forty, and not a drop wasted arguing about it,' she said. 'That's a fair sum and a fair trade.' From beneath the counter she brought out a true brass beam, salvaged whole from a scale some careless apprentice had dropped years back, and set it on the counter between them. 'Somebody'll need this more than my dust does.'

**apoth_mid**
The first pass came out wrong, twenty patients short of the true answer, and Prudhoe corrected it aloud without a shred of gentleness, tapping each step with a chalk-white nail until the sum sat right. 'There,' she said. 'Forty. That's the actual number, whatever you had before.'

She handed the true brass beam over anyway, muttering something about wasted breath being better spent than wasted argument. 'Get it right next time, Sister. I shan't always be in a generous mood.'

**cistern_intro**
Coop kept a still on the cistern stair and a reputation for stock that had fallen off the back of somewhere respectable. He dealt in flasks nobody official would admit existed, at prices that assumed you were desperate, because you generally were.

'Got a fresh case in,' he said, thumbing a crate that had definitely not come through any guild docket. 'Carrier's manifest says one in six of these arrives cracked this time of year, roads being what they are. Open one blind and take your chances, or walk away with nothing. Your call, Sister.'

**cistern_good**
The seal held. The flask inside was whole, and better yet, wedged in the same crate under a layer of old rag, Coop turned up a brass beam off some scale he claimed to have 'come by honestly', a claim Maude did not examine too closely, because a clinic that asked too many questions about the Ashlight's supply lines tended not to stay a clinic for long.

'Call it a courtesy,' Coop said, which in his dialect meant it wasn't, quite, but nobody was going to say so.

**cistern_fail**
The flask came out cracked at the neck, its contents already sour on the straw, exactly as one in six of them apparently did. Coop shrugged, entirely unbothered by another man's bad luck. 'Some days the odds just don't run your way. No refunds on chance, Sister, you know that.'

She left with nothing to show for the visit but a wasted hour and a slightly firmer opinion of Coop's business practices.

**fen_intro**
Warrick Coles was still at the door when Maude came back, exactly where she'd left him, wearing the particular grey look of a man doing sums about his own daughter and not liking any of the answers.

'She's fifty-third on that list,' he said, before Maude had got her coat off. 'Fifty-third, and you've got medicine for forty if you're lucky. I've watched you work the numbers on other people's children all morning and I want to know where mine lands in them, and I want her moved up while there's still time to matter.'

---

**fen_fight** (scene intro)
It was not a fight, not really, though it had every shape of one: a man frightened past politeness, and a woman who had exactly one fair way to run a ward and intended to keep running it that way even now.

'I'm not moving her because you shouted loudest,' Maude said. 'I'll move her if the numbers say she needs moving, same as anybody. Sit down and let me show you the list, or stand there and waste both our time.'

**Warrick Coles — hitText**
Maude laid the true numbers out flat, Ivy's fever chart against the other fifty-two, and Coles's anger had nowhere left to stand once the figures were in front of him.

**Warrick Coles — strikeText**
'You don't know what it's like,' he said, and for one bad moment the whole queue behind him took up the same fear at once, loud enough that Maude nearly lost the thread of her own argument.

**Warrick Coles — defeatText**
Coles ran out of things to be angry at that weren't the fog, the Turn or the guild three streets up, none of which were in the room to take it. He sat down hard on the step, all at once looking a great deal more tired than dangerous.

---

**fen_win**
'She's eleventh,' Maude told him at last, turning the ledger round so he could read the working himself. 'Not first. Eleventh, by fever and breath alone, same as I'd rank anyone's child. You'll have her seen within the hour, and you'll have it because the numbers put her there, not because you shouted, and I'd thank you to remember the difference next time you're frightened.'

Something in him gave way, quietly, the way frightened men do once the fright has somewhere useful to go. He dug in his coat and pressed a brass fitting into her hand, a beam off his mother's own dispensary scales, kept against harder days and rent he couldn't cover.

'Take it,' he said, gruff. 'You'll need it more than I need what it'd fetch. Just get her seen.'

Component found: A True Brass Beam.

---

**fen_retreat**
He would not sit, would not hear the ledger, would not be talked down today, and Maude, who had buried enough patience arguing with frightened parents to know when arguing further only burned daylight, let him vent himself out on the step instead of on the numbers.

He left eventually, no calmer and no richer for the exchange, and Ivy stayed exactly where the fever chart put her, eleventh, whether her father had heard the number or not.

Maude made a private note to check on the Coles household again before nightfall, whatever the ledger said. Some sums you finish later, off the page.

---

**converge**
By the time Maude got back to the cots, the list had crept to sixty-three and showed no sign of stopping, and the mixture she had, true brass beam or none, still would not stretch to cover everyone on it by simple wishing.

She sat down with the ledger, the concentrate and a very short pencil, and did the fairest thing she knew how to do with numbers that would not be kind: she pooled the lot, and started dividing it evenly, because a smaller fair share beat a larger unfair one every single time she had tried the alternative.

**share_good**
The ward monitor, a stooped old Rustbound clerk called Hollis who checked her arithmetic the way some men check a bridge before they'll walk across it, went over the fraction twice and found nothing to fault.

'Five in seven,' he said. 'That's proper sharing, that. Not the sort of sum most Rise doctors bother learning.' He unlocked a drawer and set out a small set of true grain-weights he'd been keeping back 'for emergencies', and allowed that this, finally, counted as one.

Component found: The Grain Counterweight.

---

**share_mid**
Her instinct for fairness was sound; her first pass at the fraction was not, and Hollis corrected it without much patience, tapping the highest common factor out with one bony finger until five-sevenths sat right on the page.

'Fair's no good to anyone if the sum's wrong,' he said, and kept his grain-weights locked away this round, not from meanness, only from a clerk's plain refusal to reward a mistake twice in one afternoon.

**bottle_found**
The wax seals told the story, once she looked properly: one a shade paler than the manifest described, the dust undisturbed around it for months, while the other's seal matched exactly and had clearly been handled inside the week. Simple elimination, nothing mystical about it, the true bottle was the one that had actually been used.

Clearing the dead flask off the shelf to make room, she found behind it a small steady glass dish, precisely the shape a proper beam scale wanted for weighing out grains. Somebody had shelved it and forgotten it years ago, the way useful things get forgotten right up until the day they're needed.

Component found: A Steady Glass Pan.

**bottle_miss**
In the end she went by smell and the shape of the cork, which happened to be right, and told herself firmly that happening to be right was not the same as knowing, and that she would not be trying that particular shortcut again on a day with less luck in it.

No dish turned up behind the wrong bottle this time. Some shelves keep their secrets whether you guess correctly or not.

**forge** (scene intro)
Old Bramwell's workshop sat two doors down, cluttered with the innards of machines nobody else in Sallow Row could still name, and Bramwell himself grinning at the sight of a true brass beam the way other men grinned at good news.

'A scale, is it,' he said, already clearing bench space with one forearm. 'Bring me the makings and I'll true it up proper. Grains don't lie, Sister, not once the beam's honest. It's people that lie. Machines just get built wrong.'

**forge — forged**
He set the true brass beam across its fulcrum, hung the steady glass pan from one arm and weighed it dead level against the grain counterweight on the other, adjusting by shavings of brass no thicker than a hair until the beam held perfectly still on its own. 'There,' he said, satisfied. 'Honest to the grain. Weigh anything on that and you'll know exactly what you're giving, which is rather the whole point of the exercise, when you think about it.'

Maude took it in both hands. It felt, for the first time all day, like a number she could finally trust.

**forge — notforged**
He worked with what she'd brought him, which was not quite everything the job wanted, and said so plainly rather than pretend otherwise. 'It'll weigh true enough for government work,' he said, tapping the beam so it settled a fraction off level and stayed there, 'but I'll not tell you it's honest to the last grain, because it isn't, and you'd find out the hard way at the worst possible moment. Mind the difference and you'll be fine.'

Maude took it anyway. An imperfect scale beat no scale at all, and she had never once in eleven years had the luxury of only using perfect tools.

**finale**
By evening the ward had its rhythm back: dose measured, dose given, name crossed off the list in a hand too tired to be neat about it. Ivy Coles took her feverbane at the correct strength, slept through till morning without the rattle, and woke asking, with the particular outrage of the very young, why nobody had brought her anything to eat yet.

Warrick Coles came back at dusk, sober-faced, and did not apologise, because Maude did not deal in apologies and had made that plain to him some years before this morning ever happened. He simply sat by his daughter's cot until the shift changed, which was, from him, the same thing said differently.

On the empty crate by the door, half-hidden under packing straw nobody had bothered to clear, was a small chalked mark Maude recognised and did not mention to anyone: the sort of mark that meant a certain someone's money had quietly covered the guild's shortfall invoice, the same as it covered a great deal else in Sallow Row that everybody knew about and nobody said aloud. She scrubbed it off the crate before the next delivery boy could see it and think about it too hard, and went back to counting vials, because the sums still needed doing whoever, in the end, had paid for them.

---

**end — goodText**
By the week's turn the Kettle Cough had run its course through Sallow Row without a single name added to the churchyard's own ledger, which was not nothing, in a winter like this one. Bramwell's true beam proved so exact that the parish surveyor came by twice to borrow it for his own work, and Maude let him, on the understanding it came straight back. She banked one whole vial's worth of concentrate against the next crate's certain shortfall, because there would be a next crate, and there would be a next shortfall, and a woman who had done this sum enough times stopped being surprised by either.

**end — midText**
Most of Sallow Row pulled through, which mattered more than the arithmetic of it being tidy. A handful of the later names on the list spent two uncomfortable, feverish nights waiting their turn, and Maude did not pretend that was the same as everyone being fine, only that everyone being fine had never actually been on offer, not with a crate that short. She kept her promises to every name on the list, in order, and considered that, on balance, the better use of a short week than pretending the numbers had come out kinder than they had.
