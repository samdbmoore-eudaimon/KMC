# gf_colworth — "The Interest That Was Never Owed"

**tagline**
A widow's chalk debt has grown teeth over eleven unwitnessed years, and somebody patient had better read the statute all the way to the bottom.

---

**intro**
Magistrate Colworth's court sat in the back half of the Sump Bench, a low brick room in the Under with one window too grimed to let much through and a stove that argued with itself all winter.

On a good day it heard boundary squabbles, unpaid writs and the occasional dispute over a stolen barrow. On a bad day it heard the same, only slower.

Colworth found it tolerable, which for Colworth was the same as liking it. He worked the morning's stack the way a mill works grain: steadily, without curiosity, and without much caring what came out the far end provided it came out the right shape.

Two rent disputes, a chalk debt and a complaint about a goat. He was three dockets in and entirely unmoved by any of them when the chalk debt came round again with a Distress Writ pinned to its corner, and he stopped on it for exactly one second longer than habit allowed.

One second. That was where the whole business started.

---

**docket_check**
Strick versus Loom. An old grocer's tab against Sarn Loom, widow of a lamplighter, for a debt that had begun eleven years ago at three sovereigns and had since become something Strick's covering note described as compounded per the usual custom, five in the hundred, every year, as is proper.

*As is proper* was doing a great deal of work for four words.

Colworth had read ten thousand dockets and had learned the slow way that when a phrase works that hard, it is generally carrying something it would rather not carry in the open.

He did not reach for the gavel. He reached for the drawer where the clerk before him had kept a loupe, on the grounds that if a phrase intended to lie to him it could at least do so in large print.

---

**loupe_found**
It was still in there, wedged behind a box of nib cases nobody had thrown out in a decade, brass gone dull and glass still true.

Through it the covering note gave up its small print without complaint. A footnote, half a line, citing the Common Floor as the fallback rate for any tab not properly renewed.

Colworth did not yet know what the Common Floor was. He knew that somebody had put a footnote in about it, and that nobody in the room appeared keen for him to read it.

In his experience that was usually the entire case, sitting quietly in six-point type, waiting for somebody to be sufficiently tired not to notice it.

The Loupe of Small Print is yours. (+1 Logic, kept forever.)

---

**loupe_missed**
The small print stayed small, and his eye, unaided and forty years into the work, went past the footnote the way an eye does when nothing has told it to stop.

Something in the phrasing nagged at him anyway, the way a wrong note nags at a tuner who cannot place the string.

He put the drawer back as he had found it and made a private note, in the margin of his own patience, to read further down next time.

(The Loupe of Small Print stays lost, this time. Another run through this case may find it.)

**advocate_dispute** (scene intro)
The door opened before he had finished the footnote and Advocate Grieve came through it at the speed of a man who has practised looking unhurried and not quite managed it.

"Magistrate. I trust we're stamping the Loom writ today. My client has waited eleven years for his money and rather feels that's enough patience for one lifetime."

Colworth did not look up at once, which in a lesser man might have read as rudeness and in Colworth was simply the shape of a thought finishing.

"Your client's covering note cites the Common Floor," he said eventually. "I should like to know what that is before I stamp anything."

Grieve's smile did not falter so much as recalculate. "A formality, Magistrate. Standard practice. Nothing that need trouble the bench."

"Then it will not trouble me for long," said Colworth, and reached for his own copy of the Act.

**Advocate Grieve — hitText**
Colworth answers plainly and exactly, and Grieve's fluency takes a visible dent, like a well-oiled door meeting a step nobody had mentioned.

**Advocate Grieve — strikeText**
Grieve comes back with three precedents at once, fast and confident, and for a moment the whole room agrees with him purely because he said it first.

**Advocate Grieve — defeatText**
Grieve runs out of precedents some time before he runs out of confidence, which happens to advocates more often than they enjoy admitting, and settles for looking wounded, which costs him nothing and proves less.

---

**dispute_won**
"The writ is stayed," said Colworth, "pending a proper reading of the debt's history. One week."

Grieve opened his mouth, reviewed the last five minutes, and shut it again with the dignity of a man deciding this was not the hill.

"A week, Magistrate." He left at considerably less than his entering speed.

It was a small thing by the standards of the Sump Bench. A stay, not a ruling, granted on the strength of a footnote.

Colworth found, to his mild and entirely unwelcome surprise, that he wanted to know what the footnote said. He could not remember the last time a docket had made him want anything.

---

**dispute_retreat**
Grieve's precedents came thick enough that the stay Colworth carved out was smaller than he wanted. Three days, and a pointed reminder that the Bench's patience with delay was, in Grieve's professional opinion, not unlimited, Magistrate, whatever the footnotes say.

Three days was not much. It was more than none.

A magistrate who waits for ideal conditions, Colworth had learned a long time ago, never rules on anything at all.

**crossroads**
Three days or a week, the question did not change. Somewhere behind Strick's tidy compounding was what Sarn Loom actually owed, and somewhere behind that was the reason nobody had wanted a magistrate reading footnotes.

Three ways at it. Walk to Strick's shop and go through the tab himself. Send to the Roll Office for whatever original paperwork had survived eleven years of filing. Or have Strick's own clerk brought in and asked, plainly, to account for his own handwriting.

None of them appealed more than the others. Colworth did not, as a rule, have preferences. He took the one most likely to produce something that would hold.

---

**shop_route**
Strick's shop smelled of paraffin and old flour, and Strick himself was a heavy, not unkind man with chalk worked permanently into the creases of his knuckles, who watched a magistrate come through his door with the wariness shopkeepers keep for anybody carrying a ledger of their own.

"If this is the Loom account, I've done nothing but what's customary. Five in the hundred, every year, same as everyone charges."

"Custom is not the same word as lawful," said Colworth, "though I grant you they are often seen together. Show me the docket. The original, if you still have it."

---

**shop_good**
Strick got it out from under the counter and put it down, and then stood there while Colworth read it, and then went a colour Colworth associated with men remembering something they had hoped to have forgotten.

One signature. One date. Eleven years ago.

"There should be a renewal against every quarter," said Colworth. "There are none."

"I never renewed it." Strick's voice had gone very quiet. "Never occurred to me I had to. Nobody renews. Everyone does it this way."

"Everyone," said Colworth, "is not, on this evidence, doing it lawfully."

He wrapped the single sheet with the care he gave anything that might later be wanted in a hurry, and left Strick standing behind his own counter looking, for the first time in the conversation, rather smaller than his shop.

Component found: The First Signature.

**shop_mid**
Strick could not find it, or would not, and spent twenty minutes looking in places he had already looked.

Colworth waited him out, which is a skill, and eventually the sheet came up out of a drawer that had been searched twice already, which told him something about Strick and nothing he could put in a ruling.

He got the docket. What he did not get was the moment of Strick finding it himself, and whatever Strick might have said unprompted while he was holding it.

**archive_route**
The Roll Office kept eleven years of Sump Bench paperwork the way a magpie keeps shiny things: thoroughly, and with no system a sane person would recognise. Pigeonholes floor to ceiling in a room lit by whatever daylight the one high window could be talked into parting with.

The clerk on duty was a Laachmi woman two heads taller than Colworth even sitting down, who looked at his request with the weary patience of somebody who has heard *it should be under Loom, or possibly Strick, or possibly the year* a great many times.

"Somewhere in there." She waved at eleven years of boxes. "Mind the ninth shelf. It's held a grudge since the flood."

---

**archive_good**
The box was where a sensible system would have put it, which in the Roll Office counted as a minor miracle. Loom, under L, correct year, one sheet inside that nobody had touched since the day it went in.

He read it twice, from habit rather than doubt, and found what he had come for, which was an absence.

One signature at the top, one date beside it, and then eleven years of blank ruled lines below where the renewals were supposed to have gone. Forty-four of them, quarter by quarter, every one of them empty.

Nobody had ever failed to renew that tab. Nobody had ever tried to.

**archive_fail**
The ninth shelf lived up to its reputation and put a box of unrelated tannery disputes across the floor at the worst possible moment, and by the time that was gathered up and apologised for, the light through the high window had gone entirely.

The Loom docket surfaced the following morning, brought over by an apologetic runner who had plainly drawn the short straw. It was the right sheet.

It simply arrived a day later than it might have, and in a case already running on borrowed time a day is not nothing.

**chambers_route**
Clerk Sowerby kept Strick's books the way some men keep secrets: close, careful, and with a visible flinch whenever anybody asked to see them.

He arrived at the Sump Bench already sweating, sat where he was put, and answered the first three questions with the fluent unhelpfulness of a man who has rehearsed exactly three answers.

"I only keep the book, Magistrate. Mr Strick tells me the rate and I write it down. Five in the hundred, compounded, same as always. I don't ask about renewals. Not my place."

"It is entirely your place," said Colworth. "You are the one holding the pen."

---

**chambers_fight** (scene intro)
Sowerby dug in the way frightened men do, which is to say badly and at length, repeating *not my place* at increasing volume as though saying it louder might make it truer.

Colworth did not raise his voice. He never had. He simply went down the book's own pages, one quarter at a time, turning each one round so Sowerby could see it, and asked the same question about each: where is the signature.

Forty-four times. He was entirely prepared to ask it forty-four times.

**Clerk Sowerby — hitText**
Colworth turns another page round and asks the same question in the same tone, and *not my place* comes out a half-tone higher and rather less convinced of itself.

**Clerk Sowerby — strikeText**
Sowerby produces a fresh hurried excuse about missing pages, delivered fast enough that for a moment it nearly passes for an answer.

**Clerk Sowerby — defeatText**
Sowerby runs out of excuses well before Colworth runs out of pages, which was, by his own account afterwards, the single most frightening quarter of an hour of his working life.

---

**chambers_win**
"It's in the strongbox," Sowerby said at last, in the flat voice of a man who has decided that whatever happens next is less bad than another minute of this. "The original. Only ever signed the once. I told Mr Strick as much, years back. He said everyone does it this way."

"Everyone," said Colworth, not for the first time that week, "is not, on this evidence, doing it lawfully."

The strongbox, when it was fetched, held precisely what Sowerby had promised. One sheet. One signature. Eleven years old and entirely alone.

Component found: The First Signature.

---

**chambers_retreat**
Sowerby held to *not my place* with the stubborn endurance of a man defending the only ground he had, and after the better part of an hour Colworth concluded, with some regret, that a magistrate has other cases and cannot spend a morning on one clerk's nerves however deserved the spending might feel.

Sowerby went home a good deal more shaken than he arrived and, as it turned out, told his wife everything, who told the woman next door, who told the greengrocer, so that by supper half the Under knew roughly what the Sump Bench was looking into.

Information finds its own way through a city, Colworth reflected, whatever the strongbox says.

---

**converge**
Whichever road had produced it, he had the original in hand now and the shape of the thing was clear. Eleven years. One signature. Forty-four renewals that had never happened and that nobody had ever expected to happen.

That was the fact. What he still wanted was the law, set down properly enough that no advocate could talk it away.

For that he needed the Act itself. Not the summary. Everybody in Gifford quoted the summary at each other in place of having read the thing, including, he was obliged to admit, himself.

---

**ledger_good**
Strick's book, when it came, said everything by saying nothing.

There was a column for the rate and a column for the running total and no column at all for renewals, because the man who ruled that book forty years ago had not thought to put one in, and everybody since had simply used the book they had been given.

Not one hand in that ledger had ever been asked to record the one thing that made the rate lawful.

Colworth sat with that for a while. It was, he thought, an unusually pure example of how a thing goes wrong in this city: not by anybody deciding to do it, but by nobody ever being asked.

Strick handed the book over without argument, which was either honesty or exhaustion. Colworth took it either way.

Component found: Strick's Own Ledger.

---

**ledger_mid**
Strick argued about the book for half a morning, not because he had anything to hide in it but because it was his and it had been his father's, and men are strange about ledgers.

Colworth got it in the end and got what he needed out of it, which was the absence of a renewals column.

The getting had cost the morning rather more patience than he had budgeted, and he was aware, walking back, that he had been short with a man who had not deserved short.

**statute_success**
The Roll Office held three green copies of the Act and all three claimed to be the Act, and two of them were summaries that had quietly stopped saying so somewhere in the last forty years.

The catalogue marks gave it away. The full copy had been bound a year before either summary existed, which meant it could not be a copy of them and they could only be copies of it.

He opened it at the eleventh clause and read the actual words, rather than the tidy paraphrase the whole city had agreed to trust instead.

It said, considerably more plainly than he had expected, exactly what he had begun to suspect. A tab not renewed earns the Common Floor. The Common Floor is simple, and there are no further clauses attached, and there never had been.

Eleven years of custom, and the law had been sitting in a green book two streets away the entire time saying otherwise in plain English.

He took the page out along its original fold, carefully, on the grounds that a page people need to read is of more use loose than shelved.

Component found: The Uncut Eleventh Clause.

**statute_fail**
The three copies gave up their differences slowly and he traced two of them back to the wrong parent volume before the catalogue marks finally sat right.

By then the light had gone and the duty clerk was making the particular noises clerks make when they would like to lock up, so he copied the eleventh clause out by hand instead of taking the page, word for word, checked twice against the source.

It would serve. It cost him an evening he had not planned to spend and a hand cramped from careful copying, and he was sixty-five years old and felt every year of it on the walk home.

**public_reading** (scene intro)
The Sump Bench filled up faster than Colworth had ever seen it, word having gone round the Under the way word does, which is entirely and immediately.

He stood to read a ruling aloud for the first time in longer than he cared to think about, and found his voice considerably less steady than his reasoning.

---

**public_good**
He read the clause first, the actual words, slowly, twice, and then he put the covering note beside it and read the footnote, and then he explained to a room full of people who had come expecting either a formality or a scandal what the difference between those two pieces of paper had cost one widow over eleven years.

He did not soften any of it. He was not able to and did not try.

What he said, at the end, was that custom is not law, that it had been treated as law in this room for as long as he had sat in it, and that this was his own failing rather more than it was Strick's.

Sarn Loom, at the back, did not cheer. She sat very still, in the way people sit when a thing they had stopped hoping for turns out to be arriving anyway.

Near the door, Strick looked considerably relieved not to be owed as much as he had believed he was owed, which was a face Colworth had not expected to see on anybody that morning.

The ruling stood and landed with a good deal less weight than it might have, and several people afterwards asked Sarn Loom to explain what had actually happened, because the magistrate had plainly not managed it.

**quiet_filing**
He wrote the corrected writ out in his own hand, checked it against everything he had gathered, and sent it through the clerks in the ordinary way with no more ceremony than any other paper crossing his desk that week.

It was, he told himself, the more proper channel.

It was also, if he was honest with himself, which he tried to be about as often as anybody manages, a great deal easier than standing up in front of a full room and saying out loud which parts of the last eleven years had been his own fault.

**forge** (scene intro)
Instrument-Warden Coyle kept a bench behind the Roll Office where broken measuring devices went to be mended or honestly buried, and looked over the three things Colworth put on it, one old signature, one crooked ledger and one torn statute leaf, with the interest of somebody being asked to build a thing nobody has requested before.

"A reckoner," she said, turning them over. "One that holds the difference between what's claimed and what's lawful, so nobody has to go and dig up a green book every time. Give me the evening."

**forge — forged**
What she handed back the next morning was a small brass thing no bigger than a pocket watch, with two dials rather than one. The claim as made on the left face. The claim as the Act allows it on the right.

Anybody holding it could see at a glance how far a demand had wandered from the law, which meant nobody in that courtroom would ever again have to take four words like *as is proper* on trust because the bench was tired.

It would not make the next case less tedious. It would make the tedium considerably harder to argue with.

**finale**
The corrected writ went out under Colworth's own seal, at a figure Sarn Loom could actually meet without losing her rooms over it, with a note appended to Strick's shop licence reminding him in the driest language the Act allowed that custom is not law and never has been, however comfortably the two sit together when nobody checks.

In the grand ledger of the Sump Bench it was a small case. Three sovereigns, some interest, a widow, a grocer and a clerk who would think rather harder about strongboxes from now on. Nobody outside the Under would ever hear of it.

He filed it himself, in the ordinary manner, and found somewhat to his own surprise that he minded doing so rather less than usual.

What stayed with him was not the ruling. It was that the answer had been in a green book two streets away for forty years, in plain English, and that the only thing standing between it and the people it concerned had been that nobody had been sufficiently awake to read to the bottom of the page.

He would hear larger cases before he was done. Cases with a great deal more at stake than a widow's rooms, where a whole room would be waiting for him to accept four words on trust because everybody always had.

He rather thought he might not.

For now he set the Loom file on the shelf where closed files went and reached, without any particular feeling either way, for the next docket in the stack. It was, he noted, considerably more legible than the last. Small mercies. He had learned to take them where the stack allowed.

---

**end — goodText**
The clerks spoke of the Loom ruling for months, in the careful approving way clerks speak of paperwork that finally came out right.

Sarn Loom kept her rooms, paid down what she actually owed at a rate that left her enough for coal, and sent Colworth, entirely against custom, a small parcel of dried lavender with no note attached, which he kept in his desk drawer and never once explained to anybody who asked.

Strick amended his practice, grudgingly at first and then rather more thoroughly than the law required, on the grounds that a second visit from the Sump Bench was not an experience he cared to repeat.

The Uncut Eleventh Clause went back to the Roll Office properly catalogued under its own listing, so that the next magistrate who wanted it would not have to work out which of three green books was lying.

Colworth would never have called any of it satisfying. But he read his dockets to the bottom after that, footnotes and all, and something in the reading had very quietly stopped being nothing at all.

**end — midText**
The ruling held and Sarn Loom kept her rooms, and the case left the Sump Bench a good deal more tired than it needed to, and Colworth privately suspected he had made harder work of it than it deserved.

Strick paid without further argument. Sowerby went back to his books considerably more nervous about strongboxes. The Uncut Eleventh Clause was filed away, correctly if late.

It was not the clean quick resolution Colworth privately preferred, on the rare occasions he preferred anything. It was, nonetheless, the right one, arrived at the long way round, which he supposed, turning to the next docket in the stack, was still arriving.

What he did not say to anybody, then or afterwards, was that in eleven years nobody had asked the question, and that for nine of those eleven years the man who had not asked it had been him.
