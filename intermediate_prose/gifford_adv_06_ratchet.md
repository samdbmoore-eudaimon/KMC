# gf_ratchet — "The Ninth-Bell Run"

**tagline**
A sealed quittance, a debt of his own he cannot shake, and the ninth bell coming in fast.

---

**intro**
The back room over the Ashlight smelled of oil, spilled gin and paper going soft with damp. Silas Vane sat where he always sat with one boot up on the table among his own ledgers, reading something upside down with the ease of a man who long ago stopped needing to hide what he already knew.

"Ratchet." He did not look up, which was worse than if he had. "Quick job. You're quick."

He put a small wax-sealed chit on the table. A quittance: the kind that says a debt is done, not owing, not shamed, finished. One name on the front in a clerk's careful hand.

Etta Coombe.

"Eight months she's been scraping that together. Every grist of it honest." Vane turned a page. "I want it logged at the Sixmark counter before ninth bell. After ninth bell the Long Lane outfall seals that window till morning, and her name goes onto tomorrow's owing list still marked, when it isn't. One more day of that street reading that list and thinking what it thinks."

He looked up then. There was nothing kind in his face and nothing cruel either. Just a man who understood exactly what a name on a list does to a person, and who had priced it, and who was choosing to spend a runner on it.

"Ninth bell vents Long Lane," he said. "That's the job. Don't be clever. Be quick."

---

**briefing**
Ratchet had the chit in his pocket before the sentence was finished, because that was the trick of the whole business. Move first. Feel it later, if there turned out to be time.

He knew what he was. He worked for a man who lent money to people nobody would lend money to and then never pressed them, which is worse and not better, because it means you never finish owing. Ratchet still owed nine marks himself, taken on at sixteen, and Vane had not mentioned it once in four years, and Ratchet thought about it roughly nine times a day.

He knew what he had in his pocket, both ways at once. Relief for a woman he had never met. And one more small useful kindness that kept the whole rotten arrangement looking almost decent.

Outside, Gifford's clocks were already arguing about how close to ninth bell it actually was, the way they always did.

He did not wait to hear who won.

---

**skillcheck1**
The Ashlight's side door let onto Gullet Row, where the Boundary Watch changed shift on a schedule that was supposed to be secret and had been an open secret for years.

Get it right and the Row was empty for about the length of a held breath. Get it wrong and it was full of men with docket books and nowhere to be for twenty minutes, which for Ratchet was considerably worse than any vent.

He stood in the doorway and listened.

That was all it took, if you had done it enough. The old shift came off talking. The new shift came on quiet, because nobody talks at the start of a night watch. What you waited for was the gap in between where nobody was saying anything at all, and it did not last long, and no clock in Gifford would have told you when it was.

**watch_found**
He went through the gap before the Watch had finished changing, and nobody ever knew, which is the only kind of victory a runner gets.

On the step outside the side door, dropped by some previous runner in a hurry that had evidently not ended well, lay a second-hand watch on a snapped chain. Face cracked. Hands still going.

Ratchet turned it over once, decided against wondering too hard whose it had been, and put it in his coat.

The Second-Hand Watch is yours. (+1 Speed, kept forever.)

**watch_missed**
He went a beat early and had to fold himself into a doorway while the tail of the old shift came past close enough for him to smell the tallow on their coats.

Nobody saw him. That was the whole of the good news.

Whatever had been on that step was gone by the time he could move again, and there was no time now to go back for something that had never been his.

**warden_gate** (scene intro)
The footbridge at Cutter's Seam was the only quick way up onto the Rise side of Long Lane, and one of Ashcombe's boundary wardens was standing square in the middle of it with his docket book open, going through everybody's papers with the slow thoroughness of a man three hours from the end of a long shift.

He was not cruel about it. He was worse than cruel. He was fair, and he was going to read Ratchet's papers exactly as carefully as he read anybody's, and Ratchet's papers were good enough to survive a glance and nowhere near good enough to survive being read.

"Evening," said the warden, not unfriendly, and held out a hand for a docket that did not exist.

**The Boundary Warden — hitText**
Ratchet answered fast and easy, in the voice of a man with nothing in his pockets worth asking about, and the warden's attention slid half a step past him, which was all the room he needed.

**The Boundary Warden — strikeText**
The warden's eyes came up off the page onto Ratchet's face and stayed there, and Ratchet had to talk considerably faster than he liked while the whole crossing tipped against him.

**The Boundary Warden — defeatText**
The warden sighed, stamped a blank corner of his own book out of pure procedural habit, and waved him through with the air of a man deciding this was not worth the last three hours of his shift.

---

**gate_win**
Ratchet came off the far end at a fast walk that badly wanted to be a run, and kept it a walk, because a man running on a bridge is a man worth following.

Long Lane opened ahead of him, busy and ordinary and entirely unaware that its own outfall was about to make a liar of the whole evening.

Behind him the warden had gone back to his book and had forgotten Ratchet's face before Ratchet had finished forgetting his, which was the arrangement and suited both of them.

---

**gate_retreat**
The warden was not having it.

Ratchet backed off the bridge with an apology that cost him nothing and left behind an entry in a docket book that would cost somebody else a headache in a fortnight, and went down onto the towpath instead.

Five minutes and a wet boot, on an evening with no five minutes going spare.

He came up the towpath steps onto Long Lane breathing hard, having learned nothing except that Ashcombe trains his wardens properly, which was infuriatingly to the man's credit.

---

**crossroads**
Long Lane went three ways before it reached the Sixmark Exchange, and down all three of them the outfall was already clearing its throat.

Ratchet stood at the fork and did the thing he hated, which was choosing fastest instead of safest, because fastest was the only thing that mattered before ninth bell and safest was a luxury for people who were not carrying somebody else's name in their pocket.

The clatterlift cable ran high and dead straight over Fenning Gap, quick if his nerve held. The old flood culverts ran low and hidden and out of sight of every patrol on the surface, slower but nobody's business. And the rooftops over Bramwell's yard cut the last corner off entirely, if he did not mind going over a certain workshop, which he did mind, for reasons he had never said out loud.

---

**cable**
The clatterlift cable at Fenning Gap was not built for running. It was built for cargo cradles and the occasional maintenance man with a harness and a great deal of patience, and Ratchet was neither of those things.

But it went straight across, two hundred yards dead level, and straight was what he needed.

Below the gantry the outfall grate had begun to breathe: a thin curl of white nosing up between the slats like something waking slowly and in no particular hurry to be reasonable about it.

---

**cable_good**
He went out onto it and did not look down and did not slow, and he was off the far gantry before the grate below had properly made up its mind, with his heart going considerably faster than his legs had.

Wedged in the gantry rivets, left there years ago out of some maintenance man's dropped kit, was a small disc of cracked watch glass, ground thin and true.

He nearly left it. He had never in his life left a useful thing lying about when he had a use for one, and he was not going to start on a Tuesday.

Component found: Cracked Watch-Glass.

**cable_mid**
He went out too slow at the start and had to make it up in the middle, and the last stretch turned into a graceless scramble along a cable in a rising cloud with his coat torn and his hands not doing what he told them.

He got across. He cut it a great deal finer than he liked to think about, and he thought about it for the rest of the night.

He still had the wit, halfway along, to snatch a disc of cracked watch glass out of the rivets, mostly because his hand closed on it before the rest of him had finished deciding.

Component found: Cracked Watch-Glass.

**culvert**
The flood culverts ran under the Under itself, brick throats slick with a hundred years of runoff, lit by whatever leaked down through the grates above.

Nobody official ever went into the culverts, which was exactly why every runner in Gifford who valued their knees used them anyway.

The trouble was the backwash: short bursts of scalding water that came up through the side vents on no schedule anybody had ever pinned down, except that they came, and that standing in the wrong place when one arrived was not a mistake anybody got to make twice.

---

**culvert_good**
He heard the brick change note ahead of him, which is the only warning that lane ever gives anybody, and he was through the gap at a dead sprint and out the far side with his boots barely wet when it hit the wall behind him with a sound like a kettle losing an argument.

Snagged in the grate he had just come under, God knew how long, was a small disc of cracked watch glass, ground thin and true.

He took it out of pure reflex.

Component found: Cracked Watch-Glass.

---

**culvert_bad**
He was thinking about the chit instead of listening, and the note changed, and he did not hear it.

It caught him across the boots and shins and went through his socks, and he came out the far end soaked and furious and moving slower than he could afford, and he would be limping tomorrow and cursing culverts for a good deal longer.

He still had the presence of mind to take the watch glass out of the grate as he went past, because a runner who has stopped noticing useful things has stopped being a runner.

Component found: Cracked Watch-Glass.

**rooftop**
The rooftops over Bramwell's yard cut clean across the last stubborn corner of Long Lane, if you did not mind a climb, a drop and being seen by somebody whose opinion of you mattered more than the job did.

Through a skylight thick with soot Ratchet got one bright second of the workshop below. Lamp glow. A boiler half stripped down. The old man himself bent over the bench at nine at night with a scraper in his hand and no reason on earth to still be working.

Everybody in the Under knew what that shop was. Ratchet had been told about it at fifteen by a man who had meant it kindly, and had gone as far as the door twice, and had not gone in either time, and could not now have told anybody why.

He did not stop. He never stopped on a job. That was the job as well: wanting the five minutes and not taking them.

---

**rooftop_fight** (scene intro)
Old Fenner did the yard's night rounds the way he had done them for eleven years, lamp in one hand and temper in the other, and he was not a man who missed a shape moving where no shape had business moving.

He came up onto the flat roof by the ladder just as Ratchet was crossing it, lamp swinging round.

"Oi." He did not sound angry so much as put out, a man whose evening had just acquired a complication. "This is Bramwell's roof, not the parish common."

He was not wrong, and he was not trying to hurt anybody, and neither of those things made him easier to get past.

**Old Fenner, the Night Watchman — hitText**
Ratchet talked fast and moved faster, ducking a stack of guttering while the lamp came round a half-second behind him.

**Old Fenner, the Night Watchman — strikeText**
The lamp caught him full in the face, hot and blinding, and he nearly put a foot straight through a rotten patch of felt.

**Old Fenner, the Night Watchman — defeatText**
Fenner gave it up at the roof's far edge, wheezing, and settled for shouting something about respectable premises after Ratchet's back, which Ratchet privately agreed with rather more than he would ever have admitted.

---

**rooftop_good**
He went off the far gutter into the alley behind the yard and came up grinning, the way he always did after outrunning somebody whose actual job it was to catch him, and then stopped grinning about four seconds later for reasons he did not examine.

Caught in the gutter ironwork, rattling loose as he swung past, was a small disc of cracked watch glass.

He pocketed it without breaking stride, the way you pocket anything useful when you have spent your whole life owning very little.

**rooftop_bad**
The lamp caught him properly and he went down the last stretch of roof more by luck than by any plan, and landed in the alley in a heap that would ache tomorrow and probably the day after.

Nothing broken. Everything bruised, including, quietly, his opinion of himself.

He still had the wit to take a disc of cracked watch glass out of the gutter ironwork on the way down, mostly because his hand closed on it before his brain had finished deciding whether that was sensible.

Component found: Cracked Watch-Glass.

**citycrossed**
Whichever way he had come, Ratchet came out at the top of Sixmark Street with the same thing hammering behind his ribs.

The light.

The lamp over the Exchange counter window was still burning gold, which meant the window was still open, which meant he was not finished but he was close.

The ninth bell had not gone. But the outfalls in this city do not wait for anybody, and Ratchet had learned a long time ago that not yet was the most dangerous phrase in Gifford.

---

**second_good**
He went through the gap between the crates a half-step before it shut, spinning past a barrow-seller who swore at him with real warmth, the way Sixmark folk swear at anybody quick enough to have earned it.

Loose in the gutter where the barrow had been standing, small and coiled and easy to miss, was a bent mainspring, the kind that comes out of a watch too cheap to be worth mending properly.

He had it up without slowing.

Component found: Bent Mainspring.

---

**second_bad**
The gap shut a half-step before he got to it and he took a crate corner hard across the hip, and came out the far side of the crowd rather less gracefully than he would have liked, to the barrow-seller's considerable enjoyment.

Nothing in the gutter caught his eye going past. He did not have the time to go back and look properly, and some things you get one clean chance at.

**third_check**
The Exchange's side alley was gated for the night. A proper iron gate with a proper Exchange lock, put there to keep out exactly the sort of person Ratchet currently was.

But the gate had a trick in it, if you knew the mechanism. Three pins, and only one of them actually caught anything.

He had watched a locksmith work this exact gate once, months back, for reasons that had nothing whatever to do with tonight, and some part of him had filed the shape of it away without being asked.

Now was the moment to find out whether that part of him had done its job.

---

**third_good**
He did not try them one at a time. He remembered.

The middle pin. Always the middle pin. The outer two were dummies fitted specifically to waste a burglar's evening, and the man who had built them had been proud enough of the idea to explain it out loud to an apprentice, in a doorway, within earshot of a boy nobody had noticed standing there.

The gate gave with a small embarrassed click, as though it thought the whole arrangement was rather much.

Fallen into the mechanism, filed thin by whoever had cut those dummy pins in the first place, was a slim brass latch-pin, exactly the sort of small precise thing a good clock is built around.

He took it, and then he took the shortcut, in that order.

Component found: Filed Latch-Pin.

---

**third_bad**
He went at it by feel instead of memory, tried the wrong pin first, and lost the better part of two minutes he did not have to a gate with no interest at all in his schedule.

It gave eventually. Gates generally do, given patience and luck in roughly equal measure, and he was short of one of those.

Whatever was loose in the mechanism stayed loose. He was not going fishing for it tonight.

**forge** (scene intro)
The Exchange counter kept a bench in the back for exactly this sort of thing, because half of Sixmark Street's business ran on mended timepieces and nobody trusts a runner who cannot keep to a schedule he can actually read.

Old Press minded the bench most nights for a cut of whatever got mended on it, and looked up at Ratchet with the unimpressed patience of a man who has watched every kind of desperate come through that door.

"Cracked glass, a bent spring, a filed pin." He turned them over one at a time under the lamp. "You've been busy. D'you want me to make something of it or just admire it?"

**forge — forged**
Press worked fast and said nothing while he did it, which from Press was very nearly a blessing.

The glass went in true, the spring wound tight and even, the pin seated with one clean click, and what came off that bench was a watch that actually kept the time it claimed to, which in Gifford was rarer than it had any business being.

"The Counted Second," Press said, handing it over. "Keeps its own time. Doesn't care what the church clocks think, and neither should you."

The Counted Second is yours! (+1 Arithmetic, kept forever.)

**finale**
The window was still lit when Ratchet put the chit down in front of the Exchange clerk, breathing like a man who had personally argued with the whole of Sixmark Street and mostly won.

The clerk unfolded it, read the name, checked the seal against the ledger, and logged it in a hand as unhurried as if there were no such thing as a ninth bell anywhere in the world.

Etta Coombe. Quit and settled. Struck off tomorrow's list before anybody had drawn the list up.

Out over Long Lane, right on cue, the outfall let go with a long scalding sigh, and the white came up thick and orange under the gaslight and sealed the route he had spent the whole evening racing.

He watched it through the counter window in no hurry at all, and let himself feel the thing he had been putting off since the back room over the Ashlight.

He had done one decent thing today. He had done it for an organisation that mostly did not deal in those, and he had done it because a man he owed nine marks to had told him to, and every part of that was true at the same time, and none of it cancelled any of the rest.

He had stopped expecting it to.

**end — goodText**
He got back to the Ashlight with breath to spare and told the whole run at length to anybody who would listen and several who would not. Vane heard the report without much comment, which from Vane was as close to satisfied as that job ever paid out.

Word came back inside the week, the way it does on Sixmark Street. Etta Coombe had gone to market the morning after with her head up for the first time in eight months, and nobody there had known enough about it to make anything of it, which was rather the point.

Ratchet never met her. He did not need to.

What he thought about, now and then, was that the same man who had paid for that quittance had also let him carry nine marks for four years without ever once mentioning it, and that both of those were the same kindness, and that he could not work out which way round to be grateful.

**end — midText**
He got the chit to the counter in the end, scraped and out of breath and rather more battered than the job had strictly called for, and the clerk logged it without a flicker of interest in the state of the runner who had brought it.

Vane heard the report and made a noise that in poor light might have passed for approval.

The job got done. It nearly did not, twice. Ratchet spent the walk home working out how close was too close and whether he would trust himself with a margin that thin again, and decided he would, because the alternative was admitting he was not as quick as he liked to think.

Somewhere behind that, unexamined, was the roof over Bramwell's yard, and the old man at the bench at nine at night, and a door Ratchet had stood outside twice and had never once gone through.
