# gf_adavoss — "The Weight of a Good Reason"

**tagline**
A marking season, a paper that is right in every answer and wrong in every working, and Second Examiner Ada Voss has to decide what a good reason is actually worth.

---

**intro**
Ada Voss had marked six sittings of the Calibration and she had stopped, some time in the fourth, reading a script for the number at the bottom.

You read it the way you read weather. For the shape of the thing.

The script in front of her belonged to a girl called Sorrel Kettle. Stratum not yet posted. Boiler row, Cutlers' Wynd. Fourteen years old. Marked once, properly, by a First Examiner who had initialled every page in a hand steady enough to trust.

Twenty-six right out of thirty. Four wrong.

It was the four that had come up to her desk in a folder with a Tallow Street docket clipped to the front, because somebody downstairs had noticed what Ada was noticing now and had not liked the look of it enough to sign it off alone.

The four wrong answers were not wrong.

That was the thing. Ada read them three times to be sure she was not imagining it and a fourth to be sure she was not hoping. Every one of the four arrived at the answer the scheme wanted. Every one of them arrived there by a route the scheme did not recognise, and the scheme awarded its marks for the route.

---

**loupe_check**
A First Examiner initials a script when she trusts her own reading of it. That does not mean nobody else's reading is owed, which is the entire reason the title Second Examiner exists and the entire reason the Office pretends most weeks that it does not.

Ada set the four workings side by side under the good lamp and followed each of them from the first line to the last, in the girl's order, without once letting herself skip to the place where the taught method would have gone next.

If a thing was going to survive a properly hostile examiner, it had better survive her first.

---

**loupe_find**
It survived.

All four of them got there. Not approximately. Not by luck. Four different questions, four different shapes, and the same unfamiliar route through each of them, arriving every time at exactly the answer the scheme had printed in the margin as correct.

That is not carelessness. Carelessness does not arrive anywhere four times running.

In the drawer under the good lamp, where three generations of Second Examiners had kept whatever oddments made the job survivable, Ada found the loupe her predecessor had left behind. Brass, chipped at one edge, magnifying nothing so much as the habit of looking twice.

She had not taken it out in a year. She took it out now.

The Marginal Loupe is yours. (+1 Arithmetic, kept forever.)

---

**loupe_miss**
She could follow three of the four and the fourth kept getting away from her, because she kept sliding back into the taught order without noticing she had done it, which was itself instructive and no use to her at all tonight.

Three out of four was the pattern and it was loose enough that a hostile examiner, a real one, the kind who wanted the answer to be nothing, could take it apart in a sentence.

She put the loupe back in the drawer where it had sat since her predecessor retired, and told herself the pattern would still be there in the morning whether or not she had it proved to the standard she wanted.

(The Marginal Loupe stays in the drawer this time. Another run through this adventure may find it.)

**gate_contention** (scene intro)
A script under discretionary review did not simply sit on a Second Examiner's desk. It lived in the Sealed Script Room three floors down, and getting one back out after hours needed a signature from Warden Fitch, who had held that room's one good lock for nineteen years and treated every request against it as a personal slight to the lock.

"Reopening a closed script," said Fitch, not looking up from his ledger, "is a Contention same as anything else in this building. You want it out past closing, you argue it out past closing. Rules don't care whose desk the folder landed on."

Ada had won Contentions against harder men than Fitch. She had never enjoyed one less.

**Warden Fitch — hitText**
Ada puts it plainly, in order, with the docket number first, and whatever objection Fitch had loaded folds itself up and goes back in his pocket.

**Warden Fitch — strikeText**
Fitch finds a clause she has not accounted for and reads it at her with real satisfaction, and for a moment the whole thing tips his way.

**Warden Fitch — defeatText**
Fitch shuts his ledger, which from Fitch amounts to unconditional surrender, and slides the signature across the counter without another word.

---

**gate_win**
"Midnight," said Fitch, stamping the release docket a good deal harder than the docket had earned. "Back by midnight and signed in, or it's my post as well as yours, and I've a great deal less patience for losing mine than you have for losing yours."

It was not friendliness. Ada had stopped expecting friendliness from that floor some years back.

It was a signature, and on that floor a signature was the only currency there was.

---

**gate_retreat**
Fitch won it on a technicality narrow enough to post a letter through, and the script stayed exactly where it was, three floors down, behind his one good lock.

There are other ways into a locked room than through its warden. Slower ones. Less official ones.

In six years Ada had never once found Fitch's rulebook covering everything, which she suspected Fitch knew, and which was possibly why he guarded the door rather than the book.

---

**crossroads**
A script cannot be re-marked on a hunch, however many scraps of working sit behind the hunch. What a Second Examiner needs is the same thing corroborated somewhere outside the paper, in at least one place the Office cannot dismiss as an examiner's private opinion.

Three doors, and not one of them comfortable.

The invigilator who watched that row on the day, and might remember more than her log has room for. The script itself, under a proper lamp, followed line by line. Or five years of the boiler-row scripts in the basement, and whoever has decided that basement is theirs.

---

**route_invig**
Mrs Grieve had invigilated the boiler-row sitting for six years and kept a log neat enough to eat your dinner off, which was more than could be said for most things in the Under.

She did not want a Second Examiner in her kitchen and said so, and put the kettle on anyway, which in the Under is not a contradiction but a system.

"Spot-checks," she said, once Ada got the question out straight. "Four rounds that sitting. Girl's desk got checked twice. Nothing on her, nothing near her, nothing under the desk. If she'd had a rule or a card or so much as a marked cuff I'd have had it off her."

"I believe you," said Ada. "That's not what I came about."

Mrs Grieve's hands stopped on the pot.

---

**invig_good**
"I came about what you've seen," said Ada. "Six years on that row. Not what they had. How they worked."

And Mrs Grieve sat down, which she had not intended to do, and told her.

Boiler-row children did it differently. All of them, or near enough. Not one method exactly, but a family of them, and the family came up out of the wynds and the laundries and the compressor houses, and it was taught at kitchen tables by women who had never sat a Calibration in their lives.

"Finished early, that girl," Mrs Grieve said. "Finished early and sat still with her hands on the desk for the last half hour like she'd been told to. I've watched boiler-row children finish early on that row six years running."

"Is it in your log?"

"There's no box for it." Mrs Grieve looked at the table. "There's a box for a device and a box for talking and a box for looking at another desk. There's no box on that form for *she got there another way*, so it's never once been written down, by me or by anybody, in six years."

Ada wrote it down.

---

**invig_mid**
Ada asked it badly, the way you ask a question when you already know what you want the answer to be, and Mrs Grieve heard the shape of it and closed up by about half.

What she got was the spot-check log, correct in every particular, and the flat assurance that there had been nothing under that desk.

What she did not get was six years of a woman's own observation, because Ada had come in asking about cheating, and a woman who has been asked about cheating does not then volunteer that she has watched forty children do something unusual and never written it down.

She got a thread. She got it thinner than it needed to be, and it was her own fault, and she knew it walking home.

The Corroborated Timeline is one thread closer, however it got there.

**route_script**
The Sealed Script Room lent her one hour and one lamp and the lamp was not generous.

Ada laid the four disputed answers flat and read them the way the loupe had taught her, which is to say for the working and not for the number.

Question nineteen was the clearest. It concerned a boiler losing pressure, which was about as Under a question as the Calibration ever dared to set, and which had presumably been written by somebody on the Rise who had never stood next to one.

The scheme's model answer took one route. Sorrel Kettle had taken another.

Ada meant to find out where the girl's route actually went, by walking down it herself, in the girl's order, with witnesses and a docket and a lamp.

---

**script_good**
She walked it through twice and got there both times.

The girl's route was shorter. That was the part Ada sat with for a while, alone, three floors down, with an hour on the docket and a lamp that gave her about half of what she needed.

Not shorter by a step or two. Shorter by a third, on a question about a boiler, worked by a fourteen year old who had been taught it in a kitchen on Cutlers' Wynd, and marked wrong in the margin by a scheme that awarded its marks for a longer road.

She did the other three the same way. Same result each time.

Nobody had smuggled anything into that script. What was in that script was a method, and a method is what it looks like when a thing has been taught properly by somebody.

The Corroborated Timeline is one thread closer.

---

**script_mid**
She lost it twice on the first pass, both times in the same place, because at the third step her hand went where thirty years of her own schooling told it to go instead of where the girl's working said.

It took her the whole hour and most of her temper to get all the way down it without correcting the girl on the way.

She got there in the end. What she took away, and did not enjoy, was that the hardest part of the entire evening had been not doing it her own way.

The Corroborated Timeline is one thread closer, however long it took to pull.

**route_archive**
The Rustbound archive lived in a basement under the record office that the record office pretended, most weeks, not to have a key to.

Getting into it meant getting past Under-Warden Speck, who had spent thirty years deciding what counted as somebody else's business.

"Five years of boiler-row scripts." Speck did not move from the doorway. "That's not a Second Examiner's business, that's an appeals matter, and appeals go through the Office in writing with a docket, same as always."

"I'm not appealing anything," said Ada. "I'm trying to find out whether I need to."

"Same thing to my ledger," said Speck.

---

**archive_fight** (scene intro)
Speck folded his arms in that doorway the way he had folded them a thousand times before and won most of them.

"Appropriate channels. There's a form. There's always a form, and you'll find the form takes about as long as five years takes."

"Then we had better start," said Ada, and did not reach for a form at all.

**Under-Warden Speck — hitText**
Ada names the docket, the schedule and the clause that actually governs it, in that order, and Speck's next objection dies somewhere behind his teeth.

**Under-Warden Speck — strikeText**
Speck finds something about appropriate channels and reads it at her twice, and the doorway seems to get narrower.

**Under-Warden Speck — defeatText**
Speck steps back at last, muttering about dockets, and decides that five years of other people's paper can stop being his problem for one evening.

---

**archive_win**
He let her in and stood over her shoulder the whole time, resentful and helpful in exactly equal measure, which is how basements are.

Five years gave up its shape quickly once she knew what to look for. Eleven other scripts across five sittings, the same family of methods, the same working that got where it was going by a road the scheme did not have printed.

Every one from a child whose registered address began in the same six streets.

Not one of them had been caught at anything, because there was nothing to catch. All eleven had lost marks for it anyway. A mark here and two there, never enough on any single paper to make anybody look up.

Ada copied it out longhand, dates and names and marks lost, and did not yet let herself think about what a list like that was a list of.

The Corroborated Timeline is one thread closer.

---

**archive_retreat**
Speck did not budge, not for reasoning, not for rank, not for the docket she eventually produced and very nearly raised her voice over.

What she got was ten minutes standing in the doorway itself, reading upside down over his folded arms while he recited clauses at her like a man reading scripture to avoid having to think about what the scripture said.

Ten minutes was not five years. It was enough to find the same road in three scripts off the top of the nearest pile, before he noticed what she was looking at and shut the drawer.

Three is not eleven. Three is still a pattern, and patterns do not need permission to be true.

The Corroborated Timeline is one thread closer, however narrow the door it came through.

---

**converge2**
However she had come by it, Ada had the same thread from three directions now: an invigilator with six years of observations and no box to write them in, a script whose road was shorter than the scheme's, and a basement's worth of the same thing happening over and over.

Not enough to walk into the Grandmaster's office and demand anything. Enough to stop calling it a hunch.

Two questions left, and both mattered more than she wanted them to.

Was the method a real teachable thing, or a lucky accident dressed up as one afterwards? And had it been happening long enough and widely enough that quietly fixing one girl's mark would fix nothing at all?

---

**primer_good**
Bess Callow took in washing on Cutlers' Wynd and had taught it to every child on that street for twenty years, including, as it turned out, Sorrel Kettle.

She sat Ada down at her own table with a slate and taught her, and Ada, who had marked six sittings of the Calibration and come fourth stratum herself twenty-two years ago, was slower at it than a woman's ten year old grandson.

"It's old," Bess Callow said, watching her work. "Older than the Calibration, if it comes to that. My grandmother stoked boilers by it and hers before her. Nobody called it wrong until somebody decided there was one right road to an answer, and the right road turned out to be the one they teach in the schools our children don't get sent to."

She wrapped the chalk stub in a twist of paper and put it in Ada's hand before she could object.

"Take it. If you're going to stand up somewhere and say my sums are honest, you'd best be able to do them yourself in front of whoever makes you prove it."

The Boiler-Row Primer is one piece truer.

---

**primer_mid**
Ada got most of the way and went wrong near the end, and Bess Callow corrected her without a flicker of triumph, the way a woman corrects a thing she has taught a hundred children and expects to teach a hundred more.

"Slower than my girls manage it," she said, not unkindly. "But you got there."

She did not offer the chalk. Some things have to be earned properly, even by examiners with good intentions, and possibly especially by those.

---

**tally_find**
The shape held. Fourteen children across five years, one method between them. Of the fourteen, six had done well enough on the rest of their papers that the marks lost to the working had actually moved their stratum, every single time, by exactly one place. Never two. Never once in the other direction.

One place. Never enough on its own to be worth an appeal. Always enough, added up across one street over five years, to be worth noticing, if anybody with the standing to notice had ever thought to add it.

Ada had thought to add it. She sat back from the registers at four in the morning and understood, with a coldness that had nothing to do with the hour, that she was not holding a hunch about a girl.

She was holding evidence about a method that the Office had never needed to write a single conduct amendment for, because the mark scheme was doing the demoting on its own, quietly, one honestly taught step at a time, and nobody had ever had to sign anything.

The Five-Year Tally is complete.

---

**tally_miss**
The registers would not stay in order past two in the morning and somewhere in the third year she lost her own place and had to begin again, and the night ran out before the tally did.

She had a strong impression of the shape and no tally she would put her name to.

An impression is not nothing. It is considerably less than a Second Examiner needs before walking into anybody's office with it, and Ada had watched what happens to people who go early.

---

**forge** (scene intro)
There is no smith for a thing like this. What there is, in the examiners' room after everybody has gone home, is a good lamp, a clean sheet and Ada's own steadiest hand, such as it is at that hour.

"The timeline, the primer, the tally," she said to the empty room, because talking to an empty room had become a habit she had stopped apologising for. "All three, laid out properly, and I'll write you something worth filing."

**forge — forged**
The three pieces sat under the lamp where she had left them and for once nothing about the arrangement felt like a hunch.

She wrote it longhand, every claim tied to a working she could defend to a hostile examiner without flinching. Six years of an invigilator's observations that no form had a box for. A road shorter than the scheme's. Fourteen children and six strata.

She did not address it to the Grandmaster.

A document like this, sent up through the ordinary channels, was exactly the sort of thing that grows a four-line amendment overnight and comes back down pointed at Sorrel Kettle rather than at the mark scheme. Ada had been in that building six years and she had watched a smaller document than this one go up and come back down as somebody's conduct.

She addressed it to the file instead. A formal annex, correctly witnessed, correctly dated, sitting inside Sorrel Kettle's own script where nobody could take it out quietly without the gap being noticed.

The Weighed Verdict is yours! (+1 Science, kept forever.)

**forge — notforged**
Ada laid out what she had and looked at it for a while.

Short. A verdict wants all three threads pulled the whole way through, or it is a very well organised suspicion, and a very well organised suspicion filed under her own name would end her in that building and change nothing for the girl.

The lamp would be there tomorrow night. So, she suspected, would all of it.

**finale**
In the morning she amended Sorrel Kettle's mark by the one place the working actually owed her, initialled it in her own hand, and filed the annex where the docket said an annex belonged. Quietly, correctly, and impossible to un-happen without somebody noticing the gap where it had been.

She did not report Bess Callow. She did not report Cutlers' Wynd, or the six children, or the five years.

Some time around the second week she went down to the reading room on the third floor, looking for a precedent, and found instead an internal minute file that had been opened three times in forty years.

Sixty-first year. A First Examiner called Halloran. Nine pages.

She read all nine of them twice, standing up, and what stopped her was page seven, where a man considerably better than her had written that he had satisfied himself the practice was not malicious and that its purpose was protective, and that he found he was not able to write down what it protected.

Somebody had told him. That was plain in the sentence. Somebody had told him and he had taken it, and gone quiet for the rest of a long and comfortable career.

Ada put the file back on the shelf and sat in the reading room until the lamps went.

What she decided, that evening, and wrote down afterwards in a hand she did not much recognise as her own, was this: that there was going to be a reason, and that the reason was going to be a good one, and that a person had better decide in advance what they intend to do with a good reason before somebody hands them one.

She was thirty-six years old. She could not take the arrangement down and she did not pretend to herself that she could.

She could put one honest thread back where it belonged and leave the rest of it in a file where the next person, with a good reason and steadier hands, would be able to find it.

It was not enough. She wrote that down too, in the margin, where nobody official ever thinks to look for the truth of a thing.

**end — goodText**
Sorrel Kettle's stratum moved up one place on the Tallow Street board, quietly, in a season when quiet was the only sort of justice going.

Nobody thanked Ada, because nobody outside the examiners' room ever knew there was anything to thank her for, which was rather the point of how she had done it.

Bess Callow's washing got a good deal busier that winter in a way that had nothing to do with washing. Ada never asked who was sending the children and Bess Callow never said, and between the two of them that counted as an entirely clear conversation.

In the drawer under the good lamp, the Weighed Verdict sat filed exactly where a careful person with a good reason and steadier hands might one day come looking.

She had stopped billing herself for the years in which she had not yet been that person. She had not, quite, stopped hoping to be.

**end — midText**
The mark went up by the one place it was owed, though it took Ada three goes at the wording before the annex could not be quietly misread, and she went home each of those three nights certain she had got it wrong.

She had not got it wrong. The verdict sat in the file, thinner than she wanted, built out of a narrow doorway and a lost tally and a question she had asked badly in somebody's kitchen, and true in every line that was actually in it.

It was not the clean case she had wanted. Ada had learned by then that the clean case is mostly a thing examiners hope for and almost never get, and that the untidy one, filed carefully anyway, still counts.

What she had not learned yet, and would not for some years, was how long a person can go on filing untidy things carefully before they start to feel like the arrangement rather than a crack in it.
