# gf_adavoss — "The Weight of a Good Reason"

**tagline**
A second marking season, a second paper that will not add up the way it is supposed to, and Second Examiner Ada Voss has to decide, again, what a good reason is worth.

---

**intro**
Second Examiner Ada Voss had marked eleven sittings of the Calibration since the one everybody in the examiners' room still called, carefully, 'the business with the boy from Ferrow Lane,' and not one word more than that. Eleven sittings, and she had learned to read a script the way you read weather: for the shape of the thing, not just the numbers written on it.

The script in front of her now belonged to a girl called Sorrel Kettle, stratum not yet posted, boiler row, Cutlers' Wynd. Fourteen years old. Marked once, properly, by a First Examiner who had initialled every page in a hand steady enough to trust. Twenty-six answers right out of thirty. Four wrong.

It was the four that had been sent up to Ada's desk in a folder with a Tallow Street docket clipped to the front, because somebody downstairs had noticed what Ada was now noticing herself, and had not liked the look of it enough to sign off alone.

The four wrong answers were not random. They were wrong by a small amount that was always in the same direction and always explained by the same habit: rounding at the end of every step instead of once at the very end. It took Ada three read-throughs to be sure she was not imagining it, and a fourth to be sure she was not hoping to.

**loupe_check**
A First Examiner initials a script when they trust their own arithmetic. That does not mean nobody else's arithmetic is owed a second look, which is the entire reason the title 'Second Examiner' exists and the entire reason the Office pretends, most weeks, that it does not matter.

Ada set the four wrong workings side by side under the good lamp and did each sum four separate ways, on four separate scraps of paper, refusing to let her pencil agree with itself out of laziness.

If the pattern was going to survive a properly hostile check, it had better survive Ada's own first.

---

**loupe_find**
It survived. Four times over, in ink, then in pencil, then in the back of her own head with her eyes shut for good measure: every one of Sorrel Kettle's wrong answers was short of the true answer by a whole unit, worked out by rounding after every step instead of once at the end. That is not carelessness. Carelessness is never that polite about where it goes wrong.

In the drawer under the good lamp, where three generations of Second Examiners had kept whatever oddments made the job survivable, Ada found the loupe her own predecessor had left her: brass, chipped at one edge, magnifying nothing so much as it magnified the habit of looking twice. She had not used it in a year. She used it now.

**crossroads**
A script cannot simply be re-marked on a hunch, even a hunch with four scraps of pencil working behind it. A Second Examiner who wants a pattern to survive contact with the Office needs it corroborated from outside the paper itself, in at least one place the Office cannot wave away as an examiner's private opinion.

Three doors stood open to her, and none of them looked comfortable.

Through the first, the invigilator who had watched over Sorrel Kettle's row on the day, and might remember more than her own log admitted to. Through the second, the script itself, held up to a proper light and a proper eye for what its ink was actually saying. Through the third, five years of the Rustbound archive, and whoever in the record office had decided that was theirs to guard.

**route_invig**
Mrs Grieve had invigilated the boiler-row sitting for six years and kept a log neat enough to eat your dinner off, which was more than could be said for most things in the Under. She did not want a Second Examiner in her kitchen, and said so, and put the kettle on anyway, which in the Under is not a contradiction. It is a system.

'Spot-checks,' she said, once Ada finally got the question out straight. 'Four rounds, that sitting. I check one desk in three, each round, at random, same as always. Girl's desk got checked twice out of the four. Nothing on her, nothing near her. If she'd had so much as a slide-rule under that desk, I'd have had it.'

'Would you, though,' Ada said, not unkindly. 'Truly. Every single time.'

Mrs Grieve's mouth went thin, the way mouths do when a fair question lands somewhere fairer than the asker meant it to. 'Sit down,' she said, 'and I'll show you the arithmetic of it, because I've done this six years and I still don't like the answer half so much as I ought to.'

**route_script**
The Sealed Script Room lent Ada exactly one hour and exactly one lamp, and the lamp was not generous. She laid Sorrel Kettle's four wrong answers flat on the table and read them the way the loupe had taught her: not for the number at the bottom, but for the arithmetic breathing underneath it.

Question nineteen of the paper was the clearest. It concerned a boiler losing pressure, which was, Ada reflected, about as Under a question as the Calibration ever dared to set.

The mark scheme's model answer worked the loss in one clean run of multiplication, start to finish. Sorrel Kettle's working, marked wrong by the scheme's own reckoning, rounded the pressure to the nearest whole unit at the end of every single hour before carrying on. It was not the taught method. Ada meant to prove, by doing the sum properly herself, in front of witnesses, exactly what that method had cost the girl and exactly why.

**script_good**

**route_archive**
The Rustbound archive lived in a basement under the record office that the record office pretended, most weeks, not to have a key to. Getting into it properly meant going through a clerk called Under-Warden Speck, who had spent thirty years deciding what counted as somebody else's business.

'Five years of boiler-row scripts,' Speck said, once Ada stated her business plainly, the way she stated everything. 'That's not a Second Examiner's business. That's an appeals matter, and appeals go through the Office, in writing, with a docket, same as always.'

'I'm not appealing anything yet,' Ada said. 'I'm trying to find out whether I need to.'

'Same difference, to my ledger,' said Speck, and did not move from the doorway.

**tally_find**
The shape held. Fourteen children across five years had used some version of Ivy Fettle's method on the Calibration. Of those fourteen, six had scored well enough on the rest of their papers that the rounding cost had actually changed their stratum, every single time, by exactly one place, never more, never less, never once in the other direction.

One place. Never enough, on its own, to be worth an appeal. Always enough, added up across a whole street's worth of children over five years, to be worth noticing, if anybody with the standing to notice had ever thought to add it up.

Ada had thought to add it up. She sat back from the registers at four in the morning with the tally in front of her, and understood, with a coldness that had nothing to do with the hour, that she was no longer holding a hunch about one girl. She was holding evidence about a method the Office had never once had to write a conduct amendment for, because the mark scheme itself was doing the demoting, quietly, one honestly-taught rounding step at a time.

**finale**
In the morning, Ada amended Sorrel Kettle's mark by the one place the arithmetic actually owed her, initialled the change in her own hand, and filed the Weighed Verdict where the docket said an annex belonged: quietly, correctly and impossible to un-happen without somebody noticing the gap where it used to be.

She did not report Ivy Fettle. She did not report the boiler row, or the six children, or the five years. She had turned the question over a hundred times under the lamp, the way an examiner called Halloran must have turned it over once, decades before, until he found the particular kind of silence that let a man live out a whole career comfortable in it, and she had decided, in advance, the way she had once told a young officer to decide in advance, what she was actually going to do with a good reason once she had one.

A good reason for doing a wrong thing quietly for a hundred years is how you get a city where four clerks and a docket slip hold up the whole arrangement. Ada could not, this year, take the whole arrangement down. She could put one honest thread back where it belonged, and leave the rest of it sitting in a file exactly where the next person with a good reason, and steadier hands than hers, would be able to find it.

It was not enough. She wrote that down too, in the margin, where nobody official would ever think to look for the truth of a thing.

Sorrel Kettle's stratum moved up one place on the Tallow Street board, quietly, in a season when quiet was the only kind of justice on offer. Nobody thanked Ada for it, because nobody outside the examiners' room ever knew there was anything to thank her for, which was rather the point.

Ivy Fettle's laundry got a little busier that winter, in a way that had nothing to do with washing. Ada never asked who sent the children, and Ivy Fettle never said, and between the two of them that counted as a perfectly clear conversation.

At her desk, in the drawer under the good lamp, the Weighed Verdict sat filed exactly where a careful person, with a good reason and steadier hands than Ada's currently were, might one day go looking for it. She had stopped billing herself for the years she had not yet been that person. She had not, quite, stopped hoping to be.

**end — midText**
Sorrel Kettle's mark went up by the one place the arithmetic owed her, though it took Ada three tries at the wording before the annex could not be quietly misread, and she went home each of those three nights certain she had got it wrong.

She had not got it wrong. The Weighed Verdict sat in the file, thinner than she would have liked, built out of a narrow doorway and a slipped hundred and a tally she had abandoned once and had to start again, but true in every line that was actually in it.

It was not the clean case she had wanted. Ada had learned, over eleven sittings since Ferrow Lane, that the clean case is mostly a thing examiners hope for and almost never get, and that the untidy one, filed carefully anyway, still counts.
