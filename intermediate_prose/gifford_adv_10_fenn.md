# gf_fenn — "The Sixth Board"

**tagline**
Fenn Okonkwo came out sixth of eleven at the Calibration, no triumph and no disaster, and now he has four days to find out what that is actually worth.

---

**intro**
Fenn Okonkwo had sat the Calibration in a hall built for six hundred and lit like a courtroom, and had come out of it the way everybody comes out of it: quiet, hungry, and unable to remember a single answer he had written down.

Now it was posting day. Tallow Street was three deep in children who had not slept properly in a week, all of them looking up at a lintel with eleven boards under it, waiting for men in Council grey to come out with the ladders.

His mother had wanted to come. He had told her no, gently, because if it went badly he did not want an audience for whatever his face was going to do, and if it went well he wanted to be the one who told her, in his own words, at his own table.

Sootfall was starting, fine and black, the way it did around fourth bell. Fenn turned his collar up and waited with the rest.

---

**crowd_check**
The boards were not posted by name. They were posted by docket number, first stratum at the top of the leftmost board and running down and across, in a clerk's hand that had stopped caring somewhere around row forty.

Six hundred children trying to read six hundred numbers off eleven boards at once had already put one boy on the cobbles twice.

Beside Fenn a girl of about fourteen in a coat two sizes too big was saying her own docket number under her breath like a prayer, over and over, and getting nowhere with it, because the boards were nailed at a height that suited the men with the ladders and she was four foot nine and there were three hundred people between her and the lintel.

Nobody in that crowd was going to move for her. Nobody was being unkind about it. They simply all had their own number to find.

---

**help_found**
Fenn was tall for fourteen and he had a brother the same age as this girl, so he did the obvious thing, which nobody in three hundred people had thought of.

"Up," he said, and got a shoulder under her, and lifted.

She found her number in about nine seconds from up there. Ninth, and she went white, and then oddly relieved, because ninth was not what she had been afraid of.

He put her down. She stood getting her breath for a moment.

"Nobody was going to let me through," she said.

"No."

"Why did you?"

Fenn thought about that and had no answer that was not going to sound better than it was, which was that he had spent the whole walk over being frightened and had wanted something to do with his hands.

Her brother, hovering behind her with the particular gratitude of somebody who has just been made to feel useless in public, pressed a slate into Fenn's hand before he was towed away. Half rubbed clean, chalk dust still ghosting somebody's last working.

"Go on. You'll want it more than I will today."

---

**help_missed**
He tried to talk her through where the number ought to be and the crowd shouldered them both sideways twice while he was doing it, and by the time he had her pointed at roughly the right board her own brother had solved it by simply asking a Council clerk, which apparently anybody was allowed to do and which nobody had thought to mention to anybody.

Three separate strangers trod on Fenn's foot.

No matter. There were other slates in the world. Right now there were eleven boards and his own docket number and a crowd that was not getting any thinner.

---

**sixth_board**
He found board six before he found himself on it, which was its own small mercy: time enough to get his breathing steady before his eyes landed on the number.

There it was.

Not near the top, where the gilt frame caught what little gaslight the lintel offered and people stood doing the hushed dangerous smiling that good news does to a face. Not near the bottom either, where the eleventh board hung nailed flat and nobody stood long, because standing there too long invited comment.

Dead centre. Sixth of eleven.

Around him the morning did what posting day always does. Somebody shrieked three boards along. Somebody else sat straight down on the cobbles as though the news had gone through their knees on the way past. A boy near the eleventh board was quietly and thoroughly sick against the wall while his friends looked at the sky, which was the kindest thing available to them.

Fenn kept his finger on the board for a full ten seconds after he had found it. Not to check. It simply seemed like a thing that was owed ten seconds.

Sixth of eleven. Not the top, not the bottom, the exact unglamorous middle. And somewhere under the sootfall a voice that sounded uncomfortably like his own was already asking the only question that mattered, which was what that was going to be worth on Monday, and the Monday after that, and in eleven years.

The placement offices shut their books in four days. He went to find out.

---

**gate_fight** (scene intro)
He did not get twenty yards.

A thin man in a decent coat came off the edge of the crowd with the unhurried motion of somebody who had done this exact walk several hundred times, and fell into step beside him like an old friend.

"Sixth board." Not a question. "Hard one, sixth. Not bad enough to give up on, not good enough to coast." A smile arrived on schedule. "I could help with that, for a small consideration. Call it a Contention. Sporting stakes. If your reckoning's as good as your stratum says, you've nothing to lose."

He was not offering a Contention. A Contention is a thing that happens in a hall with a Proctor in it, and men like this borrow the word the way they borrow everything else.

His name, over the next unpleasant minute, turned out to be Dobbin Hask, and his idea of sporting was one where the terms moved the instant you stopped watching them.

**Dobbin Hask, Contention Tout — hitText**
Fenn says no once, plainly, and then says nothing at all, and Hask's patter runs out about nine seconds later with nowhere to go.

**Dobbin Hask, Contention Tout — strikeText**
Hask changes the terms mid-sentence, quick as a card trick, and by the time Fenn has caught up two cogs have left his pocket that never agreed to go.

**Dobbin Hask, Contention Tout — defeatText**
Hask puts both hands up, all injured innocence, the way they always do the moment it stops being on their side. "No harm meant," he says, already drifting toward the next stunned face coming away from the boards. There is always a next face coming away from the boards.

---

**gate_win**
Fenn walked on with his pockets as full as they had started, which on a day like that counted as a real victory.

Hask would find somebody else. Tallow Street had six hundred fresh strata that morning, and every one of them would be an easy mark for a few hours yet.

Fenn intended to stop being an easy mark starting immediately, and the intention lasted rather better than most intentions formed on posting day.

---

**gate_retreat**
He got out of the conversation and put three streets between himself and Hask before he trusted his pockets.

Two cogs gone. And a length of chalk he had not noticed going, which annoyed him considerably more than the cogs.

It was not a large loss. It was, he decided, walking faster, going to be his last one today.

---

**crossroads**
Four days, three real leads, and a mother who was going to ask him at supper whether he had a plan, in the voice of somebody who already suspected the honest answer was not yet.

The Engine Yard on Founder's Row was taking a handful of sixth to eighth stratum boys onto the millwright apprenticeship: slow, respectable, four years to a proper writ. The Signal Office at the clatterlift terminus had far more applicants than posts and was settling it by lot, which paid at once if it paid at all. And the vent-warden crews down in the Under were short-handed again after last winter, the training being short and the work being exactly as dangerous as everybody already knew.

Four days to find out what each was actually worth. Not the wage. Everybody knew the wage. What it was worth.

---

**engineyard_setup**
Founder's Row smelled of hot metal and clean oil, which after four days of sootfall and wet wool felt almost indecent.

The hiring clerk had ink to the cuffs and no patience for wasted time, and laid the terms out on a single sheet, the way you do for somebody you expect to actually read it.

"Millwright's apprenticeship. Four years. First two you're paid badly and you're worth less than you're paid. Second two, once you're trusted near anything that turns, it goes up. Come out with your writ and you're a qualified fitter for as long as your hands hold, which round here is generally a good long while."

She tapped the sheet.

"Everybody asks whether it's worth it, so I'll save you the trouble of asking. I've no idea. I've had four lads off your board through that door this week and two of them will be gone by winter and I could not tell you which two."

---

**engineyard_good**
"What makes them go?" said Fenn.

The clerk stopped, halfway to the ledger, and looked at him properly for the first time.

"That," she said, "is a better question than the one they usually ask." She came back. "Two years of bad money is what makes them go. Not the work. The work's fine. It's two years of watching a lad your age on the outfall crews come past the gate on a Friday with silver in his hand while you've got coppers, and doing that fifty-two times, and then fifty-two more."

"And the ones who stay?"

"Have somebody at home who can carry them through the two years, or are stubborn past the point of sense." She reached under the counter and came up with a mainspring, salvaged off a scrapped test-gauge, coiled tight and sound as the day it was wound. "Keep that. Sign or don't, no charge. Anybody who asks me what makes them leave instead of what they'll earn is worth the spring."

**engineyard_mid**
Fenn asked about the wage, because it was the question in his mouth and he was fourteen, and got the wage, which he already knew.

The clerk answered him fully and correctly and went back to her ledger, and the conversation was over about a minute before Fenn realised it had been.

She tossed him a mainspring off the same scrapped gauge on her way past.

"For the walking," she said. "Come back and ask me a better one before the books shut."

Component found: A Sound Mainspring.

**signal_setup**
The Signal Office queue ran three times round the terminus yard, under the brass cables where the clatterlifts hauled up and down the face of the Rise all day.

Forty-two names in for three posts this season, a clerk announced, bored, and the drawing would be by lot, out of anybody's hands.

"Paid from the first Monday if you're drawn," said the man next to Fenn, who had clearly explained this several times already and was starting to enjoy it. "No apprenticeship, no writ, no waiting about." He leaned in. "Course, it stays exactly what it is for ever. Man I know's been on that counter nine years and he's on the same money as the day he walked in."

The lot-drum sat on the counter, brass and turning, and somewhere inside it Fenn's name was one small folded paper among forty-two.

---

**signal_good**
The drum came up kind three weeks later. Fenn's name, one of three.

Paid from the first Monday, no training, no writ, and precisely nothing promised about the Monday after the season.

The clerk stamped the docket without looking up, which told Fenn most of what he needed to know about the next nine years.

Behind the counter, wedged in the gears of the drum itself where some earlier winner's paperwork must have gone astray, sat a small coiled spring: the kind that made the thing turn true instead of sticking on the same three names every week.

He worked it free while nobody was watching. It seemed a fair trade for what that drum had just cost forty-one other families.

Component found: A Sound Mainspring.

---

**signal_fail**
The drum came up unkind. Somebody else's name, then another, then a third that was not his either.

He stood in the yard for a while afterwards, not because he had expected differently, but because knowing a thing is unlikely and standing in a yard while it fails to happen are two entirely separate experiences and nobody ever tells you that.

On the way out he stopped to help the man behind him lever a jammed die out of the counter's edge, and it came away with a small coiled mainspring caught round its rim, dropped by nobody knew who.

He took it. Waste not.

**ventward_setup**
The vent-warden yard sat hard up against Outfall Nine, where the Turn breathed its scalding white four times a day and somebody had to be near enough to feel it coming or the whole lane downstream got cooked for nothing.

A lane fills in about forty of your own breaths. It does not clear again for a good deal longer. Every warden in that yard carried those two facts in his knees and none of them had ever written either down.

"Two years' training," said the foreman, a burned-looking man with a foreman's contempt for wasted breath. "Short training, because there isn't much to teach past feeling it come and not flinching. Comes out at good money for as long as your nerve holds, which round here is not always as long as your contract."

He looked Fenn over without much interest.

"Ask around the lane about the flinching."

There was a queue for that too, and at the front of it stood a boy built like a coal cart, thinning it out by hand.

---

**ventward_fight** (scene intro)
"Foreman pays me by the head I turn away," Bosk said, not troubling to sound sorry. "Flinch on that grate and you don't lose your place in a queue, you lose a hand. So he likes it thinned before the training costs him anything."

He jerked his chin at the outfall rail, four feet away, where the grate had already started to go warm.

"Stand there till I say."

He did not wait for an answer.

**Corrin Bosk, the Foreman's Heavy — hitText**
Fenn stands where he is put and stays there while the grate comes up warm under his boots, and does not move, and Bosk's sneer slips half a beat.

**Corrin Bosk, the Foreman's Heavy — strikeText**
Bosk shoulders in on him at exactly the wrong moment and Fenn goes down hard against the outfall rail with the breath knocked half out of him and grate-heat coming up through his sleeve.

**Corrin Bosk, the Foreman's Heavy — defeatText**
Bosk steps back at last, rubbing a knuckle, weighing up whether this one is worth a bruise report. "Fine. You don't flinch today. Doesn't mean you won't flinch in February."

---

**ventward_good**
The foreman had watched the whole thing from the doorway with his arms folded and gave the smallest nod available to him, which in that yard passed for a warm welcome.

"Come back tomorrow if you still want it," he said, and then, unexpectedly, as Fenn was leaving: "And ask your mother first. Not for permission. Ask her what she'd do with the money if the hand went."

Underfoot, kicked loose from the rail in the scuffle, lay a small coiled mainspring off one of the dozen pressure gauges that yard went through in a week.

Fenn picked it up before anybody thought to charge him for it.

**ventward_retreat**
He backed off before Bosk got a second go in, which was the sensible thing and did not feel remotely sensible standing there afterwards with his ears ringing and his place in the queue gone to somebody else.

The foreman had not so much as looked over.

He was most of the way down the lane before he noticed the mainspring stuck to the sole of his boot, kicked there in the scuffle and travelling with him since, small and coiled and entirely indifferent to whether he had won anything.

He kept it. A morning that costs you something should at least leave you holding something.

---

**converge**
Three leads, three mornings, one mainspring salvaged three different ways, and Fenn no closer to knowing which of them was a life he could run a household on.

He knew what all three paid. He had known that on the first afternoon. What he did not know was which of them a person could still be doing in eleven years.

There was somebody who thought about that sort of thing for a living, over a counter, for a fee, and he had just enough chalk credit left to ask her properly.

---

**marta_setup**
Marta's shop smelled of lamp oil and other people's debts. She kept the tab for half the street on a board behind the counter and a second ledger in her head for anybody who asked nicely.

"Sixth stratum," she said, when he had laid all three of them out. "That's not nothing and it's not much, and you know that already or you'd not be in my shop looking like a boy who hasn't slept."

"I want you to tell me which."

"No you don't." She put both hands flat on the counter. "You want me to tell you it's all right to want the fast one. I've been behind this counter forty years, love, and I'm not doing that, and I'll tell you why in one question."

---

**marta_good**
"Your mother," said Marta. "Year three. Not this year, not next. Year three. Where is she?"

Fenn opened his mouth.

Then he shut it, because he had done four days of asking about wages and hours and writs and training, and he had not once asked himself where his mother was going to be in three years, and he knew, standing at that counter, exactly what the answer was and exactly which of the three jobs had a man at home in it and which two did not.

"That's it," said Marta. "That's the whole sum and there's not a figure in it. Every lad who comes in here has done the money and none of them have done the third year." She slid a small disc of glass across the counter, cut clean and whole, salvaged off some clock too far gone to save. "For not arguing with me. Go on, before I charge you rent for the stool."

---

**marta_mid**
"Year three," said Marta. "Where's your mother?"

Fenn told her the wage again, because it was the only thing he had brought with him, and she let him finish and then sat with it for a moment.

"Come back tomorrow," she said. "And don't bring me the money. I can read the money off the notice board same as you."

He came back the next day and got there, in the end, mostly on his own, which Marta pointed out was still getting there.

She did not offer him anything for the counter that time, which felt, on reflection, like the more honest of the two responses.

**sconce_setup**
Old Sconce mended clocks and, by his own account, consciences, whichever the customer could pay for first, out of a shopfront so narrow you had to turn sideways to reach the counter.

Fenn's father's watch had been in his good coat pocket for three years, stopped, because a stopped watch that had been his father's was easier to carry than a working one that reminded him his father was two hundred miles down a coastal engine line and increasingly unreliable about writing.

"I don't mend sentiment," Sconce said, turning it over in fingers gone black at the nail. "I mend movements. This wants a mainspring, a face-glass and a minute-hand, and I'm short of all three this week, so unless you're carrying spares we're both stuck."

He nodded at the counter, where his own accounts sat open and badly kept.

"While you're stood there. My eyes have gone. Read me that column out loud. I've not had the stomach for it this fortnight."

---

**sconce_found**
Fenn read it out. There was nothing wrong with it, which he said.

Sconce did not answer for a while.

"No," he said eventually. "There isn't. I know what's in it. I wanted to hear somebody else say it in a voice that wasn't mine." He took the ledger back and closed it. "Forty years in this shop and it's going. Not this year. Next, or the one after. Nobody mends a watch any more, they buy a worse one."

Fenn stood in a shop nine feet deep and did not know what to do with that.

"Don't look like that," said Sconce. "You asked me nothing and I told you anyway, which is the privilege of being old." He reached into a drawer that plainly did not get opened for just anybody and came up with a minute-hand, thin as a whisker and dead straight. "Keep it. Anybody who'll read a dying man's ledger out loud without pretending to find a mistake in it has earned a straight hand."

**sconce_missed**
Fenn read the column out and, wanting to be useful, said he thought there might be something wrong in the middle of it.

Sconce looked at him for a long moment.

"There isn't," he said. "There's nothing wrong with it at all. That's the trouble with it."

He shut the ledger and did not explain, and the drawer that did not get opened for just anybody stayed shut, and Fenn went out sideways past the counter with the distinct sense of having failed a test he had not been told he was sitting.

**finale**
That night he laid it out on the kitchen table for his mother, all three of them side by side rather than one at a time.

The Engine Yard: two years of bad money and then a writ nobody could take off him, and a clerk who had told him honestly that two of four lads would be gone by winter and she could not say which.

The Signal Office: money from the first Monday, the same money for nine years, and forty-one other families in that drum.

The vent-warden crews: the best money of the three, a foreman who paid a boy by the head to thin his own queue, and a question the foreman had asked him on the way out that Fenn had not been able to stop thinking about since.

His mother listened to the whole of it without once telling him what to think, which from her was a kind of respect.

"The vent yard pays quickest," Fenn said at last. "That part's not close. It's genuinely not close, the first two years."

"And?"

"And Marta asked me where you'd be in the third year." He turned the watch over, listening to it tick, the way you check a thing is still telling the truth. "And the foreman asked me what you'd do with the money if the hand went. And I've had four days and I can't answer either of them, and I've worked out that not being able to answer them *is* the answer."

His mother did not say anything for a while.

"Sixth of eleven doesn't get me the exciting one," said Fenn. "I'd stopped hoping for that by about the second afternoon. It gets me a decent one, if I choose carefully and don't spend the four days feeling sorry for the boy on board six instead of the one who has to turn up on Monday."

**end — goodText**
He signed the Engine Yard's papers on the last morning the books were open, and walked home past the Signal Office queue, still three deep, still hoping, without a flicker of regret he could locate anywhere.

It was not the fast money. It was the one he had asked the hardest questions about, and the one that stopped being an emergency and started being a life.

His mother put the Counted Half-Hour on the mantel where the stopped watch used to sit, and from that day it kept extremely good time.

Some Calibrations end in a gilt frame and some end nailed flat under the eleventh board, and most, like Fenn's, end somewhere in the ordinary unglamorous middle, which turns out, if you weigh it properly and refuse to be ashamed of it, to be worth rather more than those boards ever bothered to post.

**end — midText**
He signed the Engine Yard's papers in the end, though it took him two more trips back to Marta's counter before he would put his name to it, and he was not certain even walking out of the office.

His mother said the waiting had made him careful rather than slow, which was how she said she was proud without spending a word more than the sentence needed.

The middle of eleven boards is not a thing anybody tells stories about at a mantel for years afterwards.

But Fenn kept the Counted Half-Hour running true in his coat pocket every day after, and turned up early for every shift that yard ever gave him, and eleven years later he was still there, with his hands, which is not nothing at all and which none of the six hundred children on Tallow Street that morning would have thought to want.
