**finale**
The Turn chose that exact moment to vent, as it did four times every day whether or not the Under was ready for it, and a wall of hot white fog rolled down between the Hall and the yard, and the whole queue surged forward at once in the particular panic of people who cannot see where the front of the line has got to. Coats and caps and satchels all went sideways together, and somewhere in the crush a harried clerk, unable to see faces for fog, began simply counting grey coats aloud and waving them toward a roped-off corner to wait for someone to claim them. Pip felt himself lifted half off his feet by the crowd and set down again facing the wrong way entirely, and before he had drawn one more breath he was standing in the corner with perhaps forty other small children in identical charity grey, all of them somebody's, none of them, for the moment, anybody in particular. He held very still, the way you do when you are trying not to become more lost than you already are, and closed his fist tight around the little brass tag at his collar.

'Forty-one,' the clerk was saying to nobody, not unkindly, only tiredly, ticking a slate. 'Forty-one grey coats, small, various.'

It was a Laachmi clerk who found him properly, a tall horned woman named Nettle who kept the main ledger and had, Pip now remembered, once given Rooke a peppermint for getting a whole column of sums right three years running. She was working her way along the roped corner peering at faces rather than coats, the way almost nobody bothered to any more, and when she reached Pip she stopped.

'I know you,' she said, and it was not a question. 'Halloway, isn't it. The little one who used to trail after that sharp-tongued sister of his and correct the ledger from the queue, cheeky article.' She crouched to his height, which for a Laachmi meant folding a considerable distance, and read the brass tag at his collar without needing to be shown it. 'Pip Halloway. There. That's better than small, various, I should think.'

Pip found he could breathe again all at once, in the way you can only once you notice you had stopped.
